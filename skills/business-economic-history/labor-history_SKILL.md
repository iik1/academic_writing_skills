# Journal Writing Skill: Labor History

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** BUS HIST & ECON HIST
- **Publisher:** Taylor & Francis/Routledge
- **Scope:** Peer-reviewed journal publishing historical studies on labor worldwide, with emphasis on labor movement history in the United States, Europe, and other regions; examines capitalism, labor rights, unionization, collective bargaining, and occupational safety.
- **Guidelines URL:** http://www.tandfonline.com/action/authorSubmission?journalCode=CLAH20&page=instructions (institutional access required; direct guidelines page is paywalled)
- **Field baseline:** business-economic-history-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 4 abstracts and article records from Volume 66 (2025) and online article listings

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows field baseline. Introduction, substantive main sections, conclusion. Literature review may be woven into introduction or separate; sources-and-methods section expected for archival and quantitative work.

### Length
**ENFORCED:** Manuscript should target approximately 8,000 words (including endnotes and references). Taylor & Francis guidelines for Labor History specify this as a "useful target" length. Submit as Microsoft Word document, Times New Roman, 12-point font, double-spaced.

### Abstract
**ENFORCED:** Abstract should be approximately 100 words, unstructured (descriptive rather than structured). Abstract should be italicized and indented, preceding the main text. It should describe the main arguments and conclusions without technical apparatus.

### Citations
**ENFORCED:** Chicago Manual of Style, Humanities (Notes-Bibliography) system. Use superscript numerals in text corresponding to numbered endnotes. Full citations in endnotes on first reference; shortened form in subsequent notes. Include a separate List of References/Bibliography at the end. This applies to all Labor History submissions.

### Headings
Follows field baseline. No explicit constraints found; apply Chicago conventions. Headings should be clear and used to organize substantive sections (thematic or chronological).

### Tables and Figures
Figures, tables, and images should be submitted as separate files from the manuscript. Number sequentially. Captions should accompany figures and tables with full sourcing. Chicago style conventions apply to citation of sources within tables.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Manuscripts must be anonymized for double-blind peer review. Author name(s) and institutional affiliation must not appear in the manuscript text; provide only article title. Author information is supplied separately in cover email.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Citation Density and Style
**ENFORCED (Minor Divergence):** While FIELD_SKILL notes that citation density is high and distributed throughout (not concentrated in a literature review), Labor History specifically mandates Chicago Notes-Bibliography format with endnotes rather than footnotes. This is a formatting constraint, not a density change, but it operationalizes citations differently: endnotes appear at the end rather than at page foot, which can affect reader navigation and the visual rhythm of the prose. The underlying citation density remains consistent with FIELD_SKILL.

### Manuscript Submission Format
**ENFORCED:** Labor History requires submission via ScholarOne (https://mc04.manuscriptcentral.com/dup-lab), though this is a procedural constraint rather than a writing style delta. Manuscripts must be double-spaced, Times New Roman 12pt, in .docx format.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- **Voice and Register:** FIELD DEFAULT (formal scholarly register, mix of first-person plural and impersonal framing, measured tone)
- **Sentence and Paragraph Style:** FIELD DEFAULT (medium-to-long sentences, substantial paragraphs, light signposting)
- **Argumentation and Rhetorical Moves:** FIELD DEFAULT (CARS-like structure, historiographical gaps, revisionist framing, counter-narratives valued)
- **Methods Conventions:** FIELD DEFAULT (procedural detail on sources and methods, past tense, reflexivity on archive limits)
- **Results and Reporting Norms:** FIELD DEFAULT (narrative integration of evidence, direct quotation from primary sources valued)
- **Discussion and Conclusion Norms:** FIELD DEFAULT (restate contribution, connect to broader debates, acknowledge evidence limits)
- **Hedging and Epistemic Caution:** FIELD DEFAULT (moderate hedging, measured causal claims, confident thesis statements)

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Exceeding word limit:** Target 8,000 words including endnotes and references. Submissions significantly over this threshold may face desk rejection or desk revision requests.

2. **Insufficient anonymization:** Ensure the manuscript itself contains no identifying information (author name, institution, identifying references). Provide author details only in the cover email and ScholarOne submission form.

3. **Format errors in notes and bibliography:** Labor History uses Chicago Notes-Bibliography (endnote) style, not author-date or footnote style. Ensure all citations follow superscript endnote numbering and full citation on first reference. Maintain consistent short form for subsequent citations.

4. **Inadequate source/methods transparency:** Even for narrative archival pieces, Labor History expects clear disclosure of which archives, databases, or sources were consulted and limitations of the evidence. Include a brief sources section or integrate source discussion into the introduction.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

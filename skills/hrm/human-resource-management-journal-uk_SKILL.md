# Journal Writing Skill: Human Resource Management Journal (UK)

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 4*
- **Field:** HRM&EMP
- **Publisher:** Wiley-Blackwell (John Wiley & Sons Ltd)
- **Scope:** Multidisciplinary business and management journal (ISSN 1748-8583) focused on the management of people at work and the issues/tensions this raises, open to any aspect of employment studies; not organisational-psychology-only work. Distinct from the US-based "Human Resource Management" (Wiley, ISSN 1099-050X) and from "Human Resource Management Journal" (ABS 4, US/general, a separate skill already exists for that title).
- **Guidelines URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/page/journal/17488583/homepage/forauthors.html (returns 402/paywall in this environment; guidance below is triangulated from the journal's 2024 editorial perspective piece and confirmed against recently published articles)
- **Field baseline:** hrm-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 11 (Farndale et al. 2020 editorial "A look to the past, present, and future..."; Budhwar et al. 2024 editorial "Articulating scholarship in human resource management" [full text, DOI 10.1111/1748-8583.12567]; Vardi & Collings 2023 "What's in a name? talent"; Li et al. 2024 "Untangling human resource management and employee wellbeing relationships"; Lai et al. 2024/2025 "The impact of ability-, motivation- and opportunity-enhancing HR sub-bundles..."; Nadiv 2025 "Bounded professionalism nexus"; Begley et al. 2025 "Practitioner Experiences With Digitalization in Human Resource Management" [full text obtained]; O'Brady, Doellgast & Starcevic 2025 "Worker Voice and Mutual Gains From Remote Performance Management"; Rofcanin & Budhwar 2026 "Reinvigorating the Micro Foundations of HRM"; Haar 2026 "Enhancing HR Research Through an Indigenous Centred Research Approach"; Ren 2026 "Toward Inclusive Green Human Resource Management")

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Standard IMRaD-with-theory (Introduction, Theoretical Background/Literature Review, Methods, Findings/Results, Discussion, Conclusion), consistent with FIELD_SKILL. HRMJ additionally requires, immediately after the abstract/keywords, a **"Summary" (Practitioner Notes) box** in three labelled bullet-point subsections: "What is currently known?", "What this paper adds?", and "Implications for practitioners" (sometimes "...for practitioners and/or policymakers"). Guidance is no more than four bullets per subsection, each approximately 80 characters. This is confirmed present in recent published articles (e.g., Begley et al. 2025) and is a distinctive, non-optional structural element not found in FIELD_SKILL.

### Length
No confirmed hard word/page limit found in accessible sources; do not assume figures found for other Wiley HRM titles apply here.

### Abstract
Unstructured, single-paragraph abstract (confirmed in spot-checked articles); no explicit word count found in accessible guidance. Followed immediately by a keyword list and then the mandatory Summary/Practitioner Notes box (see Structure above).

### Citations
APA reference style, author-date in-text, alphabetical reference list (confirmed via reference list formatting in full-text article).

### Headings
Numbered section headings ("1 | Introduction", "2 | Theoretical Background", etc.) are the house style as rendered by Wiley; follows FIELD_SKILL norm otherwise.

### Tables and Figures
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm; no journal-specific deviation identified.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Per the 2024 editorial perspective (Budhwar et al.), HRMJ explicitly states it will NOT accept: research not focused on a specific aspect of HRM; manuscripts that are primarily organisational psychology with little/no HRM engagement; papers that do not engage with theory; studies based solely on student samples; small-scale studies; purely replication studies; and studies written by AI. The editorial also states that a paper "clearly crafted for—and rejected by—another journal" has little chance without substantial revision, and that both theoretical and practical implications are required in every submission (not optional).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
The mandatory Summary/Practitioner Notes box (What is currently known? / What this paper adds? / Implications for practitioners) immediately following the abstract is an ENFORCED, journal-specific structural requirement not present in FIELD_SKILL or in most other HRM&EMP journals. Classification: ENFORCED (confirmed in guidance summaries and directly observed in full-text spot-checked articles).

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Contextualisation is emphasised more heavily than the FIELD_SKILL baseline implies: the editorial guidance devotes an entire dedicated section to contextualising HRM research (national business systems, sector, occupation, under-researched settings) and frames failure to contextualise as a leading cause of desk rejection. Authors targeting non-US/non-UK or under-researched contexts should make the contextual contribution explicit in the introduction, literature review, and discussion. Classification: ENFORCED/emphasised (stated explicitly in editorial guidance across multiple contributors, consistent with the four submission types accepted: regular empirical, provocations/conceptual, theoretical, and reviews/registered reports).

## Common Pitfalls
- Omitting or under-developing the Summary/Practitioner Notes box, or writing bullets that exceed the ~80-character/4-bullets-per-section guidance.
- Submitting organisational-psychology-flavoured work with no explicit HRM framing — editors state this is a common, avoidable desk-reject reason.
- Treating "implications for practice" as an afterthought; the editorial guidance is explicit that both theoretical and practical implications are required, not optional, and vague practical implications (e.g., "raises managers' awareness") are viewed unfavourably.
- Insufficient contextualisation of findings (e.g., not discussing how national/sectoral/institutional context shapes results), flagged repeatedly as a top reason for rejection.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Family Business Review

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** ENT-SBM
- **Publisher:** SAGE
- **Scope:** Research focused on the interface of family and business systems;
  explicitly open to diverse formats and methods (quantitative, qualitative,
  conceptual, systematic reviews) as long as the family-business interface is
  central.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/author-instructions/fbr
  (fetched successfully via official SAGE submission-guidelines PDF)
- **Field baseline:** entrepreneurship-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 2 full-text (Palm, Diaz-Moriana, & Kammerlander,
  2024, restructuring/SEW multi-case study; Yilmaz, Raetze, de Groote, &
  Kammerlander, 2024, systematic literature review on resilience), plus
  front-matter/abstract review of 6 additional 2023–2024 articles (Carbone
  et al. 2024; Calabrò et al. 2024; Bauweraerts et al. 2024; Martin et al.
  2024; Hoon et al. 2023; He et al. 2023). Total: 8 articles.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No fixed section template is mandated — FBR explicitly states it is "open
to many different formats and styles of presentation" as long as the paper
addresses the family-business interface. This is a genuine RELAXATION
relative to FIELD_SKILL's more rigid hypothesis-testing spine: qualitative
multi-case studies, systematic literature reviews, and conceptual pieces
are all first-class citizens at FBR, not secondary formats.

### Length
Hard cap: manuscripts must not exceed **40 double-spaced pages, all
inclusive** (text, references, figures, tables, appendices), 12-point Times
New Roman, one-inch margins. This is materially tighter than the
appendix-heavy norm implied by FIELD_SKILL for survey-based ETP/JBV papers;
extensive robustness appendices common in top ENT-SBM journals must be
trimmed or dropped to fit the 40-page ceiling. ENFORCED (confirmed by both
spot-checked articles falling within a comparable page range).

### Abstract
**100-word maximum**, unstructured, no fields/headings. This is
considerably shorter than typical ABS 4/4* abstracts. ENFORCED — both
full-text articles reviewed carry concise single-paragraph abstracts
consistent with this limit.

### Citations
APA style, in-text author-year parenthetical citations, alphabetical
reference list. Matches FIELD_SKILL default; no delta.

### Headings
No specific heading hierarchy mandated beyond standard APA levels; journal
guidelines are silent. Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.

### Tables and Figures
Distinctive submission mechanic: all tables and figures must be placed at
the **end of the main document, one per page**, numbered in two separate
series (one for tables, one for figures), with placement markers in the
text (e.g., "Insert Table 2 about here"). This is a SAGE/social-science
convention rarely emphasized in FIELD_SKILL and should be followed exactly
for FBR submissions.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- No simultaneous submission or prior acceptance/publication elsewhere;
  prior working-paper or conference distribution must be disclosed.
- Anonymized (double-anonymize) review: author-identifying information
  must be fully absent from the main document (separate contact-information
  document carries author names/affiliations).
- A maximum of 5 keywords.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (first-person plural, active voice) in both
articles reviewed ("we conducted," "we suggest," "we find").

### Statistical Reporting
Not assessed as a delta dimension for the qualitative/review sample
spot-checked; no quantitative hypothesis-testing article was accessible in
full text. PRACTICE ONLY observation: the qualitative case-study article
(Palm et al.) reports rich descriptive tables of case/interview counts
rather than regression output, consistent with FBR's openness to
non-hypothesis-testing designs (see Structure above) rather than a
departure from FIELD_SKILL's reporting norms for quantitative work.

### Citation Density
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm — dense, multi-citation paragraphs in
introduction/theory sections in both articles reviewed.

### Structural Deviations
FBR readily publishes pure systematic literature reviews (e.g., Yilmaz
et al., 87-article review) and inductive multi-case qualitative studies
(Gioia-style coding, propositions instead of hypotheses) as flagship
research articles, not just special formats. Authors targeting FBR with
qualitative or review-based work should not force-fit a
hypothesis-deduction structure; a PRISMA-style flow diagram (systematic
review) or Gioia data-structure figure (qualitative) is an accepted
substitute for the hypothesis-testing spine. RELAXATION of FIELD_SKILL's
implied default toward hypothesis-driven quantitative structure (consistent
with FBR's ABS-3 status, which permits relaxing but not redefining the
baseline).

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Propositions (in place of numbered hypotheses) are the norm for inductive
qualitative papers, phrased as formal statements at the end of each
findings subsection (e.g., "Proposition 1: Family firm owners preserve
associated firm- and portfolio-level SEW through retention..."). This
mirrors general qualitative-management convention rather than a
FIELD_SKILL default (which assumes hypothesis-testing quantitative design)
and is PRACTICE ONLY, confirmed in the one qualitative article reviewed.

- Contributions enumerated explicitly in Discussion: FIELD DEFAULT

## Common Pitfalls
- Exceeding or approaching the 40-page all-inclusive limit by treating it
  as a text-only limit; tables, figures, and appendices count against the
  cap, so extensive robustness appendices common in top-tier ENT-SBM
  submissions must be condensed.
- Submitting an abstract longer than 100 words or with structured
  headings — FBR's abstract is a single unstructured paragraph.
- Leaving author-identifying information (names, self-citations phrased as
  "our prior work," acknowledgments) in the main document instead of the
  separate contact-information document, jeopardizing the double-anonymize
  process.
- Per the journal's own published desk-rejection editorial (Craig, 2010,
  FBR 23(4)), a weak or poorly targeted literature review is treated as a
  central "deal breaker" for desk rejection, more so than at some peer
  ABS-3 journals — reviewers use it as the primary signal of fit with the
  journal's specific family-business-interface mission, not merely general
  literature competence.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

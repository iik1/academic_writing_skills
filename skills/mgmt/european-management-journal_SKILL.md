# Journal Writing Skill: European Management Journal

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN (generalizes to all management)
- **Publisher:** Elsevier
- **Scope:** Flagship generalist journal publishing internationally leading research across all areas of management. Articles challenge the status quo through critically informed empirical and theoretical investigations.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/european-management-journal/publish/guide-for-authors (paywalled; extract via Paperpile or Elsevier submit interface)
- **Field baseline:** mgmt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 3 (Bhukya et al. 2022 "Forty Years of EMJ: A Bibliometric Overview"; Muratbekova-Touron 2024 "Letter from Editor-in-Chief"; Cho et al. Nov 2024 article; limited access; primarily via titles, abstracts, and editor letters from ResearchGate and IDEAS/RePEc)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
- Divide article into **clearly defined and numbered sections** (1, 2, 3, etc.).
- Subsections numbered as 1.1, 1.1.1, 1.1.2, etc.; then 1.2, etc.
- Abstract is **not numbered**.
- Standard sections for empirical work: Introduction → Theory/Methods → Results/Findings → Discussion → Conclusion. Combined Results and Discussion is acceptable. Conclusion may be standalone or subsection of Discussion.
- Acknowledgments, Declarations of Competing Interest, and Data Availability statements appear at article end.

### Length
- **Full-length research articles: up to 10,000 words maximum**, including tables, figures, references, and appendices.
- ENFORCED: word limit is strict and stated in guidelines.

### Abstract
- **Unstructured.** No prescribed sections (Background/Methods/Results/Conclusions required).
- Concise and factual.
- Should state briefly: purpose of research, principal results, and major conclusions.
- **No citations.** References avoided; if essential, cite author and year only.
- Word limit not explicitly stated in guidelines; practice suggests 150–250 words (FIELD DEFAULT range).

### Keywords
- Required.
- Will be used for indexing purposes.

### Highlights (Optional but Encouraged)
- **3 to 5 bullet points** (maximum 85 characters **including spaces** per bullet point).
- ENFORCED: Highlights are optional yet highly encouraged, as they increase discoverability via search engines.
- Capture novel results and new methods used.
- Submit in separate editable file in online submission system.

### Citation Style
- **APA in-text citations:** (Author, Year) or Author (Year) when author is part of the sentence.
- Multiple citations ordered alphabetically or chronologically: (Jones, 2010; Smith & Lee, 2015).
- Et al. for three or more authors from first citation.
- ENFORCED: APA style required (confirmed via Paperpile).

### Headings
- Main headings numbered (1, 2, 3, etc.).
- Subheadings use decimal notation (1.1, 1.1.1, 1.2, etc.).
- Headings appear on separate lines.
- Practice: management journals typically use 2–3 levels; EMJ follows this pattern.

### Tables and Figures
- Table grids: use one grid per table, not a grid per row.
- Without grids: use tabs (not spaces) to align columns.
- Figures and tables included in 10,000-word count.

### Explicit Prohibitions and Requirements
- Generative AI: Authors must declare the use of AI (including generative AI) in manuscript preparation upon submission.
- Conflict of Interest: All authors must disclose financial and personal relationships that could inappropriately influence their work (employment, consultancies, stock ownership, honoraria, paid expert testimony, patent applications/registrations, grants/funding).
- Cover Letter: Highly recommended. Should outline main arguments and **how they advance ongoing debates in the journal**. List people who have viewed manuscript, thesis committee members, colleagues with conflicts of interest, and any other circumstances affecting anonymity integrity.
- Language: Good English (American or British usage accepted; mixing styles discouraged).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Hard Length Limit
DELTA (ENFORCED): EMJ enforces a strict **10,000-word maximum** for all content (including tables, figures, references, appendices). FIELD_SKILL does not specify a journal-wide limit; management outlets range 8,000–15,000 words by article type. EMJ is below the FIELD_SKILL norm range. Implication: must trim ruthlessly; no room for long narrative syntheses or extensive literature engagement.

### Abstract Format
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm: both structured and unstructured abstracts appear across management outlets. EMJ explicitly requires **unstructured abstracts** (no "Methods/Results" headings mandated). This aligns with FIELD_SKILL, which allows both. No delta.

### Highlights (Novel Feature)
DELTA (GUIDELINE ONLY / JOURNAL-SPECIFIC): Optional but encouraged 3–5 bullet-point summaries (max 85 chars each) are a notable EMJ feature. **Not mentioned in FIELD_SKILL.** Highlights are increasingly common across Elsevier journals; this is an Elsevier convention, not a management-field norm. Submitting highlights improves discoverability; omitting them does not desk-reject.

### Cover Letter Requirement
DELTA (ENFORCED): EMJ explicitly emphasizes that cover letters should **outline main arguments and how they advance ongoing debates in the journal.** FIELD_SKILL (per management baseline) recommends cover letters but does not specify this rhetorical frame. Implication: must address "how does this work forward existing conversations in EMJ specifically?" rather than generic contribution claims.

### AI Disclosure
DELTA (ENFORCED): Generative AI use must be declared upon submission. FIELD_SKILL does not address this; it is a recent Elsevier/EMJ mandate (circa 2023–2024) responding to LLM adoption. Failure to disclose triggers desk rejection.

### Conflict of Interest and Integrity Declarations
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. FIELD_SKILL specifies "Declarations of Competing Interest" as standard in management; EMJ enforces this and adds explicit mention of anonymity protection procedures (cover letter guidance on listing prior readers). No structural delta, but emphasis is stronger.

### Voice and Register
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. First-person plural "we" throughout, direct assertions with moderate hedging, active voice for argument and interpretation. No delta observed in sampled editor letters and bibliometric articles.

### Citation Density and Engagement
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Introduction and theory sections are citation-dense; results/discussion sections cite as needed to interpret findings; reference lists are extensive (40–80+ for empirical papers, more for reviews). No delta from FIELD_SKILL.

### Statistical Reporting and Methods
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Quantitative work reports coefficients with standard errors, asterisks for significance, and robustness checks. Qualitative work reports methods rigorously with theoretical saturation and triangulation. No delta.

### Hedging and Epistemic Stance
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Moderate hedging ("may," "suggest," "appear to") for interpretive claims; direct statements for methods and established findings. Phrases like "Our findings suggest," "We argue," and "We propose" are standard. No delta.

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Article-Type Variation:** FIELD DEFAULT. EMJ publishes empirical (quantitative and qualitative), theoretical/conceptual, and review articles, following standard FIELD_SKILL templates for each.
- **Introduction (CARS Model):** FIELD DEFAULT. Establish territory → niche → occupy niche (research question + contributions) is standard across management; no EMJ-specific deviation.
- **Theory Section and Hypotheses:** FIELD DEFAULT. Quantitative papers develop theory and close with numbered hypotheses (H1, H2, etc.); qualitative papers use theoretical backgrounds. Conceptual papers use propositions (P1, P2, etc.). Follows FIELD_SKILL precisely.
- **Results and Discussion:** FIELD DEFAULT. Quantitative work reports results in tables with prose interpretation; qualitative work presents findings as analytical categories with quotations and vignettes. Combined Results and Discussion is acceptable. No delta.
- **Conclusion and Future Directions:** FIELD DEFAULT. Restates core contribution, avoids new findings, notes limitations and future research. Standard FIELD_SKILL pattern.
- **Methods Rigor:** FIELD DEFAULT. Identification concerns (endogeneity, selection bias) are addressed explicitly in quantitative work; robustness checks expected. Qualitative methods are described with access, sampling, coding, and saturation details. Follows FIELD_SKILL.
- **Table and Reference List Formatting:** FIELD DEFAULT. Reference lists centered with "References" heading; tables use one-grid rule and tab-separated columns. No delta from FIELD_SKILL formatting norms.

## Common Pitfalls Specific to European Management Journal

1. **Exceeding 10,000 words.** EMJ rejection desk criteria favor lean manuscripts. Authors accustomed to 12,000–15,000-word journals will see their first submission flagged for length. Trim ruthlessly; every word must earn its place.

2. **Omitting or trivializing the cover letter.** A pro forma cover letter ("This is our manuscript...") does not explain how your work advances *EMJ's* intellectual agenda. Editors expect the letter to position the contribution within specific ongoing debates. Weak cover letters correlate with desk rejection.

3. **Failing to declare AI use.** If generative AI was used in any stage of manuscript preparation (drafting, editing, analysis, interpretation), non-disclosure triggers automatic desk rejection. Be explicit: e.g., "ChatGPT was used for copyediting only; no generative AI was used for research design, data analysis, or interpretation."

4. **Mixing British and American English.** Guidelines state either is acceptable; mixing both signals careless editing. Choose one and stick to it throughout. Violating this is not desk-reject-level but weakens perception of care.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

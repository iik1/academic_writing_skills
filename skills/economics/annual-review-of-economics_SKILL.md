# Journal Writing Skill: Annual Review of Economics

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Annual Reviews Inc. (nonprofit)
- **Scope:** Invited, critical review articles synthesizing the state of research on a selected economics topic — not a venue for original empirical or theoretical results.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.annualreviews.org/pb-assets/authors%20assets/authorhandbook-harvard.pdf (generic Annual Reviews author handbook, governs Economics); https://www.annualreviews.org/page/authors/general-information
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 9 (abstracts/front matter via WebSearch, IDEAS/RePEc, and Annual Reviews landing pages): "Decision Under Uncertainty: State of the Science" (Gilboa, 2025); "Poverty and Health" (Lleras-Muney, Schwandt & Wherry, 2025); "The New Economics of Industrial Policy" (Juhász, Lane & Rodrik, 2024); "Automation: Theory, Evidence, and Outlook" (Restrepo, 2024); "Understanding Spatial Agglomeration" (Koster & Thisse, 2024); "Labor Market Insurance Policies in the Twenty-First Century" (Boeri & Cahuc, 2023); "On Measuring Global Poverty" (Ravallion, 2020); plus two additional Volume 16/17 titles identified in tables of contents.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No unsolicited submissions: articles are commissioned by the editorial committee and written by invitation only. There is no fixed section template (unlike empirical/theory papers in FIELD_SKILL); structure is left to the author but must have clearly formatted headings throughout, numbered or unnumbered at multiple levels. Required components beyond body text: Title Page (title, authors, affiliations, ORCID, corresponding author), Keywords (up to 6), Abstract, headed sections, Literature Cited. Optional but common components with strict format rules: Summary Points list (up to 8, complete sentences), Future Issues list (up to 8, complete sentences/questions), Terms and Definitions list (up to 20, 20 words max each), Reference Annotations (up to 10, 15 words max each), Related Resources list (up to 10), and a Sidebar (50–200 words on an adjacent topic).

### Length
Length is individually assigned per article by the editors in the invitation letter, not a fixed journal-wide word limit; authors must keep to this assigned length (figures/tables count against it). An online Article Length Estimator is provided. Manuscripts double-spaced, 12-point type.

### Abstract
150 words maximum (structured field-by-field abstracts are not used; single unstructured paragraph). Up to 6 keywords required.

### Citations
Economics uses Harvard (author-year) style, consistent with FIELD_SKILL's author-year norm — no delta there. However: Economics is one of only four Annual Reviews journals (with Financial Economics, Physical Chemistry, Resource Economics) that DOES permit citing unpublished information/personal communications in the Literature Cited list itself, rather than only parenthetically in text as most Annual Reviews journals require. Footnotes are discouraged. Reference lists must use last-name-first, no periods between initials, double-spaced, un-indented, alphabetical by author surname then year.

### Headings
Clearly formatted at multiple levels (first/second/third-level headings shown in handbook); numbering optional. No canonical section template comparable to FIELD_SKILL's Introduction/Data/Empirical Strategy/Results/Conclusion — reviews are organized topically as the author sees fit.

### Tables and Figures
Tables and figures both count toward the assigned page length. Figures require author-obtained permissions (with fees) for reused material; tables at end of manuscript or in a separate file. Standard Annual Reviews graphic specifications apply (figure sizing, captions bundled separately, color figures free).

### Explicit Prohibitions
No unsolicited manuscripts accepted. Each review "must be an original work prepared for Annual Reviews" — not a repackaged prior publication. Authors are cautioned against both over-citing (padding for "completeness") and under-citing competitors. Postprint/published version may not be posted on the open internet (only the Annual Reviews–supplied ePrint URL, and a preprint before acceptance).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Voice and Register
RELAXATION/RECHARACTERIZATION: FIELD_SKILL's "we find/we show/we develop" empirical-results voice does not apply here, because these are literature-synthesis reviews, not original-results papers. The dominant verbs across spot-checked abstracts are "we survey," "we review," "this review discusses/documents/provides an overview," reflecting the genre difference rather than a stylistic relaxation. Single-authored reviews (e.g., Gilboa 2025, Ravallion 2020, Restrepo 2024) are common and still use "we" or impersonal review framing ("this review..."), not "I."

### Structural Deviations
ENFORCED: FIELD_SKILL's near-universal empirical/theory section architecture (Introduction, Data, Empirical Strategy, Results, Conclusion) does not apply. Annual Review of Economics articles are organized as topical literature syntheses with author-chosen headings, often built around themes, mechanisms, or subliteratures rather than an empirical pipeline. No standalone "Identification Strategy" or "Robustness" sections are expected, since no new estimation is being reported.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
PRACTICE ONLY: Several spot-checked reviews close with an explicit editorializing stance on the literature's trajectory (e.g., Juhász, Lane & Rodrik 2024 conclude that "the recent crop of papers offers a more positive take on industrial policy" and identify where the field is being "reshaped"), consistent with the optional Future Issues list format. This evaluative closing stance is more pronounced than FIELD_SKILL's brief, hedged "directions for future research."

ENFORCED (format-only): The optional Summary Points / Future Issues / Terms and Definitions apparatus is a distinctive Annual Reviews house format with no analog in FIELD_SKILL's ABS 4/4* journals.

## Common Pitfalls

- Treating this as a regular submission-track journal: it is invitation-only, so drafting an unsolicited manuscript to its exact template is moot unless responding to an actual invitation.
- Importing the FIELD_SKILL empirical-paper skeleton (Data/Empirical Strategy/Results) — reviews should be structured thematically, not as an empirical pipeline.
- Exceeding the 150-word abstract cap, which is stricter than typical ABS 4/4* abstracts with no fixed cap.
- Forgetting the assigned (not journal-wide) length limit — the binding constraint is stated in each author's invitation letter, not a fixed journal policy.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

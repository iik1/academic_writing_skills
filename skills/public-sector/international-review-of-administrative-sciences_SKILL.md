# Journal Writing Skill: International Review of Administrative Sciences

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** PUB SEC
- **Publisher:** SAGE, on behalf of the International Institute of Administrative Sciences (IIAS), the European Group of Public Administration (EGPA), and the International Association of Schools and Institutes of Administration (IASIA)
- **Scope:** Comparative and international public administration; oldest journal in the field (founded 1927), positioned to bridge academic and practitioner audiences worldwide.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/author-instructions/ras
- **Field baseline:** public-sector-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 13 (titles/abstracts via publisher TOC and search-indexed abstract snippets; no full-text access): "Comparing public reactions to diversity among government employees and government contractors" (Dec 2024); "In unsteady waters: How mayors and chief administrative officers make sense of a public service bargain in disequilibrium"; "Information on local financial reforms and cognitive processes of citizens"; "Integrating the neo-Weberian state and public value"; "Relocation of public institutions and local public finance: Evidence from South Korea"; "Like a bridge over troubled water: Wellbeing and trust in governance during turbulent times"; "Private consulting firms' intervention in public health policymaking: An exploratory review"; "How do non-Western authoritarian countries respond to disasters?"; "Transformational leadership and public employee performance"; "The spread of participatory budgeting in Belgium"; "Streamlining governance: How the 'One Matter Initiative' reduces administrative burden and optimizes the regulatory climate in cities" (2025); "Developing trust to achieve complementarity for collaborative governance: Lessons from a government programme" (2025); "Meta-analysis in public administration research" (2025).

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Standard research-article structure per FIELD_SKILL. The journal additionally offers a distinct **"Review Article" format** (introduced recently) for theory-focused, state-of-the-art literature syntheses — these follow the same scientific rigor as research articles but foreground theoretical synthesis over original empirical analysis. The journal also regularly runs symposia (3–5 articles) and themed special issues (6–8 articles).

### Length
Hard cap of **7,000 words total**, including title page, abstract, notes, references, tables, and biographical statement — the entire submission package, not just body text. This is a stricter global limit than FIELD_SKILL implies (FIELD_SKILL does not specify limits).

### Abstract
**Unstructured** abstract stating purpose, major findings, and conclusions. Critically, the journal requires a mandatory **"Points for Practitioners"** component associated with the abstract — 2–3 short, direct, practice-facing takeaway statements (e.g., "Trust in governance is different for various political institutions..."; "Civil servants should be aware of citizens' wellbeing during crises..."). This is a distinct, labeled, enforced feature, not merely a discussion subsection as FIELD_SKILL describes more generally.

### Citations
SAGE Harvard reference style (author–date, in-text; initials without spaces/points; up to three authors listed in-text before "et al."; no ibid./op. cit.). This aligns with FIELD_SKILL's author-date norm — no delta in citation form itself.

### Headings
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.

### Tables and Figures
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm; counted within the 7,000-word cap (see Length).

### Explicit Prohibitions
Manuscripts must be **fully anonymized** for double-anonymized review — no identifying information in the main manuscript; author/institutional details go on a separate title page only. Funding must be acknowledged under a dedicated, consistently labeled heading; conflict-of-interest declarations are encouraged.

### Structural Deviations
ENFORCED — mandatory "Points for Practitioners" as a distinct, labeled abstract-adjacent element (typically 2–3 bullet-style statements) is a stronger, more formalized version of FIELD_SKILL's general "practitioner implications frequently pulled out" tendency. Where FIELD_SKILL treats this as common practice, this journal makes it a required, separately labeled submission component tied to the abstract itself, observed consistently across the sampled abstracts.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
ENFORCED (RELAXATION, consistent with ABS 3 status) — the 7,000-word all-inclusive cap is materially tighter than the ABS 4*/4 outlets FIELD_SKILL was derived from, which typically allow longer word counts exclusive of references/tables. Authors targeting this journal should expect to compress literature review and robustness discussion more aggressively than FIELD_SKILL's baseline assumes.

## Common Pitfalls
- Omitting the "Points for Practitioners" element or burying it inside the discussion instead of presenting it as a distinct, labeled abstract component.
- Exceeding the 7,000-word all-inclusive cap by treating it as body-text-only (as is common at less restrictive ABS 4*/4 outlets).
- Insufficient anonymization of the main manuscript (self-citations, acknowledgments, or institutional identifiers left in the body rather than moved to the title page).

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

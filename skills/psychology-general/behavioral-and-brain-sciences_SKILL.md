# Journal Writing Skill: Behavioral and Brain Sciences

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 4
- **Field:** PSYCH (GENERAL)
- **Publisher:** Cambridge University Press
- **Scope:** Publishes theoretical target articles (theoretical synthesis/model-building,
  novel interpretation or critique of existing work, or occasionally broad-scope
  empirical work) in psychology, neuroscience, behavioral biology, and cognitive
  science, distinguished by an Open Peer Commentary format: each target article
  is co-published with 20-40 invited commentaries and an author response.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/information/author-instructions/preparing-your-materials ; https://assets.cambridge.org/BBS/BBS_ifc.pdf
- **Field baseline:** psychology-general-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 (Binz et al., "Meta-learned models of cognition," target article, 2024, DOI 10.1017/S0140525X23003266; Coombs & Trestman, "A multi-trait embodied framework for the evolution of brains and cognition across animal phyla," target article, 2025; Rosenholtz, "Toward a paradigm shift in visual attention," author response, 2025; Belledonne & Yildirim, "A new algorithm of human attention," commentary, 2025; Chater & Loewenstein, "Where next for behavioral public policy?," author response, 2023, DOI 10.1017/S0140525X23002091; plus general search-indexed abstract/metadata for additional 2023-2025 target articles). Full text largely paywalled; assessment based on guideline documents plus abstract/metadata/outline-level access.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Target-article submission package, in order: (1) four separate word counts
(abstract, main text, references, entire text); (2) title; (3) author
names/affiliations/country/email/homepage URLs; (4) short abstract (100
words); (5) long abstract (250 words); (6) keywords (5-10, alphabetical);
(7) main text with numbered headings/subheadings; (8) acknowledgements
(optional); (9) funding statement; (10) conflicts-of-interest statement;
(11) alphabetical APA reference list. Authors must also submit a separate
list of 20-30 suggested commentators with emails and a ~500-word rationale
document justifying why the piece merits Open Peer Commentary. This
commentary-and-response ecosystem (target article -> 20-40 commentaries ->
author response) is the journal's defining structural feature, already
noted in FIELD_SKILL as unique to this venue.

### Length
Target articles: max 14,000 words, preferably shorter (guideline text
elsewhere cites ~12,000 words for main text specifically). Target Article
Proposals: 1,000-2,000 words. Commentaries: max 1,000 words (2,000 for
Multiple Book Review commentaries). Commentary Proposals: optimal
100-500 words. Author Response: 6,000-9,000 words, and cannot exceed half
the length of the target article.

### Abstract
Two abstracts required, not one: a 100-word short abstract and a 250-word
long abstract. Commentaries instead take a single 60-word abstract.
Neither is internally structured (no IMRaD-style subheadings within the
abstract).

### Citations
APA author-date style; alphabetical reference list; DOIs required when
available; specific author-count rules for "et al." usage (three or more
authors triggers "et al." from first citation in-text per APA 7).

### Headings
Numbered headings and subheadings are mandatory in the main text
specifically "to facilitate cross-reference by commentators" — this is a
functional requirement unique to BBS's commentary format, not a generic
organizational preference.

### Tables and Figures
Separate files required for all figures/tables. Resolution: halftones 300
dpi, line drawings 1000-1200 dpi, combination figures min. 600 dpi; JPG
recommended. Arabic numeral consecutive numbering (Figure 1, Figure 1(a));
captions kept in the main manuscript file, not in the figure file;
descriptive filenames; all figures must be referenced in text; reproduced
material requires copyright permission.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Manuscript cannot be under consideration/accepted/in press elsewhere
simultaneously (personal-website/repository preprints excepted). AI tools
may not be listed as authors; AI-generated content must comply with
plagiarism policy and be attributed. Unsolicited commentaries will not be
considered — only invited commentaries and proposals are reviewed.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
Beyond the commentary/response format already flagged in FIELD_SKILL, the
dual-abstract requirement (100-word + 250-word) and the mandatory numbered
sub-heading scheme are BBS-specific and not typical of empirical-report
journals in this field, which usually use a single abstract and IMRaD
headings without a cross-referencing numbering requirement. ENFORCED
(stated in guidelines; confirmed by numbered section outlines visible in
sampled target articles).

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Keyword lists observed in sampled target articles run longer and are more
thematically/conceptually oriented (e.g., "animal minds," "embodied
cognition," "mind-body connections," "trait linkage") than the narrower,
construct-specific keyword sets typical of empirical journals in this
field — consistent with BBS's emphasis on broad theoretical synthesis
articles rather than single-study empirical reports. PRACTICE ONLY
(observed in sampled keyword lists; not separately stated as a norm beyond
the 5-10/alphabetical requirement already in HARD_CONSTRAINTS).

Argumentation in sampled target-article and author-response abstracts is
more explicitly programmatic and stance-taking than typical field register
— e.g., framing the piece around a named proposed framework or mechanism
("adaptive computation," "trait-linkage hypothesis," the "i-frame/s-frame"
distinction) stated as a thesis early in the abstract, rather than the
gap-then-hypothesis funnel typical of empirical reports. This follows
directly from the target article's role as theoretical synthesis/critique
rather than hypothesis-testing empirical work, and is consistent with the
review/theoretical-journal norms already described in FIELD_SKILL.
FIELD DEFAULT (FIELD_SKILL already anticipates this pattern for review and
theoretical journals in the field, of which BBS is one).

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting only one abstract: BBS requires both a 100-word short abstract
  and a separate 250-word long abstract, not the single abstract typical
  of other journals in this field.
- Omitting numbered sub-headings: required specifically so commentators can
  cross-reference sections of the target article in their commentaries.
- Failing to include the required ancillary submission files (suggested
  commentator list, ~500-word suitability rationale) alongside the
  manuscript — these are BBS-specific submission requirements, not
  optional cover material.
- Submitting an unsolicited commentary: BBS only reviews invited
  commentaries or commentary proposals responding to a specific target
  article.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

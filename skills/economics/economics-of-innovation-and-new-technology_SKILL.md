# Journal Writing Skill: Economics of Innovation and New Technology

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Taylor & Francis (Routledge)
- **Scope:** Theoretical and empirical analysis of the determinants and effects of innovation, new technology and technological knowledge. Welcomes contributions across innovation in product markets, process innovation, patenting, adoption, diffusion, innovation and technology policy, international competitiveness, standardization and network externalities, innovation and growth, technology transfer, innovation and market structure, and innovation and the environment.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.tandfonline.com/journals/gein20 (full author guidelines not publicly accessible; institution access required)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** Limited access (4+ titles identified; full text paywalled; structured abstracts examined via search results and journal metadata)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

> Based on Taylor & Francis standard requirements for economics journals and journal scope statement. Full journal-specific guidelines are behind institutional paywall; these reflect publisher standards and observed journal practice.

### Structure

Follows standard economics article structure (aligned with FIELD_SKILL):
- Introduction
- Data / Methodology section (may be combined or separate depending on paper type)
- Results
- Conclusion
- Optional: Robustness checks, extensions as subsections

### Length

No specific journal limit found in publicly accessible sources. Taylor & Francis journals typically permit 8,000–15,000 words including all text, tables, figures, and appendices. Abstracts are separate from word count.

### Abstract

**Format:** Unstructured prose
**Length:** 50–200 words (Taylor & Francis standard for economics journals)
**Position:** On separate sheet following title page

### Citations

**Format:** Author-year (parenthetical or in-text)
**Style:** References list follows standard format:
- Journal Articles: Author(s). Year. "Article Title." *Journal Name* Volume(Issue): Pages.
- Books: Author(s). Year. *Book Title*. Place of publication: Publisher.
- Book Chapters: Author. Year. "Chapter Title." In *Book Title*, edited by Editor Name(s), page range. Place: Publisher.

### Headings

No journal-specific deviations observed. Follows FIELD_SKILL norm: numbered (Roman or Arabic) or unnumbered section headings acceptable. No mandated heading style.

### Tables and Figures

No journal-specific deviations observed. Follows FIELD_SKILL norm: both common; figures illustrate key variation and model fit; table notes explain definitions and sample restrictions.

### Explicit Prohibitions

None identified beyond FIELD_SKILL baseline (no contractions, no colloquial expressions, high formality).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Methodological Openness

**Delta (RELAXATION):** While FIELD_SKILL baseline assumes econometric or theoretical rigor as standard, this ABS 2 journal **explicitly welcomes a broader range of methodological approaches including case studies and historical research**. The journal scope states: "The journal is open to a variety of methodological approaches ranging from case studies to econometric exercises with sound theoretical modelling, empirical evidence both longitudinal and cross-sectional."

This is a relaxation of the top-tier expectation that econometric or formal theoretical work dominates. A well-designed case study or historical analysis is more readily publishable here than in ABS 4* outlets.

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Voice and Person:** FIELD DEFAULT — First-person plural is standard; active voice strongly preferred.
- **Citation Density:** FIELD DEFAULT — High in introduction and methods, lower in results and conclusion.
- **Sentence and Paragraph Style:** FIELD DEFAULT — Medium to long sentences; high-density paragraphs (4–10 sentences).
- **Hedging and Epistemic Caution:** FIELD DEFAULT — Low hedging intensity; clear claims preferred over diffuse ones.
- **Results Reporting:** FIELD DEFAULT — Tables as primary vehicle; prose highlights key coefficients, magnitudes, and economic significance.
- **Structural Deviations:** FIELD DEFAULT — Follows canonical empirical or theory paper structure.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Case study weakness:** While case studies are welcome, they must be methodologically rigorous. A purely narrative case without explicit comparators, falsifiable claims, or theoretical grounding will not meet publication standards. Ensure case studies engage explicitly with theory or use within-case variation to test propositions.

2. **Under-theorizing applied work:** This journal bridges economic theory and empirical economics. Applied/empirical papers must ground findings in economic theory or clear economic logic. Papers that present results without connecting them to theory or policy implications are less competitive.

3. **Mixing methodologies without integration:** Papers combining case studies, surveys, and econometrics must explicitly justify why multiple methods are necessary and how they integrate. Avoid sequential method stacking without synthesis.

4. **Insufficient engagement with innovation literature:** The journal's scope is narrow (innovation, technology, diffusion). Papers must position themselves within that literature, not adjacent fields. Ensure prior work on innovation mechanisms, adoption patterns, or technology diffusion is adequately covered in the introduction.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

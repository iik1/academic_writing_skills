# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of International Entrepreneurship

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ENT-SBM
- **Publisher:** Springer Nature
- **Scope:** Analyzes internationalization in the context of entrepreneurship, addressing opportunities and challenges. Features high-quality theoretical and empirical research, comparative studies, and evaluations of competing theories. Over 50% of 2025 articles relate to one or more of the 17 Sustainable Development Goals.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://link.springer.com/journal/10843/submission-guidelines (access-restricted; extracted requirements below from available sources)
- **Field baseline:** entrepreneurship-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 12 (titles and abstracts from 2024–2025 across four issues; 2 full text examined via institutional repository and IDEAS/RePEc)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
- Standard empirical structure: Abstract, Keywords, Introduction, Literature/Theory Review, Hypothesis Development (where applicable), Methods, Results, Discussion (including Contributions, Theoretical Implications, Practical Implications subsections), Limitations and Future Research, Conclusion.
- Systematic reviews and conceptual articles also published; follow similar macro spine with thematic organization where appropriate.
- Appendices and supplementary materials permitted; supplementary files referred to as "Online Resource" in text citations.
- Consecutive numbering of figures continues from main text into appendices; figures in online appendices numbered separately.

### Length
No explicit word or page limit stated in publicly available guidelines; Springer policy permits flexible manuscript length provided articles remain appropriately concise for readership. Expectation: length proportionate to contribution, not prescribed.

### Abstract
- **Required length:** 150–250 words.
- **Format:** Should not contain undefined abbreviations or unspecified references.
- **Keywords:** 4–6 keywords required for indexing.

### Citations
Author-date system (parenthetical in-text references):
- Single source: (Author Year), e.g. "(Smith 2020)"
- Two authors: (Author and Author Year), e.g. "(Smith and Jones 2020)"
- Three or more authors: (Author et al. Year), e.g. "(Smith et al. 2020)"

Bibliography formatting:
- **Journal articles:** Author(s) (Year) Article title. *Journal Name* Volume:Pages
- **Books:** Author(s) (Year) *Book Title*. Publisher, Location
- **Book chapters:** Author(s) (Year) Chapter title. In: Editor(s) (eds) *Book Title*. Publisher, Location, pp Pages
- **Web sources:** Author (Year) Title. In: Website Name. Accessed Date

Volume and issue numbers optional for journals; article identifiers may replace page ranges for e-journals.

### Ethical and Compliance Statements
**Required declarations** (on separate title page or in a "Declarations" section):
- Funding sources and acknowledgment of funding bodies
- Competing interests (financial and non-financial; state "no competing interests" if none)
- Ethics approval statement (if human participants, their data, or biological material involved): name of ethics committee, approval status, and reference to Declaration of Helsinki or equivalent ethical standards
- Consent statement (if applicable: informed consent from participants)
- Data, materials, and/or code availability statement
- Authors' contribution statements

Failure to include required declarations may delay publication.

### Headings
Standard Springer convention. Specific hierarchy not detailed in accessible guidelines; inferred from published articles: Level 1 (Introduction, Literature Review/Theory, Methods, Results, Discussion, Conclusion); Level 2 subsections within major sections (e.g., Contributions, Practical Implications within Discussion).

### Tables and Figures
- Must be cited explicitly in text (similar to FIELD_SKILL norm, confirming norm).
- High-quality figures depicting interaction effects, mediation/moderation paths, and time trends common and integral to results narrative (FIELD_SKILL norm confirmed).
- Supplementary figures and tables may be placed in online appendices if space-constrained.
- Standard file formats required for supplementary materials.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Undefined abbreviations in abstract forbidden.
- Unspecified references in abstract forbidden.
- Authors must be correct and complete at submission; adding/deleting authors during revision generally not permitted; no changes to authorship after acceptance.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Omitting or incomplete "Declarations" section** — Springer requires a separate title page or clearly marked section with funding, competing interests, ethics approval, consent, and data availability statements. Submissions missing these are flagged during editorial screening and may be desk-rejected or delayed.

2. **Abbreviations or references in abstract** — The journal explicitly prohibits undefined abbreviations and unspecified references in the 150–250-word abstract. Violations are caught at first editorial review.

3. **Author changes after submission** — The journal does not permit adding or deleting authors during revision. Ensure authorship is final and correct before submission.

4. **Incorrect terminology for supplementary materials** — Refer to supplementary files as "Online Resource" in the text, consistent with Springer convention; avoid "supplementary material," "appendix," or "supplemental information" for files placed online rather than in the main text.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Bioeconomics

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Springer Nature
- **Scope:** Interdisciplinary dialogue between biology and economics — biology explaining economic behavior (behavioral economics, neuroeconomics, evolutionary economics, evolutionary game theory) and economics illuminating "the economy of nature" (bioeconomic modeling of fisheries, wildlife, ecosystems). Also publishes evolutionary-biology-adjacent contributions (behavioral ecology, sociobiology, evolutionary anthropology/psychology) and book reviews.
- **Status note:** The journal ceased publishing new issues after 2023 (Volume 25) and is not currently accepting submissions; Springer's live submission-guidelines page is no longer reachable. Guidance below is reconstructed from archived front matter, article abstracts, and an in-journal "how to write for this journal" column rather than a current author-guidelines page.
- **Guidelines URL:** Not accessible — journal archived by publisher; no live "Submission Guidelines" page found.
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 12 (abstracts/front-matter; full text not accessible) — "Ordinaries 9: How to write a biological economics article" (Burnham & Phelan, 2022); "Biological welfare economics" (Burnham & Phelan, 2023); "Fishing motives and economic effects of climate change" (Gren, Holmgren & Goedkoop, 2023); "Government intervention in wildlife damage management" (Xie, 2022); "The impact of vaccinations and chronic disease on COVID death rates" (Doti, 2023); "Applying evolutionary methods in economics: progress or pitfall?" (Koliofotis, 2021); plus abstracts scanned across the 2013–2023 table-of-contents listing on IDEAS/RePEc for structural/topical patterns.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
PRACTICE ONLY. A meaningful share of this journal's content is essayistic and cross-disciplinary rather than standard empirical/theoretical economics: recurring opinion/perspective columns ("Ordinaries" series by Burnham & Phelan), extended book reviews, obituary/tribute pieces (e.g., the Gordon Tullock memorial issue), and short applied notes. These pieces adopt a more discursive, argumentative essay voice than the tightly identification-focused prose FIELD_SKILL describes for ABS 4/4* empirical papers. Authors writing a conventional empirical or bioeconomic-modeling paper for this journal should still default to FIELD_SKILL voice; authors writing a perspective/review piece should expect more latitude toward essay-style argumentation, direct address to the reader, and explicit "how should economists think about X" framing — a mode FIELD_SKILL does not cover at all.

### Structural Deviations
PRACTICE ONLY. The journal publishes a substantial proportion of non-standard-IMRaD content: single-author book reviews, memorial/tribute essays, and short opinion columns that do not follow Introduction–Data–Method–Results–Conclusion. This is a much higher share of non-empirical-paper content than typical ABS 4/4* general-interest journals, reflecting the journal's small, niche, interdisciplinary community. A submission that is a conventional empirical bioeconomic paper should still follow FIELD_SKILL's empirical-paper structure; the observed deviation applies specifically to essay/review/tribute content types this journal accepts but FIELD_SKILL does not address.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
PRACTICE ONLY. Bioeconomic-modeling articles (fisheries, wildlife management, ecosystem services) are common and blend biological/ecological model components (population dynamics, carrying capacity) with standard economic optimization — expect prose that explains a coupled bio-economic model's mechanics in more expository detail than a pure-econ theory paper would, since the audience includes non-economist biologists. This is consistent with the journal's stated interdisciplinary scope rather than a contradiction of FIELD_SKILL, but authors should not assume a pure-economist readership.

## Common Pitfalls
- Do not assume this is purely an economics journal: a nontrivial share of published content (book reviews, tribute essays, opinion columns) departs from standard empirical/theoretical paper structure. Confirm which content type is intended before applying FIELD_SKILL's empirical-paper template rigidly.
- The journal ceased accepting submissions after 2023; before drafting for actual submission, verify current journal status directly with Springer, since this skill cannot confirm whether the title has been relaunched or fully discontinued as of the current date.
- Because the audience spans economists and biologists, unexplained economics jargon (or unexplained biological/ecological jargon) may land less well here than in a single-discipline ABS 4/4* outlet; more expository framing of technical terms from the "other" discipline is advisable.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

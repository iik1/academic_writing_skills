# Journal Writing Skill: Research on Professional Responsibility and Ethics in Accounting

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ACCOUNT
- **Publisher:** Emerald Publishing Limited
- **Format:** Edited book series (chapters in annual volumes)
- **Scope:** Research and case studies on professional responsibility, ethical judgment, and accountability in accounting; bridges theory and professional practice.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.emeraldgrouppublishing.com/books/prepare-your-manuscript/books-publishing-author-guidelines (general Emerald book guidelines; series-specific guidelines not publicly detailed)
- **Field baseline:** account-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 9 chapters from Vol. 25 (2023), accessed via publisher metadata, ResearchGate listings, and open educational resources

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict. Sourced from Emerald Publishing's official books author guidelines.

### Structure
Chapters follow the standard Emerald book chapter format:
- **Title page** (with author(s) and affiliation(s))
- **Abstract** (required)
- **Main text** (with numbered headings and subsections)
- **References** (APA 6th Edition)
- **Appendices** (if applicable; numbered A1, A2, etc.)

For **case study chapters** specifically: include learning objectives, case narrative or scenario, implementation guidance for instructors, and teaching notes. Case structure may follow: Issue/Context → Case Facts → Questions/Dilemmas → Discussion.

### Length
- **Chapter length:** Approximately 7,000–10,000 words (inclusive of abstract, references, and notes)
- **This is a firm constraint.** Chapters exceeding 10,000 words may be rejected or require substantial revision.

### Abstract
- **Word limit:** 250 words maximum (strict Emerald requirement)
- **Structure:** Not formally specified by Emerald, but practice in this series observes: statement of issue/context, brief description of case or research approach, key findings or implications.
- **Separate fields:** Include up to 6 keywords below the abstract.

### Citations
- **Style:** Author-date throughout (APA 6th Edition format). In-text citations appear as "(Author Year)" or "Author (Year)".
- **Reference list:** Follows APA 6th Edition conventions. All cited works must appear in the reference list.
- **Citation density:** Medium. Case studies and applied chapters tend toward lower density than theoretical chapters; citations ground ethical frameworks and prior case research.

### Headings
- **Level 1:** Arabic numerals (e.g., 1. Introduction, 2. Case Background) or spelled-out (Introduction, Case Background). Emerald book series are flexible here; follow target series conventions or, if unspecified, use Arabic numerals for consistency.
- **Subsection headings:** 1.1, 1.2 style, following the main heading convention.
- **Capitalization:** Title case (standard Emerald practice).

### Tables and Figures
- **Placement:** Can be embedded within text or at the end of the chapter (follow target volume's style).
- **Captions:** Include descriptive captions. Tables should have clear headers and notes explaining any abbreviations or data sources.
- **Numbering in appendices:** Tables/figures in appendices are labeled as Table A1, Figure A1, etc.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- **No formatting requirements for initial submission** — plain text or simple Word formatting is acceptable.
- **No unsolicited permissions issues:** All copyright permissions must be secured before submission (Emerald requires written permission for any third-party material).
- **Ethical clearance required:** Any work involving human subjects must include evidence of informed consent and, where applicable, IRB approval.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

Only dimensions where this series deviates from the ACCOUNT field baseline. Omit sections with no delta.

### Manuscript Type and Purpose
**RELAXATION (field baseline focused on journal research articles; this series encompasses case studies and applied chapters).** While FIELD_SKILL describes the hypothesis-testing, empirical research article structure dominant in ABS 4* journals, this series explicitly publishes case-based pedagogy. Case chapters do not follow the hypothesis-development → empirical results structure. Instead, they present a business or professional scenario, ethical dilemmas, questions for analysis, and sometimes teaching notes. Learning objectives and implementation guidance are required components, not optional.

**Example observed:** "Deficiencies in the Audit of Wirecard: A Case Study of the Enron of Germany" (Miller & Mintz, Vol. 25) frames the Wirecard audit failure as a teaching case with learning objectives and implementation guidance for instructors, diverging from the empirical research article structure.

### Abstract and Framing
**PRACTICE ONLY (observed in series abstracts but not stated in Emerald guidelines).** Abstracts in this series tend to open with a practical problem or ethical issue (e.g., "This case examines…"), then briefly describe the case/research approach, and conclude with implications for practice or teaching. This is more applied and less methodologically focused than FIELD_SKILL abstracts, which emphasize research design and method. FIELD_SKILL abstracts state "we test…" or "we examine…"; this series abstracts often state "this case explores…" or "this study documents…" with emphasis on the ethical or professional dimensions of the issue.

### Voice and Register
**RELAXATION (accessibility and pedagogical emphasis).** This series aims for accessibility to both undergraduate and graduate students; consequently, the register is slightly more accessible than the clinical ABS 4* accounting research baseline. While formal and professional throughout, the writing is less dense with statistical notation and more interpretive. Explanations of ethical frameworks and context are more explicit for teaching purposes. Hedging is calibrated to the pedagogical goal: findings are stated clearly, but ethical interpretations are qualified ("this suggests…," "one interpretation is…").

**Example observed:** The series editor, Tara Shawver, is known for the "Giving Voice to Values" ethical framework, which emphasizes moral agency and decision-making skill. Her editorial influence appears in chapters that address "how would you decide?" questions, requiring more accessible prose than pure research articles.

### Structural Deviations
**ENFORCED (for case study chapters).** Case chapters in this series follow a distinct structure that deviates from FIELD_SKILL's standard research article format:
1. **Introduction/Context:** Sets up the ethical or professional problem.
2. **Case Narrative or Facts:** Describes the scenario (company, situation, ethical dilemma).
3. **Learning Objectives:** Stated explicitly (not present in research articles).
4. **Case Questions or Analysis Framework:** Guides student thinking (not present in research articles).
5. **Discussion/Analysis:** May include implementation guidance for instructors.
6. **Conclusion:** Often tied back to learning objectives.

Empirical chapters (e.g., on tone at the top, whistleblowing) follow closer to FIELD_SKILL structure but remain applied in scope (not pure theory-testing).

### Citation Density
**RELAXATION (lower than FIELD_SKILL in case study chapters).** Case studies prioritize the scenario and analysis over extensive literature review. Ethical frameworks and a few foundational empirical studies are cited, but the citation density is noticeably lower than FIELD_SKILL's high-citation Introduction and Hypothesis Development sections. Empirical chapters in the series (e.g., on earnings management, dark triad traits) maintain higher citation density and follow FIELD_SKILL more closely.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- **Person and active voice:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (first-person plural, active voice throughout).
- **Hedging and epistemic caution:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (calibrated to evidence strength; well-supported claims are declarative; interpretations are hedged).
- **Paragraph and sentence style:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (medium to long sentences, dense paragraphs in analysis sections, technical precision).
- **Discussion and Conclusion:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (brief restatement of key findings, explicit limitations, contributions and implications for practice).
- **Robustness and methodological transparency:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm where applicable (empirical chapters report sensitivity checks; case chapters explain pedagogical design).

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Exceeding 10,000 words:** The chapter word limit is firm. Chapters must fit within 7,000–10,000 words inclusive of abstract and references. Exceeding this risks desk rejection.
2. **Abstract over 250 words:** Strict Emerald requirement. Abstracts longer than 250 words will be rejected or require revision.
3. **Missing learning objectives in case chapters:** If submitting a case study, learning objectives must be explicit and clearly stated. Without them, the pedagogical intent is unclear and the chapter may be rejected as incomplete.
4. **Inadequate ethical framework:** This series expects ethical frameworks or decision-making models to be integrated into the case/analysis. Cases that describe ethical issues without grounding them in ethics theory or a decision model risk being seen as insufficiently rigorous.
5. **APA citation errors:** Emerald requires strict APA 6th Edition formatting. Ensure all in-text citations and references follow the format precisely; inconsistencies invite desk rejection or requests for revision before review.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

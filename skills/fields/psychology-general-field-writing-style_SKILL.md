# Field Writing Style: PSYCH (GENERAL)

## Scope and Intended Use
This baseline describes writing conventions shared across leading general
and social/personality psychology outlets — empirical journals (e.g.,
Psychological Science, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General,
Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, Journal of Personality and
Social Psychology, Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, Journal of
Experimental Psychology: Applied), integrative-review journals (Annual
Review of Psychology, Psychological Bulletin, Psychological Review,
Current Directions in Psychological Science), and the distinctive
open-commentary format of Behavioral and Brain Sciences. Most submissions
to this field are empirical reports; review and theoretical-synthesis
articles form a substantial minority with their own structural norms,
noted separately below. Use this skill as a prior; consult a
journal-specific skill for anything narrower.

## Typical Article Structure
For empirical reports, leading journals in this field expect a canonical
IMRaD structure: Abstract, Introduction, Method, Results, Discussion,
References. A Research Transparency/Open Science statement (data,
materials, and preregistration disclosure) is treated as a near-universal
requirement at the ABS 4*/4 level, typically placed after the abstract or
in the Method section. For multi-study papers, the expected variant
nests Method-Results-Discussion within each study (Study 1, Study 2, ...)
under a general Introduction and closes with a General Discussion that
synthesizes across studies. Tables and figures are integrated near their
first textual reference rather than collected at the end.

Review and theoretical journals (Annual Review of Psychology,
Psychological Bulletin, Psychological Review, Behavioral and Brain
Sciences target articles) depart from IMRaD: they use thematic,
researcher-defined section headings organized around theoretical
constructs, historical development, or moderator/boundary-condition
structure, closing with an explicit statement of open questions and
future-research priorities. Behavioral and Brain Sciences is the one
structurally distinct venue in this baseline: target articles are
followed by numerous short open peer commentaries and an author response,
a format with no equivalent elsewhere in the field.

Practice varies on whether a standalone "Limitations" subsection appears
within Discussion or is folded into general discussion prose. No
consensus norm exists on this point.

## Voice, Person, and Register
Leading journals in this field expect first-person plural ("we") for
describing the authors' own actions and decisions (e.g., "we recruited,"
"we predicted"), combined with impersonal/passive constructions for
describing general phenomena, established findings, or the field's
knowledge state. Active voice is preferred wherever the actor is the
author team; passive voice remains acceptable and common for
procedural description in Method sections. Register is formal but not
ornate: reviewers expect direct, plain declarative prose over literary or
rhetorical flourish. Contractions are avoided; jargon is defined at first
use, and clinical or technical terms are used precisely rather than
loosely as everyday synonyms.

## Sentence and Paragraph Style
Sentences are typically short to medium in length, prioritizing clarity
and one-idea-per-sentence over syntactic complexity. Paragraphs are
short to medium, usually organized around a single claim or finding with
a clear topic sentence. Reviewers expect signposting — explicit
transitions ("First,... Second,...", "In contrast,", "Building on this,")
that make the logical structure traceable without close re-reading.
Nominalizations and heavy subordinate clauses are discouraged relative to
some other social-science fields; this field's prose favors relatively
plain syntax even when the underlying ideas are complex.

## Argumentation and Rhetorical Moves
Introductions follow a recognizable funnel pattern consistent with the
CARS (Create-a-Research-Space) model: establish the broad phenomenon or
theoretical debate, narrow to the specific unresolved question or
inconsistency in prior findings, and state explicitly how the present
study addresses that gap. Leading journals in this field expect the gap
to be framed constructively — as an open empirical or theoretical
question, a boundary condition, or an unresolved inconsistency across
prior studies — rather than as a criticism of specific prior authors.
Explicit, numbered or itemized statements of hypotheses and predictions
before the Method section are expected in confirmatory work; exploratory
work is expected to be labeled as such. Preregistration status (whether
hypotheses, sample size, and analysis plan were specified in advance) is
treated as material information that must be disclosed and is expected
to shape how confirmatory claims are framed.

## Methods Conventions
Method sections are written in past tense and are expected to be dense
with procedural detail sufficient for independent replication: exact
recruitment procedure, exclusion criteria, sample-size justification
(power analysis or resource-based rationale), stimulus materials,
measures with reliability information, and procedural sequence. At the
ABS 4*/4 level, reviewers expect explicit sample-size justification and
disclosure of all excluded participants/observations and the reasons for
exclusion — undisclosed post hoc exclusions are treated as a serious
violation. Multi-study papers are expected to justify the overall
evidentiary strategy (e.g., internal replication, conceptual replication
across operationalizations) rather than presenting each study as
free-standing. Preregistration, materials/data availability, and
manipulation/attention checks are treated as standard reporting
expectations rather than optional detail.

## Results and Reporting Norms
Leading journals in this field expect a strict separation of reporting
from interpretation: the Results section reports what was found, with
interpretation and implications reserved for the Discussion. Statistical
precision is a hard norm — exact test statistics, degrees of freedom,
precise p-values (with a convention of reporting p < .001 rather than
smaller exact values), and effect sizes accompanied by confidence
intervals are expected for every focal test, not merely significant
ones. Reporting a p-value alone without an accompanying effect size is
treated as inadequate at the ABS 4*/4 level. Tables are used for
multi-condition or multi-measure comparisons; prose is used to narrate
the pattern and highlight the tests most relevant to the hypotheses.
Figures with error bars or confidence intervals are expected wherever a
key comparison can be visualized. Robustness is typically demonstrated
through internal or conceptual replication across studies within the
same paper rather than through a single robustness-check appendix, though
supplementary/preregistered robustness analyses are also common.

## Discussion and Conclusion Norms
Discussion sections are expected to open with a concise restatement of
the key findings, then interpret them against the theoretical framing
established in the Introduction, addressing convergence or divergence
from prior literature. A dedicated, explicit treatment of limitations
and boundary conditions is expected — reviewers penalize discussions that
omit or minimize limitations. Broader theoretical and, where relevant,
practical implications are addressed, but claims of practical impact are
expected to be proportionate to the design (e.g., correlational designs
are not permitted to support causal or applied claims). Multi-study
papers close with a General Discussion that synthesizes across studies
rather than repeating each study's individual discussion.

## Citation and Literature Engagement
Citation density is highest in the Introduction and Discussion and
comparatively sparse in Method and Results (confined there mainly to
citing measures, validated instruments, or established analytic
procedures). Citation is used to paraphrase and synthesize prior
findings into a narrative of the field's current state, not to quote
directly — direct quotation of other researchers' text is rare in this
field outside of construct definitions. Disagreement with or
contradiction of prior work is framed empirically and non-personally
(e.g., "prior findings have been mixed," "X and Y report divergent
effects") rather than as a critique of specific authors' competence or
motives. Review articles (Annual Review of Psychology, Psychological
Bulletin, Psychological Review) sustain much higher citation density
throughout, reflecting their synthetic purpose.

## Hedging and Epistemic Caution
Hedging intensity is high relative to many social-science fields. Leading
journals in this field expect causal language to be reserved strictly for
experimental or otherwise causally identified designs; correlational or
observational findings must be described with associational language
("was associated with," "predicted") rather than causal verbs. Claims
about generalizability beyond the studied sample, population, or context
are expected to be explicitly qualified. Overstatement of practical or
real-world implications relative to the evidence is treated as a
significant weakness at the ABS 4*/4 level. Null or mixed results are
expected to be reported and discussed forthrightly rather than
downplayed.

## What This Baseline Does NOT Specify
This baseline does not specify: word or page limits, specific citation
formatting minutiae beyond adherence to a standard style manual, number
of studies expected per paper, specific preregistration platforms or
templates, software/statistical package requirements, figure resolution
or file-format requirements, cover-letter or submission-portal
requirements, or review-timeline expectations. These are journal-specific
and must be sourced from individual journal instructions.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

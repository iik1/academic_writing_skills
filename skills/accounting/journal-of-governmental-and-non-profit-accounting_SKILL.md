# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Governmental & Non-Profit Accounting

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ACCOUNT
- **Publisher:** American Accounting Association (Government and Nonprofit Section)
- **Scope:** Stimulates and reports premier-quality research on a wide range of governmental and nonprofit accounting issues; interdisciplinary in scope, welcoming quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-method approaches.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://aaahq.org/Research/Journals/Journal-of-Governmental-and-Nonprofit-Accounting/Guide-for-Authors
- **Field baseline:** account-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 5 (via title/abstract analysis and editorial summaries; full-text access restricted; see Sources for methodology)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows AAA Manuscript Preparation Guide: I. INTRODUCTION, II. [sections], References, Figures, Tables, Appendices. First-level headings use Roman numerals, CENTERED, BOLD, ALL CAPS. Second-level headings are flush left, bold, mixed case. Formal section ordering is enforced; deviation from canonical structure (intro→literature/theory→methods→results→discussion) is atypical.

### Length
Approximately 7,000 words (20–25 total pages), including all elements. This is a hard constraint; submissions significantly above 25 pages are flagged during editorial screening.

### Abstract
No more than 150 words. Single paragraph, unstructured (does not require explicit labels for Methods/Results/Conclusion). Must state research question, primary method or data, and key findings.

### Citations
Author-date style throughout (AAA standard): (Author Year) or Author (Year) depending on grammatical role. Multiple citations separated by semicolons, in alphabetical order. All in-text citations must appear in References; all references must be cited in text. No footnotes for citations; footnotes reserved only for extensions or useful excursions of information.

### Headings
First-level: CENTERED, BOLD, ALL CAPS, ROMAN NUMERALS (I., II., III., etc.)
Second-level: Flush left, bold, mixed case
Third-level: Flush left, bold italic, mixed case
Fourth-level: Paragraph indent, bold lowercase

### Tables and Figures
Arabic-numbered with complete descriptive titles. Every table and figure must be mentioned in text. No "Insert Table X here" placeholders. Tables created in Word table editor or Excel, not keyboard spacing. Figures submitted as separate files in EPS, TIFF, PDF, JPEG, or PNG format. Variables defined in tables or appendix with italicized formatting. Line art 600 dpi black/white bitmap; halftones 264 dpi grayscale. Include alt-text for accessibility.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Manuscripts under simultaneous consideration elsewhere are desk-rejected.
- Human subjects research without IRB approval/exemption is grounds for desk rejection.
- Failure to disclose AI-assisted writing or research tools is a violation; disclosure required.
- Plagiarism detected via CrossCheck is grounds for rejection and potential sanctions.
- Conflict of interest must be disclosed by all authors.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Manuscript Length
**ENFORCED (RELAXATION):** The journal solicits "approximately 7,000 words (20–25 total pages)" compared to the 55-page baseline permitted by most ABS 4*/4 AAA journals. This is a significant constraint: ABS 1 journals are expected to enforce tighter page budgets. Authors cannot match the depth of results presentation possible in higher-tier journals; this shapes results section organization (fewer robustness checks, more distilled findings reporting).

### Breadth of Research Traditions
**ENFORCED:** The journal explicitly welcomes "a broad array of research traditions and approaches" — quantitative, qualitative, mixed-method, descriptive, and theoretical work all appear in the same volume. FIELD_SKILL is derived from quantitative archival/experimental work at ABS 4* level. At ABS 1, the baseline structure (hypothesis development → empirical results → mechanism tests) is less universal. Qualitative papers, literature reviews, and descriptive analyses are more common relative to the ABS 4* core tradition. This does NOT override the canonical structure where it applies, but reviewers at this journal tolerate greater variance in section sequencing and naming for non-standard research designs.

### Editorial Screening for Fit
**ENFORCED:** The Editorial Policy states: "After screening by the editorial board for suitability, submissions will be reviewed by qualified reviewers." This two-stage process (screening first, peer review second) is standard at AAA journals but worth noting: manuscripts that fail suitability screening are desk-rejected without full peer review. Fit to governmental or nonprofit context is a gate-keeping criterion at this journal; a technically sound study on a general accounting question may be rejected as out of scope.

### Tone and Register: Accessibility Balance
**PRACTICE:** Articles in this journal tend toward slightly less technical prose than ABS 4* journals. The AAA Manuscript Preparation Guide requires text to be "relatively nontechnical, yet clear enough for an informed reader." At ABS 4*, technical density is expected. At ABS 1, clarity for practitioners and policy audiences is valued alongside rigor. Hedge language is less cautious; results are stated more directly even when mechanisms are not fully identified. This reflects the journal's goal to "report to the academic and practice communities" — practitioners cannot engage with overly hedged claims.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Exceeding the page budget without justification.** The 20–25 page constraint is firm. Submissions must be distilled; tables cannot be padded, and appendices must be essential. Reviewers flag overlong manuscripts immediately.

2. **Misalignment with journal scope.** The journal prioritizes governmental or nonprofit context. A methodologically sound study on, say, earnings management in public companies is out of scope unless explicitly tied to public-sector or nonprofit governance. Check the scope statement and recent articles before submitting; editorial screening weeds out off-topic work.

3. **Insufficient disclosure of AI usage.** The journal requires explicit disclosure of any generative AI or AI-assisted tools used in research, writing, or analysis. Omitting this disclosure is treated as a violation. If you used ChatGPT, Copilot, or similar tools for drafting or code, state it clearly on the title page.

4. **Mixing methodologies without clear justification.** While the journal welcomes diverse approaches, mixing quantitative and qualitative components without explaining their integration (e.g., qual methods for exploratory interviews, quant for validation) confuses reviewers. Clarity on research design hierarchy is expected.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Essays in Economic and Business History

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** BUS HIST & ECON HIST
- **Publisher:** The Economic & Business History Society
- **Scope:** Peer-reviewed articles in economic and business history covering topics ranging from industrial development and labor to firm history, technological change, and economic policy. The journal publishes both narrative-analytical pieces and quantitative economic history.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.ebhsoc.org/journal/index.php/ebhs/about/submissions
- **Field baseline:** business-economic-history-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 5 (Jackson Battista "Deindustrialization of Detroit," Fourie & Norling "Women's Employment," Susie Pak "How to Be a Success in Business," Deacle & Economopoulos "Banknote Pricing," and Peshko "Apprenticeships and Formal Business Schooling")

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows narrative-analytical form. Front matter: title, author name(s), affiliations, email addresses on cover page. Second page: title, abstract (~100 words), then article text begins. Conclude with Works Cited section. Optional Acknowledgements section permitted after conclusion.

### Length
Hard limit: 12,000 words maximum, inclusive of footnotes, figures, tables, and Works Cited. Brief letters are limited to 2,500 words including references and figure captions.

### Abstract
Approximately 100 words. Appears on second page before article text begins. Non-technical summary of puzzle, argument, and contribution. Format unstructured (single paragraph), not structured (background/methods/results).

### Citations
Author/date method with Works Cited section. In-text citations appear as "Author (Year)" or "Author (Year, page#)." Footnotes use shortened citations like "Bernstein (2008, 135)." Full citations appear only in Works Cited. Footnotes (not endnotes) numbered sequentially 1, 2, 3, etc.

### Headings
Subheadings flush left, bolded, same font as body text (12pt Times New Roman). No special heading hierarchy specified beyond this general rule.

### Tables and Figures
Label sequentially ("Table #" or "Figure #") in bold. Use Times New Roman 12pt. Include source citations. Reference explicitly in text. No grid lines; horizontal lines optional. Figures and tables count toward the 12,000-word limit.

### Formatting (Final Version)
Microsoft Word in 12pt Times New Roman, single-spaced, page numbers centered at bottom. Use American English spelling throughout (except original spellings in quotations and Works Cited titles). No underlining except for URLs.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- No previously published material
- No simultaneous submissions to other journals
- No grid lines in tables/figures
- No underlining except URLs

## Dimensions With No Delta
- **Narrative-analytical structure and voice:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Articles follow chronological or thematic organization with narrative progression, not social-science hypothesis-testing structure (outside quantitative economic history pieces).
- **First-person vs. impersonal construction:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Both first-person plural ("we") and impersonal third-person constructions ("this article") are acceptable; practice varies by article type and sub-tradition.
- **Sentence and paragraph style:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Medium to long sentences with qualifying clauses and substantial paragraph development are normal and valued.
- **Methods description:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Substantive procedural detail required for sources and data; past tense dominates. Quantitative work follows econometric conventions; qualitative work embeds methods in prose.
- **Direct quotation from primary sources:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Direct quotation from archives, firm records, correspondence, and testimony is a valued evidentiary practice.
- **Hedging and epistemic caution:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Moderate hedging with language like "suggests," "appears to have," "likely reflects" is normal. Confident central arguments are expected.
- **Discussion and conclusion norms:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Conclusions restate contribution, connect specific case/dataset to broader debates, and address generalizable implications.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Exceeding 12,000 words:** The strict word limit (inclusive of all elements) can be challenging for narrative-analytical work that typically develops multiple episodes or actors across time. Plan for tighter drafting than ABS 4 journals permit.

2. **Using endnotes instead of footnotes:** Even if your writing software defaults to endnotes, you must convert to sequentially numbered footnotes. This is non-negotiable in final submission.

3. **Mixing citation styles or Works Cited formatting:** The author/date method with shortened footnote citations requires consistency. Using full citations in footnotes or mixing Chicago author-date with other styles will require revision.

4. **Grid lines in tables:** Tables with grid lines are common in quantitative economic history but explicitly prohibited here. Strip all grid lines; use horizontal lines only at top, bottom, and (optionally) between header and data rows.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Field Writing Style: PUB SEC

## Scope and Intended Use

This skill encodes field-level writing conventions for the Public Sector
field (public administration, public management, and governance
scholarship) as observed at the ABS 4* and ABS 4 level. It is a baseline
prior, not a substitute for journal-specific guidance. Journal-specific
skills should be consulted first where they exist; this document fills
in gaps and provides the default expectation when no journal-specific
rule applies. At the ABS 4*/4 level, reviewers expect manuscripts to
read as contributions to an interdisciplinary but socially scientific
field that draws heavily on political science, sociology, psychology,
and economics, while remaining anchored in questions of governance,
administration, and public management.

## Typical Article Structure

Leading journals in this field expect a near-universal macro structure:
a structured abstract stating the problem, approach, and headline
finding; an introduction that opens with a real-world governance
problem before narrowing to the scholarly gap; a literature/theory
section that develops hypotheses or propositions explicitly (often
numbered); a research design or methods section describing data,
case selection, and estimation strategy; a results section; and a
discussion/conclusion section that separates empirical findings from
their practical and theoretical implications. Explicit numbered
hypotheses (e.g., "Hypothesis 1:") are common but not universal in
quantitative and mixed-methods work; qualitative and conceptual pieces
instead use explicit propositions or organizing questions. Limitations
are almost always addressed directly, typically in the discussion or
conclusion, and are often followed by a concrete future-research
agenda. A dedicated "Data Availability" statement and, increasingly, a
statement of pre-registration or supplementary online materials are
now standard features at this level. Practitioner-facing implications
are frequently pulled out as their own subsection or explicit set of
bullet points, reflecting the field's dual audience of scholars and
practicing administrators.

## Voice, Person, and Register

First-person plural ("we argue," "we find," "we contribute") is the
dominant voice for framing contributions and stating findings; this is
consensus across the field's top journals. Passive constructions
remain common in describing procedures and general theoretical claims,
producing a voice that blends first-person authorial stance with
impersonal description of mechanisms and institutions. The register is
formal and academic throughout, but tolerates more discursive,
essayistic prose than is typical in economics — sentences frequently
carry normative or policy-relevant asides, and moral or democratic
stakes (equity, trust, legitimacy, accountability) are named
explicitly rather than left implicit. Jargon specific to the field
(street-level bureaucracy, administrative burden, principal-agent vs.
stewardship framings, co-production, representative bureaucracy) is
used freely once introduced, on the assumption of a specialist
audience, but is typically defined briefly at first use for
accessibility to the field's broad readership.

## Sentence and Paragraph Style

Sentences are typically medium to long, often compound, and willing to
carry multiple embedded clauses and citations. Paragraphs are
built around a single claim supported by multiple citations, followed
by elaboration or a caveat. Field norms tolerate longer paragraphs
than in more terse quantitative fields, especially in literature
review and discussion sections, where paragraphs synthesize several
studies before advancing the authors' own position. Numbered or
bulleted lists are used more freely than in economics-adjacent fields,
particularly for hypotheses, contributions, and practitioner
takeaways.

## Argumentation and Rhetorical Moves

The field follows a recognizable CARS-like pattern (establish
territory, identify a niche, occupy the niche) but with a distinctive
emphasis: introductions typically open with a concrete governance
problem or policy tension before establishing the academic
territory. Contributions are framed explicitly and often numbered
("Our findings make three important contributions..."), a strong and
consistent convention across ABS 4*/4 outlets. Gaps are framed as
underexplored theoretical mechanisms, an unresolved empirical debate
between two literatures, or a policy-relevant question that prior work
has not tested causally. Authors are expected to position their study
relative to at least two contrasting theoretical perspectives (e.g.,
principal-agent vs. stewardship theory; taste-based vs. statistical
vs. implicit-bias discrimination) and to state clearly which
perspective their evidence supports or complicates — outright rejection
of an entire prior literature is rare; more common is a "contingent
and modest" framing that qualifies rather than overturns prior claims.
Disagreement with other scholars is handled through juxtaposition of
findings and contrasting citations rather than sharp rhetorical
critique.

## Methods Conventions

Methods sections use past tense to describe what was done and are
expected to be detailed enough to support replication, including
explicit discussion of identification strategy, sample construction,
and threats to validity. Quantitative papers commonly use causal
identification strategies (regression discontinuity, difference-in-
differences, survey and field experiments, instrumental variables) and
report these with the same procedural rigor found in applied
economics — including explicit validity checks (e.g., density tests,
placebo thresholds, balance tables) and robustness sections presented
either in the main text or supplementary appendices. A pre-registration
statement or reference to an OSF/AsPredicted registration is
increasingly expected for experimental designs. Qualitative and mixed
methods work (case studies, process tracing, interview-based designs)
remain fully legitimate at the ABS 4*/4 level and are held to
comparable standards of transparency about case selection and
analytic procedure, though specific reporting conventions vary by
method. Practice varies: some journals favor primarily quantitative,
causally identified designs while others publish a substantial share
of qualitative and conceptual work. No single methodological
orthodoxy exists across the field's top journals.

## Results and Reporting Norms

Quantitative results are reported with precise coefficients, standard
errors, and p-values in tables, accompanied by prose that restates the
substantive (not just statistical) magnitude of effects — reviewers
expect authors to translate coefficients into percentage changes or
real-world quantities relevant to practitioners. Figures (event-study
plots, coefficient plots, reduced-form scatterplots) are used
extensively alongside tables rather than as a substitute for them.
Results sections are typically organized around the hypotheses or
research questions stated earlier, addressed in the same order, with
explicit statements of support, non-support, or partial support for
each. Robustness and heterogeneity analyses (by subgroup, time period,
or alternative specification) are standard and are reported with the
same level of statistical detail as main results. For qualitative
work, results are organized thematically or chronologically with
direct engagement with primary source material, though this baseline
does not further specify qualitative reporting conventions, as
practice varies substantially by method.

## Discussion and Conclusion Norms

The discussion/conclusion is expected to do more than restate
findings: it explicitly separates the paper's contributions to theory
from its contributions to practice, often in distinct subsections or
paragraphs. Limitations are addressed candidly and specifically (unit
of analysis, generalizability, alternative explanations, potential
confounders) rather than as a token afterthought, and authors
routinely propose specific future studies that would resolve the
identified limitations. A closing return to the paper's normative or
practical stakes — equity, trust, accountability, effective governance
— is a consistent convention, tying the empirical contribution back to
the real-world problem raised in the introduction.

## Citation and Literature Engagement

Citation density is high throughout, including in the introduction and
literature review, where multiple citations are frequently stacked
after a single claim to demonstrate the breadth of supporting
evidence. Author-date (parenthetical) citation style is the norm.
Paraphrase dominates; direct quotation is used sparingly and mainly for
precise conceptual definitions (e.g., quoting a foundational
definition of trust or administrative burden) rather than for
evidentiary support. Disagreement or tension between studies is
typically presented through neutral juxtaposition ("while X finds...,
Y finds...") rather than pointed critique. Citing multiple theoretical
traditions side by side (economics, psychology, sociology, political
science) within a single paragraph is common and expected, reflecting
the field's interdisciplinary self-understanding.

## Hedging and Epistemic Caution

Hedging intensity is moderate to high. Causal claims are qualified
with explicit statements about the scope conditions under which
effects hold (e.g., specific institutional settings, time periods, or
populations), and authors are expected to flag alternative
explanations for their own findings even when their preferred
interpretation is well supported. Words and phrases signaling caution
("may," "appears to," "provides suggestive evidence," "we cannot rule
out") are common in both quantitative and qualitative work. Strong,
unqualified causal or normative claims are avoided in favor of framing
contributions as adding evidence to an ongoing debate rather than
settling it definitively.

## What This Baseline Does NOT Specify

This baseline does not specify: word or page limits, abstract length
limits, formatting or reference-style minutiae, submission or peer
review procedures, specific statistical software or package
citations, or any journal-specific structural requirements (e.g.,
mandatory "Points for Practitioners" boxes, specific section naming
conventions, or appendix formatting rules). It also does not resolve
whether a given journal favors quantitative, qualitative, or mixed
methods — practice varies across the field's top outlets, and
journal-specific skills should be consulted for that determination.
Where a journal-specific skill conflicts with guidance here, the
journal-specific skill takes precedence.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

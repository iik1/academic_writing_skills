# Journal Writing Skill: Economic History of Developing Regions

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** BUS HIST & ECON HIST
- **Publisher:** Routledge/Taylor & Francis (on behalf of Economic History Society of Southern Africa)
- **Scope:** Promotes study of economic change in the Global South (Africa, Asia, Latin America, Middle East). Explores influence of historical events on economic development beyond the industrialized North. Welcomes papers from multiple disciplines (general history, development economics, cliometrics, business history, labour history, financial history) provided they maintain economic history focus.
- **Editors:** Alfonso Herranz-Loncan, Alexander Klein, Jessica Vechbanyongratana
- **Guidelines URL:** Taylor & Francis Online (https://www.tandfonline.com/journals/rehd20) — detailed author guidelines not publicly accessible without authentication
- **Field baseline:** business-economic-history-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 11 articles from 2024-2025 (see Sources section)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> Author guidelines are behind Taylor & Francis paywall; the following constraints are inferred from article analysis and journal description rather than official documentation.

### Structure
Manuscripts follow the structure described in FIELD_SKILL with no apparent deviation: introduction establishing puzzle and contribution, literature/historiographical review (often woven into introduction), methods/sources section (for empirically dense work), substantive sections organized chronologically or thematically, and conclusion restating contribution. No formal "Results" sections observed in non-quantitative work; quantitative articles may include formal data/methods/results structure. Abstracts present before main text, non-author-identified. [FIELD DEFAULT]

### Length
Article page ranges observed: 30–40 pages typical. Exact word limits not stated publicly. Scope appears broader than top-tier ABS 4 journals (which enforce tight limits), consistent with ABS 2 tier. [PRACTICE ONLY: inferred from sample articles]

### Abstract
Non-technical summaries of puzzle, argument, and contribution observed. Compact format. Structured vs. unstructured: mix observed, no consistent pattern. Exact word limit not publicly documented. [FIELD DEFAULT]

### Citations
High citation density throughout, consistent with FIELD_SKILL. Harvard author-date style (inferred from article samples and Taylor & Francis house style). Both paraphrase and direct quotation of primary sources observed. [FIELD DEFAULT]

### Headings
Heading conventions not explicitly stated in publicly available documentation. Articles use numbered or unnumbered section headings. No apparent deviation from FIELD_SKILL conventions. [FIELD DEFAULT]

### Tables and Figures
Tables and figures clearly sourced and accompanied by prose interpretation, consistent with FIELD_SKILL. Quantitative papers include regression output and statistical tables. [FIELD DEFAULT]

### Submission Format
Double-anonymized peer review via ScholarONE Manuscripts platform. No other explicit formatting prohibitions publicly documented. [PRACTICE ONLY]

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Methodological Diversity
FIELD_SKILL notes that quantitative economic history converges toward applied-economics norms while qualitative/narrative work follows historiographical conventions. Economic History of Developing Regions explicitly states it "accepts papers based on purely quantitative or qualitative methods, as well as any combination of the two" — a deliberate broadening of scope that embraces hybrid approaches. Articles sampled show strong quantitative presence (numeracy estimation using age-heaping, gravity models for colonial trade, land-use suitability analysis, literacy econometrics) alongside narrative-historical work. This represents a RELAXATION of the field baseline's tendency to bifurcate: at ABS 2 tier, explicit welcome of mixed methods is stated where top-tier journals may assume it without foregrounding. [GUIDELINE ONLY: stated in journal description, not enforcement in articles]

### Disciplinary Reach
FIELD_SKILL presumes the field engages "economic history, business history, and relevant social science literatures." Economic History of Developing Regions explicitly solicits papers from "general history, development economics, cliometrics, business history, labour history, financial history, development studies and others" — a noticeably broader and more explicitly interdisciplinary framing. Articles sampled include work by economic geographers (urban suitability analysis), social/business historians (colonial trade patterns, industrial elite studies), development scholars (colonial policy effects), and demographers (numeracy estimation). This represents a WIDENING of disciplinary reach compared to FIELD_SKILL, appropriate to ABS 2 journal serving the Global South research community. [PRACTICE ONLY: observed in article authorship and framing, not stated as explicit requirement]

### Global South Focus and Postcolonial Lens
FIELD_SKILL baseline is derived from UK/US leading journals (Business History, Business History Review, Economic History Review) with implicit Western/industrialized-world focus. Economic History of Developing Regions centers the Global South explicitly. Articles sampled demonstrate consistent attention to non-Western contexts, colonial and postcolonial dynamics, and institutional/policy variation across regions. Voice remains scholarly and measured (consistent with FIELD_SKILL) but arguments often foreground agency and institutional complexity of non-Western actors. Example: analysis of contract enforcement among Moroccan business elite, numeracy development in African regions, land policy effects in Mexico. This is not a stylistic or rhetorical delta per se, but a thematic and evidential focus that shapes article framing and contributions. [PRACTICE ONLY: observed thematically, not stated as stylistic requirement]

### Citation of Global South Literatures
Articles sampled show high citation density of scholars from and writing about the Global South (African historians, Asian economic historians, Latin American scholars). This extends FIELD_SKILL's expectation of "genuinely international" engagement to explicit inclusion of non-Anglophone and non-Western scholarship. [PRACTICE ONLY: observed in article samples, no explicit guideline found]

## Dimensions With No Delta
- **Voice and register:** FIELD DEFAULT (formal scholarly, narrative-analytical, impersonal or first-person plural both acceptable)
- **Sentence and paragraph style:** FIELD DEFAULT (medium-to-long sentences with subordinate clauses; substantial paragraphs organized around episodes or mechanisms)
- **Argumentation and CARS pattern:** FIELD DEFAULT (establish phenomenon, show incompleteness/gaps in prior treatment, stake contribution; revisionist framing valued)
- **Results and reporting norms:** FIELD DEFAULT (quantitative work follows econometric convention; qualitative/narrative integrates evidence throughout; direct quotation from primary sources valued)
- **Discussion and conclusion norms:** FIELD DEFAULT (restate contribution, situate in broader historiographical/economic debates, address implications, acknowledge evidence limitations)
- **Hedging and epistemic caution:** FIELD DEFAULT (moderate hedging; confident central claims expected alongside appropriate qualifications on contested/incomplete evidence)

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Dismissing non-Western scholarship or institutional context:** This journal centers the Global South and values scholars working on and from these regions. Papers that treat Global South institutions, policies, or actors as peripheral or treat Western precedents as the default standard may receive skeptical reception. Ensure non-Western scholarship is genuinely engaged, not footnoted.

2. **Overlooking postcolonial dynamics:** Even papers not explicitly about colonialism should acknowledge colonial legacies where relevant to institutional, economic, or political-economic context. Articles sampled show consistent attention to how colonial and postcolonial institutions shape economic outcomes.

3. **Under-specifying methods for archival/qualitative work:** Like the field baseline (FIELD_SKILL), this journal expects transparency on sources and interpretive choices. Archival and qualitative papers should describe source limitations, biases, and what perspectives are absent from the record — this is particularly important for work on historically marginalized regions and populations.

4. **Assuming Western or anglophone reader familiarity:** Global South contexts, institutions, and historical events should be introduced clearly enough that readers unfamiliar with the specific region can follow. This is standard good practice but especially important for a journal reaching diverse international readership.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

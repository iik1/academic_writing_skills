# Journal Writing Skill: Advances in Decision Sciences

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Asia University, Taiwan
- **Scope:** Publishes original research and review articles across decision sciences broadly defined — Accounting, Economics, Finance, Management, Marketing, and applications of decision-making theory in related disciplines. Empirical papers spotted range from asset pricing and market microstructure to tourism, education, and EEG-based machine learning classification.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://iads.site/manuscript-format/ and https://iads.site/paper-submission/ (note: this appears to be a secondary/mirror publisher site "Scientific and Business World, Singapore" carrying the same journal branding; the canonical publisher-of-record is Asia University, Taiwan, journal.asia.edu.tw/index.php/ADS, which could not be directly fetched — DNS resolution failed in this environment. Guidelines content is corroborated by consistent structured-abstract practice observed in actual published articles, so it is treated as reliable for this journal even though sourced from the mirror site.)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No mandated body-section list beyond the structured abstract (see below). Full-text structure was not verifiable (front-matter/abstract access only); assume FIELD_SKILL's empirical-paper structure applies to the body unless contradicted.

### Length
Research articles: 6,000–12,000 words total including references and appendices (per manuscript-format page) — a competing submissions page states 6,000–10,000 words for research articles and a 6,000-word cap for review papers. Treat 6,000–10,000 as the safe target and do not exceed 10,000 without reason. Budget ~280 words per table/figure. Title capped at 15 words.

### Abstract
**Mandatory structured abstract** with labeled subsections — the exact label set varies slightly by issue but core elements are: Purpose (or "Research aims"), Design/Methodology/Approach, Findings (or "Research findings"), Originality/Value (or "Theoretical contribution/Originality"). Some articles add Practitioner/Policy Implications and Research Limitations as additional labeled subsections. Max 250 words total including keywords and article classification. Up to 12 keywords. This is an Emerald-style structured abstract, not the unstructured single-paragraph abstract typical of ABS 4*/4 econ journals.

### Citations
APA 7th edition (not the author-year-in-running-text econ convention per se, but APA's specific formatting rules for in-text citations and the reference list). All references must include a DOI where one exists.

### Headings
Concise, clearly hierarchical; journal provides a template. No specifics beyond this obtained.

### Tables and Figures
Must follow APA 7th edition table/figure formatting and be embedded in the manuscript (not collected at the end). Superscripts/asterisks require explanatory notes.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Endnotes and footnotes are prohibited entirely — any supplementary/qualifying material that FIELD_SKILL would place in a footnote must be integrated into the main text.
- "Avoid first-person pronouns throughout the abstract and manuscript body" — a direct, explicit reversal of FIELD_SKILL's first-person-plural ("we") default.
- PDF-only submission is not accepted; Word or LaTeX required (submission mechanics, not a writing-style constraint).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
ENFORCED (stated in guidelines; consistent with structured-abstract practice, though full-text voice could not be directly verified due to paywalled/inaccessible full text). Guidelines explicitly prohibit first-person pronouns. This is a direct reversal of FIELD_SKILL's "we"-as-default norm — authors should write in third person/passive or agentless constructions ("this study examines," "the analysis reveals") rather than "we examine," "we find."

### Structural Deviations
ENFORCED (confirmed across all 6 non-editorial spot-checked articles from 2022 onward: Naqvi et al. 2022, both 2023 articles, and consistent with journal's own template). The structured, labeled abstract (Purpose / Design-Methodology-Approach / Findings / Originality-Value, sometimes plus Implications/Limitations) replaces FIELD_SKILL's unstructured abstract entirely. Note: this practice appears to postdate ~2021 — the two pre-2022 articles sampled (2019, 2020) used plain narrative abstracts without labeled headers, suggesting an editorial policy change rather than a norm in force for the journal's whole history. For current submissions, treat the structured format as mandatory.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
PRACTICE ONLY (observed, not stated in guidelines). The journal accepts a notably broad topical range under the "decision sciences" umbrella — several sampled articles are not conventional economics papers (EEG/GAN emotion classification, STEM education pedagogy) but are framed with economics-adjacent JEL codes and decision-theoretic language. Authors targeting this journal from an economics angle should expect a more interdisciplinary reviewer pool than at an ABS 4/4* econ journal, and may find it useful to make the decision-science framing (uncertainty, choice, optimization) explicit even in applied or interdisciplinary papers.

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting an unstructured abstract in the FIELD_SKILL style — this journal requires the four-to-seven-part labeled structured abstract; an ordinary paragraph abstract will need reformatting before review.
- Using "we" throughout the draft — guidelines explicitly ask for its avoidance; a paper transplanted from an ABS 4/4* econ draft will need systematic voice revision.
- Placing qualifications or asides in footnotes — not permitted; must be folded into body text or an appendix.
- Missing DOIs in the reference list — APA 7 + mandatory DOI is checked at submission.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: African Affairs

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** IB&AREA
- **Publisher:** Oxford University Press, on behalf of the Royal African Society
- **Scope:** Interdisciplinary journal focused on the politics and international
  relations of sub-Saharan Africa, extending to sociology, anthropology,
  economics, and (where relevant to contemporary African debates) history,
  literature, art, and music. Explicitly targets a broad readership of
  academics, policymakers, business leaders, and the interested public,
  not only specialists.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://academic.oup.com/afraf/pages/General_Instructions
  (plus a separate house style sheet PDF, last updated January 2014)
- **Field baseline:** international-business-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 10 (Kony 2025 [124/497]; Rethinking 'farmer-herder'
  conflicts in the Ivorian internal frontier [123/493]; Property Confiscation
  in the Zanzibar Revolution [124/496]; Brutalism book review [123/492];
  2024 Botswana elections and the demise of the BDP [advance article,
  adag005]; Inequality Regimes in Africa [122/486]; Research with African
  Adolescents [121/484, Research Note]; Covid-19 Pandemic Response and the
  New Era of Austerity in Africa [124/497]; war in Ukraine, the African
  Union, and African agency [122/489]; Africa-Asia: a tale of worldmaking
  [123/491]) — abstracts, section-heading lists, and article-type labels
  via publisher abstract pages; full text was paywalled in all cases.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Articles must open with an introduction that explicitly addresses: what the
article covers, its originality, its research foundation, its contemporary
importance, and its intended audience. Beyond the introduction and a closing
conclusion, the guidelines explicitly offer authors a menu of narrative
structures rather than a single template: chronological, comparative
case-study, thematic, "keyhole" (small-scale process illuminating a broader
context), or "funnel" (narrowing from a wide focus to a specific point or
event). No hypotheses/data/methods/results sequence is prescribed or
expected.

### Length
Articles must not exceed 8,000 words, excluding footnotes. Manuscripts must
be double-spaced.

### Abstract
Maximum 200 words, unstructured, typed on a separate page, stating the
article's major argument and its significance.

### Citations
Governed by a separate house style sheet (PDF, last updated January 2014);
authors must format according to this house style rather than a generic
field convention.

### Headings
No fixed heading scheme is mandated; headings should reflect whichever
narrative structure (chronological, comparative, thematic, keyhole, funnel)
the author has chosen.

### Tables and Figures
Alt text is mandatory for all images, illustrations, and photographs, placed
directly under the figure legend and preceded by "Alt text:".

### Explicit Prohibitions
- No previously published work or working papers.
- No simultaneous submission to other publications.
- Personal memoirs are unlikely to be reviewed.
- Manuscripts must be anonymized for double-anonymized peer review.
- Long, substantive footnotes are discouraged: "points important to the
  argument should come in the text; notes should be for references, or
  brief disclaimers."
- Substantial quotations (longer than a sentence) must be block-indented,
  in smaller type, without quotation marks, with a blank line above and
  below — not run into the paragraph.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
RELAXATION. The guidelines explicitly instruct authors to favor plain,
jargon-free prose over disciplinary or theoretical language, and to explain
any theoretical terms that would not be widely understood outside the
discipline: "a good academic writer is able to express such ideas simply."
This is a deliberate loosening of FIELD_SKILL's tolerance for dense,
qualified, specialist prose, reflecting the journal's explicit aim to reach
policymakers and general readers alongside academics. ENFORCED — spot-check
of abstracts confirms accessible, low-jargon phrasing even for
theoretically framed pieces (e.g., "inequality regimes," "internal
frontier," "worldmaking").

### Structural Deviations
ENFORCED. None of the ten spot-checked articles use an IMRAD-style or
hypothesis-driven skeleton. Section headings are uniformly descriptive and
argument-specific (e.g., "Accumulation through slavery," "The demise of the
BDP," "Bandung as alterity: five principles of Afro-Asia cooperation")
rather than generic labels like "Theory," "Data and Methods," "Results," or
"Discussion." There is no separate literature-review, hypotheses, or
methods section as a matter of course, even in an ABS-3 IB&AREA journal
context. This is a real structural deviation from FIELD_SKILL's canonical
empirical-paper template, not just a relaxation of optional sections.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
PRACTICE ONLY. The journal runs several distinct short-form article types
alongside standard research articles: "Briefings" (short, timely pieces on
current events, e.g., election outcomes), "Research Notes" (short pieces
on methodological/ethical issues in African studies), and book reviews,
each carrying its own abstract/structure conventions lighter than a
full research article (e.g., Briefings observed here run 3-5 descriptive
sections plus a conclusion, no abstract citation apparatus beyond prose).
Authors should identify which article type they are targeting, since
length and structural expectations differ by type; only the standard
8,000-word / 200-word-abstract rule is stated as a hard constraint for
full articles.

ENFORCED. Block-quotation formatting (indented, smaller type, no quotation
marks, blank line above/below) is a specific mechanical convention not
addressed in FIELD_SKILL.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- Citation density and paraphrase-over-quotation norm: FIELD DEFAULT
- Hedging and epistemic caution: FIELD DEFAULT
- Argumentation via CARS-style introduction (topic, gap, contribution): FIELD DEFAULT, adapted to the journal's own required introduction content (see Hard Constraints > Structure)
- Discussion/conclusion looping back to stated contribution: FIELD DEFAULT

---

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting a conventional IMRAD or hypothesis-testing structure: this
  journal expects a chosen narrative form (chronological, comparative,
  thematic, keyhole, or funnel) with argument-specific headings instead.
- Writing in dense theoretical register without glossing terms: the
  journal explicitly penalizes unexplained jargon given its broad,
  non-specialist readership.
- Running long substantive footnotes: material central to the argument
  belongs in the main text, not notes.
- Using inline quotation marks for quotations longer than a sentence
  instead of block-indenting them.
- Missing alt text on figures/images, which the journal now treats as a
  mandatory accessibility requirement.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

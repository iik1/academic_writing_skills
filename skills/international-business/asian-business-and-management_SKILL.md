# Journal Writing Skill: Asian Business and Management

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** IB&AREA
- **Publisher:** Springer Nature (Palgrave Macmillan)
- **Scope:** Multidisciplinary coverage of Asian business and management across corporate governance, internationalization, emerging markets, corporate social responsibility, political-business ties, and organizational culture and performance.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://link.springer.com/journal/41291/submission-guidelines
- **Field baseline:** international-business-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 open-access articles (2022-2025)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Manuscripts must follow a standard business and management article structure: Introduction (with theory gap framing) → Theory/Conceptual Background → Methods (if empirical) → Results (if empirical) → Discussion/Interpretation → Conclusion. The journal is open to multiple methodologies (quantitative, qualitative, mixed-methods, conceptual) and article types (empirical, literature reviews, editorial/special issue introductions, conceptual frameworks).

### Length
Manuscripts must be **6,000–10,000 words inclusive** of all text (including abstract, references, and appendices).

### Abstract
Abstracts are mandatory and must be **100–150 words**. The abstract should state the research question/topic, the gap or contribution, the approach or methodology, and key findings or arguments. No structured abstract format is mandated (i.e., no separate headings for Background/Methods/Results/Conclusion required), though clear signaling of these elements is expected.

### Citations
Author-date parenthetical style (APA). Reference list in alphabetical order by author surname at manuscript end. For 3–6 authors: name all on first citation, then use first author + "et al." for subsequent citations. For 7+ authors: use first author + "et al." for all citations including first mention. All in-text citations must appear in the reference list; reference list must include only cited works.

### Headings
Hierarchical heading structure is expected but specific formatting conventions are not mandated in public guidelines. Spot-checked articles use numbered or unnumbered hierarchical headings (Introduction → subheadings; Methods subsections; Discussion subsections). Heading style should be clear and logically nested; no requirement for all-caps or specific font weights stated.

### Tables and Figures
Figures and tables should be embedded in the text at appropriate points or clearly referenced. Captions should be descriptive and self-contained. Color is acceptable (note: CMYK color format preferred for print). No explicit maximum number of figures/tables stated in accessible guidelines.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Authors must ensure all third-party material (figures, tables, photos, illustrations) is used with permission and copyright clearance is obtained where required. No stated prohibition on direct quotation, but spot-checked articles demonstrate the field norm of preferring paraphrase.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Citation Density
**RELAXATION**: Citation density in spot-checked articles is noticeably lower than the FIELD_SKILL norm of "multiple citations per paragraph" in theory and discussion sections. ABM articles (particularly empirical and conceptual pieces) show 1–2 citations per paragraph on average in introduction and discussion, with some paragraphs citing a single source or none. This aligns with the ABS 2 tier (lower than the ABS 4/4* baseline).

### Structural Deviations
**PRACTICE ONLY**: Empirical articles follow the standard structure (Intro → Theory → Methods → Results → Discussion → Conclusion), but the Theory section sometimes incorporates hypotheses inline with literature review rather than as a formal, enumerated Hypotheses section. Conceptual and editorial/special-issue-introduction articles frequently omit a traditional Methods section, replacing it with a literature review or theoretical framework section. Literature reviews and editorials are common article types accepted at this journal.

### Voice and Register
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Formal scholarly register with first-person plural ("we argue," "we find") standard for authors' own claims and contributions. Impersonal/passive constructions used for literature synthesis and procedural description. No deviation observed; tone consistent with field baseline.

### Hedging and Epistemic Caution
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Moderate hedging observed in claims about identified patterns and contributions ("results indicate," "we show"), with heavier hedging for forward-looking claims and generalizations ("may," "could," "appears to," "suggests").

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Exceeding the word limit**: 6,000–10,000 words is a strict constraint. Authors often underestimate reference and appendix word counts; count conservatively to avoid desk rejection.
2. **Over-citing**: The lower citation density at this tier (vs. ABS 4/4* baselines) is acceptable. Do not artificially inflate citations to match top-tier norms; dense citation can signal unfamiliarity with core literature and is discouraged.
3. **Incomplete permission documentation for figures/tables**: Third-party materials require explicit copyright clearance and permissions; missing documentation delays acceptance.
4. **Omitting abstract or undershooting word count**: The abstract is mandatory. Ensure it falls within 100–150 words and clearly signals the research question, gap, approach, and contribution.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

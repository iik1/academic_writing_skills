# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of International Business Policy

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** IB&AREA
- **Publisher:** Springer Nature (Palgrave Macmillan), official journal of the Academy of International Business (AIB)
- **Scope:** Publishes research at the intersection of international business and public policy, explicitly requiring relevance for policymakers (governments, IOs) and/or corporate/managerial policy, not just theoretical contribution.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://link.springer.com/journal/42214/submission-guidelines (paywalled/login-gated); style guide summary at https://documents.aib.msu.edu/publications/JIBP/JIBP_Style_Guide_Summary.pdf (accessible)
- **Field baseline:** international-business-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 15+ (title/section-structure survey across Vol. 2 (2019) – Vol. 9 (2026), plus close reads of: "Foreign policy: implications for multinational firms" [Andrews, Fainshmidt, Witt & Gaur, 2025]; "Green gifts from abroad? FDI and firms' green management" [Kannen, Semrau & Steglich, 2026]; "Defining the boundaries of international business policy research" [Van Assche & De Marchi, 2024]; the JIBP founding editorial [Lundan, Van Assche & Cuervo-Cazurra, 2019]; and the editorial "From the editors: Crafting impactful articles that resonate with policy actors" [De Marchi et al., 2025])

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Title page (separate from manuscript) with title, full author names/affiliations/emails, a short running title (max 40 characters), and all acknowledgments — acknowledgments must appear ONLY on the title page, not in the manuscript body. Manuscript itself must be fully anonymized (no author-identifying information anywhere, including acknowledgments). Manuscript begins with an abstract page. Reference list comes at the end, followed by figures and tables (each numbered consecutively with Arabic numerals in one series for tables and one for figures, each beginning on a new page) — i.e., tables/figures are NOT embedded in text at the point of first mention, contrary to common practice elsewhere.

### Length
Regular articles: ~10,000 words maximum (including abstract, text, references, tables, figures, appendices); longer permissible if scale of contribution warrants it. Research notes: ~4,000 words maximum. Commentaries: ~4,000 words. Perspectives: ~10,000 words (full-length, intentionally challenge mainstream views).

### Abstract
100-150 words (narrower than FIELD_SKILL's 150-250), plus 3-6 keywords.

### Citations
Author-date style but NOT full APA: year follows immediately after author name without parentheses in the reference list (e.g., "Cosset, J., & Suret, J. 1995. Political risk..."), rather than APA's "Cosset & Suret (1995)" list format. In-text citation form is not specified in the accessible guide fragment; use the reference-list convention shown for the list itself.

### Headings
Not specified beyond section order noted above; no deviation identified from FIELD_SKILL norms.

### Tables and Figures
Placed after the reference list, not inline; one consecutive Arabic-numeral series each for tables and for figures; each table/figure starts on a new page.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Does not publish manuscripts about teaching materials/methods or manuscripts aimed solely at a practitioner audience. Manuscripts that do not aim to improve knowledge relevant to developing effective international business policies should not be submitted. Poorly written or structured papers are returned to authors without review. Generative AI use in writing, editing, or data generation must be disclosed on submission.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
JIBP runs an unusually high proportion of non-standard article types relative to what FIELD_SKILL's empirical/conceptual/review taxonomy implies: nearly every issue leads with a "From the editors" editorial (framing the issue's theme, often 5-15 pages), and issues regularly include Perspectives, Commentaries (~4,000 words, aimed at a specific contemporary policy debate), and short book reviews (1-5 pages, single-authored, narrative, not academic-article-structured). A submitting author should recognize these as legitimate JIBP formats distinct from the standard empirical/conceptual template, not deviations from it. ENFORCED (stated in article-type definitions and confirmed pervasively across 7 years of issues).

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
JIBP explicitly asks authors to design the abstract and introduction for a dual audience — academic reviewers AND policy practitioners (from IOs, governments) who will "scan these sections for policy insights or recommendations pertinent to their own work" and often read no further. This goes beyond FIELD_SKILL's generic policy-implications expectation: the guidance (per the editorial "Crafting impactful articles that resonate with policy actors") is that policy relevance should be built into the research design and stated in the abstract/introduction itself, not "parachuted in" as an afterthought in the discussion. ENFORCED (stated in a dedicated editorial and consistent with the journal's stated scope/prohibitions).

## Common Pitfalls
- Placing tables/figures inline in the manuscript rather than after the reference list, or leaving acknowledgments in the manuscript body instead of the anonymized title page — both cause desk rejection risk per the style guide.
- Writing a standard theory-gap-contribution abstract without any policy-actor-facing content; JIBP explicitly wants policy relevance visible in the abstract/introduction, not deferred to the discussion.
- Submitting a paper whose contribution is purely theoretical or managerial with no stated implications for public policy actors — this falls outside JIBP's explicit scope and risks summary rejection.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

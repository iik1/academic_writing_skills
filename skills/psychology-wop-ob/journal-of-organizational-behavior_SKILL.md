# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Organizational Behavior

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 4
- **Field:** PSYCH (WOP-OB)
- **Publisher:** Wiley-Blackwell
- **Scope:** Publishes empirical reports and theoretical reviews of research in
  organizational behavior at the individual, group, and organizational levels.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/page/journal/10991379/homepage/forauthors.html
  (returned HTTP 402/paywalled on direct fetch; guideline facts below were
  triangulated from search-engine-cached summaries of that page and related
  Wiley "For Authors" hub content).
- **Field baseline:** psychology-wop-ob-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 2 full-text (open access) + ~13 additional
  titles/abstracts/DOIs surfaced via search (Knoll et al. 2025, job.2867;
  Junker, Bakker, Derks & Pletzer 2025, job.2860; Wang et al. 2025 job.2887;
  Kamarova et al. 2025 job.2832; Huang, Bowling & Wu 2025 job.70001; Han et al.

  et al. 2025 job.2873; plus issue/TOC-level metadata for Vol 44-46 Issue 2
  "Job Annual Review" special issues). Full text was accessible only for the
  two open-access articles; the remainder were judged from abstracts, DOIs,

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No single mandated section template beyond standard IMRaD; observed articles
consistently use a distinct "Theory and Hypotheses Development" (or
"Theoretical Background") heading with numbered, boldface Hypothesis
statements (e.g., "Hypothesis 1a.", "Hypothesis 2."), matching FIELD_SKILL's
noted point of varying practice — JOB's own practice leans toward the
distinct-heading variant. Multi-study manuscripts are common but not
mandated; single-study field designs with strong data (e.g., multi-wave,
multi-source) are also published routinely.

### Length
No explicit word/page limit was recoverable from accessible guideline
content (page behind paywall). Observed articles run long-form (typically
15-20+ journal pages including references), consistent with FIELD_SKILL.

### Abstract
**Unstructured** abstract, **maximum 200 words**, containing major
keywords, no section headers. Followed consistently in both full-text
articles sampled (single flowing paragraph, no bolded sub-labels).

### Citations
APA 7th edition (Publication Manual of the APA, 7th ed.), matching
FIELD_SKILL defaults — no journal-specific deviation observed.

### Headings
Standard APA heading levels; numbered section headings (e.g., "1 |
Introduction", "2 | Theory and Hypotheses Development", "4 | Study 1", "4.1
| Methods") are used in practice — a numbering convention not universal in
the field baseline. Sub-subsections (e.g., "4.1.1 | Participants and Study
Design") are used liberally to organize Method sections.

### Tables and Figures
Standard APA-style tables and figures; conceptual/path models presented as
a numbered Figure early in the Introduction or Theory section (following
FIELD_SKILL norm). No journal-specific figure/table caps observed.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Double-blind review: main text, supplementary materials, and any linked
  resources (e.g., OSF folders) must not contain author-identifying
  information; anonymized OSF links (e.g., "?view_only=...") are used for
  this reason in practice.
- Up to five keywords; keywords must reflect major concepts of the paper.
- AI-generated content tools cannot be listed as authors; any use must be
  disclosed transparently in Methods or Acknowledgements.
- iThenticate/CrossCheck similarity screening is used; excessive text
  overlap with prior work will be flagged.
- Data sharing is expected; authors select a Wiley-compliant data
  availability statement (a Data Availability Statement section is present
  in both sampled articles, even when data access is restricted to
  "available upon request").

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
JOB publishes a distinct annual "Job Annual Review and Conceptual
Development Issue" (an entire dedicated issue, e.g., Vol 44 No 2, Vol 46 No
2) featuring longer theoretical/review-style papers, and also accepts
Point/Counterpoint-format pieces — article types beyond the standard
empirical report that FIELD_SKILL does not address. When writing a regular
empirical submission, this does not change required structure, but authors
targeting a review contribution should recognize this as a
distinct-and-invited manuscript category rather than a standard submission
route. PRACTICE ONLY / journal-specific format, not documented in general
guideline text located.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Both sampled full-text articles include extensive numbered footnote-style
endnotes reporting supplementary/robustness analyses requested by reviewers
(e.g., alternative control variables, disaggregated DV analyses, MANOVA
checks on demographic covariates) rather than folding these into an online
supplement or appendix. This is a heavier and more visible use of endnotes
for robustness reporting than FIELD_SKILL's general note that robustness
is "reported either in the main text or in an online supplement." PRACTICE
ONLY, observed in both sampled articles — treat as a plausible norm but
verify against a larger sample before treating as universal.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- Voice and person: FIELD DEFAULT
- Statistical reporting: FIELD DEFAULT (exact test statistics, effect sizes,
  CIs consistently reported; non-significant hypotheses reported plainly)
- Citation density: FIELD DEFAULT
- Hedging and epistemic caution: FIELD DEFAULT (causal language explicitly
  hedged in Discussion/Limitations in both sampled articles)
- Discussion/Conclusion structure (Theoretical Implications, Practical
  Implications, Limitations subsections): FIELD DEFAULT
- Methods reporting detail (measures, reliability, analytic strategy
  subsection): FIELD DEFAULT

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting a structured or over-length abstract: JOB requires a single
  unstructured paragraph capped at 200 words — field-typical structured
  abstracts (Purpose/Method/Findings/Implications headers) are not the
  house style here.
- Failing to anonymize supplementary material links (e.g., OSF) for
  double-blind review; both sampled articles use view-only anonymized links
  rather than public ones during review, implying this is actively
  enforced.
- Treating the "Job Annual Review and Conceptual Development Issue" as a
  regular submission track — it appears to function as a distinct,
  likely invited/curated review format rather than a venue for standard
  empirical submissions.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

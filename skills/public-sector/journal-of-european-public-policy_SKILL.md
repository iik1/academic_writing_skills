# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of European Public Policy

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** PUB SEC
- **Publisher:** Taylor & Francis (Routledge)
- **Scope:** Flagship journal on public policy dynamics in Europe and the EU: theoretical/methodological approaches to public policy, national policy processes and comparisons within Europe, the nation-state/EU interface, EU institutions and political processes, and the EU as a global/regional actor. Defines "European public policy" broadly and accepts qualitative and quantitative work.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No mandatory numbered-hypothesis or fixed-section template is specified in the guidelines beyond standard research-article convention. Two distinctive article types exist beyond standard research articles: a **Research Agenda Section** (reviews state of the art in a field and proposes an innovative, broad-appeal research agenda; normal peer review) and a **Debate Section**. These are journal-specific formats not covered by FIELD_SKILL.

### Length
Standard research articles: maximum **8,000 words** (total, including notes/references — tables and figures also count against this limit, so layout choices trade off against prose length). Research Agenda articles: word limit extended to **8,000 words** (previously 5,000), same as standard articles.

### Abstract
Maximum **150 words**, unstructured (no mandatory labeled fields).

### Citations
Author-date (parenthetical) system: "(McClung 2009)"; multiple works "(McClung 2009; Vaquero and Cebrian 2013)"; three-plus authors use "et al." Reference list: journal articles as Author(s) (Year) 'Title', *Journal* Volume(Issue): pages; books as Author(s) (Year) *Title*, Place: Publisher. Same-author/same-year works distinguished with a/b/c suffixes. Only initials used for first names in the reference list. This matches FIELD_SKILL's default author-date convention — no delta.

### Headings
Not specified beyond standard practice; no journal-specific heading taxonomy found.

### Tables and Figures
Explicit guidance that tables and diagrams "cost" words against the 8,000-word limit — authors are directed to plan table/figure layout with this tradeoff in mind. This is a journal-specific budgeting constraint not present in FIELD_SKILL.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Manuscript must be submitted only to JEPP (not under review elsewhere); must contain nothing abusive, defamatory, libellous, obscene, fraudulent, or illegal. Originality screened via CrossCheck (iThenticate). Journal follows the DA-RT (Data Access and Research Transparency) Joint Statement: replication data/materials (e.g., .do files) must be deposited (Figshare is the journal's suggested repository) or a justified exemption statement provided.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm at the abstract level. Abstracts observed open directly on the empirical/theoretical puzzle (e.g., contested EU Taxonomy politics, asymmetry between negative/positive integration, crisis-driven attention shifts) rather than heavy scene-setting, consistent with FIELD_SKILL's convention of opening on a real-world governance problem.

### Statistical Reporting
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Sample too abstract-level to detect deltas in coefficient/robustness reporting; both quantitative (conjoint experiments, behavioral measures, panel data) and qualitative/process-tracing (delegated-act politics, Policy Delphi) designs appear side by side, consistent with FIELD_SKILL's "no single methodological orthodoxy" observation.

### Citation Density
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.

### Structural Deviations
The **Research Agenda Section** and **Debate Section** are journal-specific article types with distinct aims (synthesis + agenda-setting rather than original empirical contribution) not addressed by FIELD_SKILL, which only describes standard empirical-article structure. Treat these as separate genres when drafting for JEPP: a Research Agenda piece should foreground "state of the art" synthesis and an explicit forward-looking agenda rather than hypothesis-testing.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Strong and consistent thematic anchoring in EU-institutional process (Commission strategy, delegated acts, Council/Parliament dynamics, Member State bargaining) even in comparative or citizen-behavior pieces — reflects the journal's EU-institutions core rather than a stylistic delta per se. RELAXATION (ABS 3, consistent with FIELD_SKILL allowance for relaxation without redefining baseline): word limit of 8,000 is on the lower end of what FIELD_SKILL's typical ABS 4*/4 outlets might allow (no explicit FIELD_SKILL limit given, but this is worth flagging as a firm ceiling authors must budget for, including tables/figures).

## Common Pitfalls
- Exceeding the 8,000-word ceiling by under-budgeting for tables/figures, which count against the limit — plan visual elements early rather than adding them after drafting to length.
- Writing a Research Agenda submission as if it were a standard empirical research article (with hypotheses and results) rather than as state-of-the-art synthesis plus a forward-looking agenda — these are reviewed as a distinct genre.
- Omitting the DA-RT-compliant data/replication statement or a justified exemption, which the journal requires at submission/acceptance.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Economics of Education Review

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Elsevier
- **Scope:** Peer-reviewed journal publishing empirical, theoretical, and methodological contributions to education economics, with emphasis on applied studies using micro data and clear identification strategies.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/economics-of-education-review/publish/guide-for-authors (paywalled; information extracted from public search results and working paper sources)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 5 (Teacher value-added and gender; Groundwater contamination; Four-day school week; Student employment meta-analysis; Financial aid and work)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Manuscripts should be divided into **numbered sections (1., 2., 3…)** and **subsections (1.1.1, 1.1.2,...)**. Short Communications require the same numbering but do not require a detailed background section or comprehensive literature review.

### Length
- **Regular articles:** approximately 8,000 words
- **Short Communications:** at most 6,000 words
- **Online appendices:** permitted up to 20 pages (must be self-contained without appendix)

### Abstract
- **Format:** Unstructured, factual prose (not structured sections)
- **Word limit:** 250 words maximum
- **Requirement:** Must stand alone and be self-contained; avoid references if possible; if essential, cite author(s) and year(s)
- **Abbreviations:** Avoid non-standard or uncommon abbreviations; define on first mention

### Keywords
- **Required:** 1 to 7 keywords for indexing
- **Language:** English only
- **Format:** Avoid multi-word keywords using "and" or "of"; minimize abbreviations unless well-established in field

### Citations
Follows standard economics author-year format (parenthetical or in-text): (Cole, 2000); (Cole, 2000; de Brito & Scorrano, 2008); (Neumann et al., 2010). Reference list format: Author(s). (Year). Article title. *Journal Name*, Volume(Issue), page range.

### Headings
Numbered sections required. Subsection numbering follows hierarchical pattern (1.1, 1.1.1, etc.). No standard naming convention specified for conclusion section.

### Tables and Figures
- **Short Communications:** at most 5 exhibits (tables or figures)
- **Regular articles:** no strict limit stated in guidelines
- **Appendices:** visually complex material may be relegated to appendix

### Explicit Prohibitions
- **References in abstract:** Generally avoided unless essential
- **Abbreviations in abstract:** Non-standard abbreviations should not appear except at first mention with definition
- **Short Communications:** Must not contain detailed background sections or comprehensive literature reviews

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Abstract Format
The FIELD_SKILL baseline does not specify abstract structure. Economics of Education Review requires an **unstructured, factual abstract** with a strict **250-word limit**. This is tighter than many field-wide conventions and explicit about format. ENFORCED.

### JEL Classification
Articles consistently include JEL classification codes (e.g., I15, I25, F63) but guidelines do not explicitly mandate them. This appears to be field-standard practice rather than journal-specific. PRACTICE ONLY.

### Short Communication Article Type
Economics of Education Review explicitly offers a Short Communication option (max 6,000 words, max 5 exhibits) that does not require detailed background or literature review. This is a **relaxation** of top-tier expectations (ABS 4* journals rarely offer such condensed formats). ENFORCED RELAXATION.

### Section Numbering Convention
Guidelines explicitly specify numbered sections (1., 2., 3…) and subsections (1.1.1, 1.1.2,…). FIELD_SKILL mentions Roman or Arabic numerals are acceptable but does not mandate numbering format. This journal appears stricter in requiring the format. ENFORCED.

### Expedited Review Option
Journal offers an expedited review process where manuscripts are either accepted or rejected after one round with minimal revision requests. This is not typical of ABS 4*/4 journals. PRACTICE ONLY (optional author choice).

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Exceeding the abstract limit.** The 250-word hard cap is strictly enforced. Many empirical papers inadvertently run 300+ words; trim ruthlessly.

2. **Omitting abstract stand-alone requirement.** Some authors fail to make the abstract independently comprehensible to readers who may see only the abstract in search results. Avoid all unexplained references to tables, equations, or sections not described in the abstract.

3. **Failing to use numbered section format.** The guidelines explicitly require numbered sections (1., 2., 3…). Papers using non-numeric headings or Roman numerals may face desk rejects or revisions.

4. **Including background/literature review in Short Communications.** The Short Communication format explicitly relaxes these requirements. If opting for Short Communication, ensure no dedicated background section; integrate any necessary context into introduction or methods.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

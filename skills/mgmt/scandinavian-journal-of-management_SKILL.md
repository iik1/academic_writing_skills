# Journal Writing Skill: Scandinavian Journal of Management

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN
- **Publisher:** Elsevier
- **Scope:** International forum for innovative and carefully crafted research on management in any type of organization (small, large, private, public, non-profit, for-profit, entrepreneurial, craft or arts-based, community-based). Welcomes non-mainstream research approaches in methodology, epistemology, and ontology.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/scandinavian-journal-of-management/publish/guide-for-authors (behind paywall; accessible features documented below)
- **Field baseline:** mgmt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 (IDEAS/RePEc recent listings: "A paradox rarely comes alone" (Henriksen et al.), "Ending business-non-profit partnerships" (Murthy et al.), "Unfolding the airbus' strategic growth" (Kazeminia), "CEO succession with gender change" (Rigolini et al.), "Entrepreneurship as a family resemblance concept" (Leunbach), "The embedded-agency paradox revisited" (Colombero et al.) - all from Vol 37 Issue 1, 2021)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
The journal publishes three article types:
1. **Full-length articles** (standard empirical, theoretical, or methodological contributions)
2. **Focused discussion-type texts** (3,000–5,000 words; empirically or theoretically oriented)
3. **Book reviews** (occasional)

Standard article body structure follows Introduction → Methods (or Research Setting/Theoretical Background) → Results/Findings → Discussion → Conclusion. A clear introduction section must allow non-specialists to understand the publication and background of the issues involved.

### Length
- **Focused discussion-type texts:** explicitly 3,000–5,000 words (accepted article type).
- **Full-length articles:** no specific word maximum stated in accessible guidelines; submission limits not disclosed.
- No specified minimum or maximum page limits.

### Abstract
- **Format:** Unstructured (no labeled sections required)
- **Length:** Not exceeding 250 words
- **Content:** Briefly state purpose of research, principal results, and major conclusions
- **Avoid:** References in the abstract; if essential, cite author(s) and year(s) only; avoid non-standard abbreviations; define essential abbreviations at first mention
- **Standalone requirement:** Abstract must be able to stand alone when presented separately from the article

### Citation Style
- **Format:** APA author-year in-text citations (Author, Year) or Author (Year) when integrated into sentence
- **Multiple citations:** Ordered alphabetically or chronologically by convention
- **Et al. protocol:** Used for three or more authors from first citation

### Keywords
- **Count required:** 1 to 7 keywords for indexing
- **Language:** English
- **Format:** Avoid multi-word keywords using "and" or "of"; avoid non-standard abbreviations unless firmly established in the field

### Headings
- **Maximum three levels** of headings permitted
- **Differentiation:** Must be clearly identifiable using different font sizes, bold, or italics

### Tables and Figures
- Guidelines on formatting not accessible in available sources; follow standard management journal practice per FIELD_SKILL

### Ethical Compliance
- Authors must follow **Elsevier's Publishing Ethics Policy**
- Required confirmations: work not previously published (except preprint form), not under consideration elsewhere, approved by all authors and responsible authorities, will not be published elsewhere in same form without written consent
- **Human subjects research:** Ethics committee approval and informed consent must be documented; patient/research subject identities must never appear (even anonymized), including names, initials, hospital/social security numbers, dates of birth, or other identifying information
- **Competing interests, data availability statements, and acknowledgments** appear at article end (standard Elsevier format)

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Reflexivity and Researcher Positioning (ENFORCED)
Journal explicitly asks authors to address and reflect on:
- The role of the researcher/s in researching and theorizing
- The positioning of the researcher/s
- The impact of the researcher/s on the research process

This is a **stronger and more explicit requirement** than the standard management field baseline, which expects methodological rigor but does not systematically call for reflexive author positioning within the research argument itself. In SJM, reflexivity is framed as an editorial principle ("Reasoned Reflexivity"), suggesting it should be visible in the manuscript as a dimension of how findings are interpreted and presented.

**Implication:** Qualitative and theoretical papers especially should explicitly address researcher perspective, positionality, or reflexive insights in the Methods or Theoretical Background section, not merely describe analytic procedures.

### Contextual Awareness and Decentering of Management (ENFORCED)
Journal emphasizes that:
- Management is embedded in society and nature, not separate
- Management ideologies and practices must be understood as entwined with social and natural contexts
- Authors should decenter management as the sole analytical focus and foreground greater contextual sensitivity
- Knowledge contributions should address how management can be impactful for society and for nature

This is a **substantive delta** from FIELD_SKILL, which focuses management scholarship on organizational theory and practice but does not systematically emphasize societal and environmental embeddedness as an analytical requirement. SJM expects authors to situate organizational phenomena within broader social and ecological contexts.

**Implication:** Discussion sections should explicitly address broader societal or environmental implications, not merely managerial relevance. Scope conditions and limitations should address contextual boundaries (e.g., "this finding applies to organizations embedded in [Nordic/stable-democratic/resource-constrained] contexts").

### Emphasis on Challenging Existing Knowledge (PRACTICE ONLY)
The journal explicitly invites research driven by curiosity and inquiries that are "challenging" to existing knowledge. The editorial stance encourages authors to avoid confirmatory tests of well-established predictions (aligning with FIELD_SKILL norm) but goes further in explicitly welcoming research that contradicts, complicates, or fundamentally questions management orthodoxy.

**Implication:** Theoretical contributions and literature engagements should be framed as challenging, extending, or complicating prior work, not merely testing or confirming it. Introductions should clearly frame the niche as a gap, contradiction, or underexplored tension that the paper will challenge or complicate.

### Innovation in Methodology, Epistemology, and Ontology (ENFORCED)
Journal welcomes **non-mainstream research approaches** in methodology, epistemology, and ontology. This includes research that departs from conventional quantitative or qualitative templates, experimental designs, or epistemological foundations (e.g., constructivist, realist, post-modern framings).

This is slightly more explicit in SJM than in typical management outlets, which tolerate but do not systematically invite non-mainstream epistemologies.

**Implication:** Methodological sections in non-conventional studies should clearly articulate the epistemological and ontological foundations and justify why the chosen approach is appropriate for the research question. Do not apologize for unconventional approaches; frame them as integral to the inquiry.

### Article Type: Focused Discussion Texts (ENFORCED)
A unique feature: the journal explicitly accepts **focused discussion-type texts of 3,000–5,000 words** that are empirically or theoretically oriented. This is not a standard category in most top-tier management outlets and offers an alternative to full-length articles for authors with focused theoretical arguments or conceptual critiques.

**Implication:** Short, sharply argued theoretical or critical pieces that would be rejected as too brief elsewhere may be suitable for SJM if framed as "discussion-type" contributions.

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **First-person plural voice ("we"):** FIELD DEFAULT (standard in co-authored empirical and theoretical work; no journal-specific variation observed)
- **Passive voice and active argumentation balance:** FIELD DEFAULT (active voice dominates argument and interpretation; passive used for methodology description; follows standard management norm)
- **Hedging and epistemic stance:** FIELD DEFAULT (moderate hedging with modal verbs marks inferences; "we find," "we argue," "suggest" follow standard conventions)
- **Introduction (CARS structure):** FIELD DEFAULT (establish territory → niche → occupy niche model observed in accessible article titles and editorials)
- **Theory section development:** FIELD DEFAULT (logic building toward hypotheses or propositions; hypothesis/proposition labeling follows field norm)
- **Results/Findings and Discussion separation:** FIELD DEFAULT (Results/Findings present evidence; Discussion interprets and situates findings)
- **Citation density and style:** FIELD DEFAULT (Introduction and Theory citation-dense; Results/Findings sparse; Discussion engages citations; APA style standard)
- **Reference list length:** FIELD DEFAULT (typical 40–80 references for empirical papers; reviews longer; aligns with FIELD_SKILL)
- **Contribution statements:** FIELD DEFAULT (explicit, numbered contributions in Introduction; two to four contributions is norm)
- **Limitations discussion:** FIELD DEFAULT (openness about scope conditions, measurement, generalizability; no journal-specific variation)
- **Future research framing:** FIELD DEFAULT (forward-looking recommendations in Discussion closing; standard management practice)

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Treating reflexivity as optional metadata.** SJM expects reflexive positioning to be integrated into the research narrative, especially in qualitative and theoretical work. Relegating it to a methods footnote or omitting it entirely will weaken a manuscript. Authors should explicitly address "how did the researcher's perspective, identity, or position influence this inquiry?" in the appropriate section.

2. **Neglecting contextual/societal embeddedness.** Framing management phenomena as isolated from social and environmental contexts misses a core editorial value. A manuscript on organizational innovation or strategy must situate findings or arguments within broader societal, institutional, or ecological contexts. Discussion should address: "What does this reveal about how management is embedded in society/nature?"

3. **Confirming rather than challenging.** Submitting a study that tests a well-established prediction without extending or complicating it may be rejected on grounds of insufficient novelty. SJM seeks research that challenges, contradicts, or fundamentally reframes existing knowledge. Frame your contribution as a challenge or complication, not a confirmation.

4. **Overlooking the 3,000–5,000 word discussion-type option.** A tightly argued theoretical critique or conceptual piece may fit better as a focused discussion text than as a full article. If your contribution is primarily conceptual and relatively concise, consider this format explicitly when preparing the submission.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

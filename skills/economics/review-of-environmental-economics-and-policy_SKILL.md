# Journal Writing Skill: Review of Environmental Economics and Policy

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** University of Chicago Press (from 2021 onward); formerly Oxford University Press (2008–2020)
- **Scope:** Policy-oriented synthesis of environmental economics research and timely policy discussions for a wide policy audience. REEP explicitly does NOT publish original empirical work or novel theoretical contributions; instead, it synthesizes lessons learned from recent research.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://academic.oup.com/reep/pages/Instructions_To_Authors
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 8 (full abstracts and front-matter from 2025 and 2024 issues)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Submission Process
ENFORCED (stated guideline, confirmed across journal info pages)
- Proposals only, not full manuscripts initially. Submitting a complete manuscript violates submission protocol.
- Proposals must include: author names, institutional affiliations, author CVs, proposed article title, two to three-page outline with references.
- Full manuscripts submitted only after proposal acceptance and invitation from editors.

### Structure
ENFORCED (not fully specified in guidelines, but strongly indicated)
- Articles must be "broadly accessible" (stated in guidelines).
- The journal does not specify a mandatory section order; structure varies by synthesis topic.
- No standard empirical structure (Introduction → Data → Methods → Results → Conclusion) is enforced because the journal does NOT publish original empirical or theoretical research.
- Instead, articles synthesize existing evidence and policy implications.

### Length
ENFORCED (explicitly stated)
- Maximum 10,000 words including footnotes, tables, figures, and references.
- This is substantially shorter than ABS 4*/4 papers in FIELD_SKILL, which may run 15,000–25,000 words.
- Features and policy briefs are shorter: regular features, 4,000 words max; policy briefs, 2,000 words max.

### Abstract
GUIDELINE ONLY (stated in general journal guidelines, not with explicit format)
- Abstract format not specified in accessible guidelines.
- Observed practice (from article abstracts on IDEAS/RePEc): unstructured, 100–150 words typical, paragraph format.
- No required keywords section identified.

### Citations
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. (Author-year, parenthetical in-text style is standard in REEP articles observed; no evidence of deviation.)

### Headings
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. (REEP articles observed use numbered headings and standard section naming; no unique convention identified.)

### Tables and Figures
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. (General guidance provided by publisher on digital art preparation, but no journal-specific deviation from economics field norms.)

### Mathematics and Equations
RELAXATION (stated guideline strongly narrows field norm)
- "Broadly accessible" articles should contain "no equations" and "keep mathematics to a minimum."
- This is a relaxation from FIELD_SKILL, which expects theoretical and empirical papers to use equations, mathematical notation, and formal proofs freely.
- REEP enforces accessibility over technical rigor. If mathematics is unavoidable, it should be rare and clearly explained.

### Explicit Prohibitions
ENFORCED (stated in guidelines)
- AI/LLM composition and ideation prohibited: "Listed authors must maintain creative control over all of the article's expression and ensure AI is used only in a supporting role. REEP does not currently accept articles or other contributions that have been composed using generative AI or LLMs or used them to generate ideas, supporting arguments, and perspectives."
- Original empirical work and novel theoretical results are not accepted.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Fundamental Paper Type Delta
ENFORCED (this is a core mission statement of the journal)
- FIELD_SKILL baseline is built on ABS 4*/4 empirical and theory papers. REEP does NOT publish empirical or theory papers in the traditional sense.
- Instead, REEP publishes **synthesis, review, and policy discussion** articles.
- Implication: The canonical structure in FIELD_SKILL (Introduction → Data → Empirical Strategy → Results → Conclusion for empirical papers; Introduction → Related Literature → Model → Analysis → Results → Conclusion for theory papers) does NOT apply.
- REEP articles synthesize existing research for a policy audience. They may follow a structure like: Introduction/Motivation → Current state of research on topic X → Policy implications → Discussion of research gaps. Or: Problem statement → Evidence review → Policy options → Synthesis.
- Reviewed articles (2025, 2024 REEP) confirm this: titles like "Economics of Nature-Based Solutions for Mitigating Climate Change," "Climate Change Mitigation Policies for Developing Countries," "Inflation Reduction Act: Origins, Policy Implications, and Research Gaps" are synthesis/review pieces, not empirical or novel theory.

### Voice and Register
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm with one caveat. First-person plural ("we") is standard in REEP articles (e.g., abstracts use "we synthesize," "we review"). Active voice is preferred. However, because REEP is a **review/synthesis journal** rather than an empirical or theory journal, the author voice is typically more distant and editorial—reflecting a survey of literature rather than presentation of new findings. "We synthesize" is common; "we find" is less common because the authors are reporting others' findings, not their own.

### Citation Density
ENFORCED RELAXATION (high density in introduction/background, lower elsewhere)
- Introduction and literature synthesis sections: very high citation density (FIELD_SKILL norm).
- Policy implications and discussion sections: moderate to low citation density (may be lower than FIELD_SKILL norm for results/conclusion, as the authors are synthesizing and drawing implications rather than reporting new empirical results).
- This pattern is natural to synthesis articles, not a deviation per se, but it is not typical of the empirical papers in FIELD_SKILL.

### Mathematical and Quantitative Presentation
RELAXATION (observed practice aligns with stated guideline)
- Articles contain no equations or minimal mathematical notation.
- Quantitative claims use words and percentage statements rather than notation.
- Example from abstract: "Following the Paris Agreement, many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) have adopted climate change targets" rather than "Let C ⊆ LMIC denote the set of countries that have adopted targets..."
- This is a significant relaxation from FIELD_SKILL, which freely uses equations, formal models, and notation.

### Word and Sentence Economy
PRACTICE ONLY (observed but not explicitly mandated)
- The 10,000-word limit encourages concision. Sentences are medium-length (FIELD_SKILL norm) but may be slightly shorter on average in REEP due to space constraints.
- Paragraphs are still dense (4+ sentences), but dense technical content is rare, so paragraphs may be slightly shorter than in FIELD_SKILL papers on average.

### Results Presentation and Statistical Reporting
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm where empirical results are discussed. However, REEP is not primarily an empirical journal, so this section applies only when a synthesis article discusses findings from other studies. When reporting others' results, REEP follows FIELD_SKILL practice: quantitative specificity ("the policy increased employment by approximately 4 percentage points") is expected.

### Hedging and Epistemic Caution
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Clear claims are preferred. However, because REEP synthesizes literature, hedging is common when acknowledging areas of disagreement in the literature or methodological limitations of reviewed studies. This is not a deviation but an artifact of the synthesis mission.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Submitting a full manuscript instead of a proposal.** REEP requires a proposal (abstract + 2–3 page outline) before inviting full-manuscript submission. Sending a complete draft will be rejected out of hand.
2. **Writing as if original empirical or theoretical contribution is the goal.** REEP does not publish novel empirical work or original theoretical results. If your article presents new data analysis or new theorems, it does not belong in REEP; submit to an ABS 3+ journal. Frame your work as a synthesis of current knowledge and policy implications.
3. **Including equations or heavy mathematical notation.** REEP emphasizes accessibility. If you cannot explain your synthesis without equations, REEP is not the right outlet. Save equation-heavy work for a theory journal.
4. **Using generative AI to compose or ideate.** The journal explicitly prohibits AI composition and AI-generated ideas, arguments, or perspectives. Ensure authorial control and human intellectual ownership of the argument.
5. **Ignoring the policy audience.** REEP targets "a wide policy audience," not exclusively academic economists. Avoid jargon where possible; explain acronyms and concepts. A policy maker should understand the synthesis, not just specialists in the subfield.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

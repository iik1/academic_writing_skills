# Journal Writing Skill: Oxford Development Studies

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Routledge (Taylor & Francis)
- **Scope:** A peer-reviewed quarterly forum for rigorous and critical analysis of processes of social, political, and economic change characterizing development. Publishes empirical analyses (quantitative, qualitative, mixed methods) and theoretical contributions grounded in single or multiple world regions, with welcome for interdisciplinary work.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.tandfonline.com/journals/cods20 (author guidelines behind paywall; see Sources)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 (titles from 2025–2026: "Long-term impacts of mobile money…", "From field to family: agricultural mechanization…", "ROSCAs and household welfare…", "Towards improved menstrual health…", "Debt attitudes and debt accumulation…", "Development Humanities" essay)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

### Structure
Not accessible (paywall). Based on article sample and scope, the journal accepts both empirical and theoretical papers. Empirical papers follow near-standard structure (Introduction, Data/Methods, Results, Conclusion); theoretical and mixed-method papers show variation. All articles appear to include abstract.

### Length
Not specified in accessible guidelines. Observed range in recent articles suggests 8,000–12,000 words typical for full papers; shorter format papers also published.

### Abstract
Length limit not stated in accessible sources. Development economics norms (per CGD guidance on cognate journals) suggest 150–250 words typical. Observed abstracts emphasize research question, methodology, and results with some policy or theoretical implications.

### Citations
Author-date (parenthetical citations) is standard, consistent with FIELD_SKILL. No field-specific deviation observed.

### Headings
Numbered headings (Arabic numerals) observed in sample articles. Heading norms appear consistent with FIELD_SKILL baseline.

### Tables and Figures
Both used commonly in empirical work. Descriptive statistics tables appear standard; figures used for illustrating variation and outcomes. Norms consistent with FIELD_SKILL.

### Explicit Prohibitions
None stated in accessible guidelines. Journal actively encourages submissions from Global South researchers and traditionally marginalized perspectives in development studies.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Scope and Audience
RELAXATION: Broader disciplinary base than top-tier economics journals. Articles explicitly welcome from politics, anthropology, sociology, geography, history, and other social sciences. This differs from FIELD_SKILL (written for economists). Implications: technical economics vocabulary should not be assumed; methods from other disciplines are legitimized.

### Methodological Pluralism
RELAXATION: Qualitative and mixed-methods work are actively welcome and published alongside quantitative research. FIELD_SKILL baseline emphasizes quantitative empirical work and formal theory. ODS actively solicits qualitative case studies, process evaluations, and ethnographic work. This is a material softening of the pure empirical-quantitative emphasis in top-tier economics.

### Voice and Person
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (first-person plural "we" standard even in single-authored papers). No observed delta.

### Hedging and Caution
RELAXATION: Observed hedging intensity appears slightly higher than FIELD_SKILL, particularly in qualitative/mixed-method work, where claims are more frequently qualified with "suggests," "indicates," or "appears." This may reflect greater comfort with exploratory findings and methodological diversity. No direct evidence from guidelines; observed in article abstracts and titles.

### Regional and Development Focus
DOMAIN-SPECIFIC: Articles are grounded in specific regions or comparative studies; "universal" theoretical results without regional instantiation are less common than in top-tier economics. This is not a violation of FIELD_SKILL but a emphasis: development studies privileges geographical and contextual specificity.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Over-assuming economics audience.** ODS publishes for social scientists across disciplines. Avoid technical jargon (e.g., "local average treatment effect," "partial equilibrium") without brief explanation or paraphrase for non-economists.

2. **Dismissing non-quantitative evidence as preliminary.** Qualitative findings and mixed-methods designs are treated as full empirical contributions, not supplements. If using qualitative data, frame it as equally rigorous, not as supplementary validation.

3. **Focusing results section purely on statistical significance.** Development contexts are often data-sparse and heterogeneous. Practical significance, effect heterogeneity by region/group, and unexpected null results are valued.

4. **Assuming top-tier methodological sophistication.** ABS 2 journals accept robust quasi-experimental designs and difference-in-differences work but do not require cutting-edge identification strategies. Standard, well-justified methods are publishable.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

## Historical Context
Oxford Development Studies was established in 1933 as *Farm Economist*, renamed *Oxford Agrarian Studies* in 1972, and adopted its current title in 1996. The journal administers two annual prizes: a £500 award for the best article and a £1,000 biennial prize for the best student-authored article (Sanjaya Lall Prizes). Current editor-in-chief: Cheryl R. Doss (University of Oxford). This evolution reflects a broadening of scope from agricultural economics to development studies more widely.

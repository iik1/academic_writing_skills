# Journal Writing Skill: Feminist Review

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN (note: journal is actually feminist studies/critical humanities, not management; ABS classification appears misaligned)
- **Publisher:** SAGE Publications
- **Scope:** Transnational, interdisciplinary feminist scholarship with emphasis on Black, Indigenous, decolonial, and activist perspectives. Publishes academic articles, experimental pieces, visual media, interviews, and poetry. Explicitly committed to intersectional analysis of gender, race, class, sexuality, ableism, ethnicity, capitalism.
- **Frequency:** 3 issues per year
- **Guidelines URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/author-instructions/fer
- **Field baseline:** mgmt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 1 full editorial + journal scope/guidelines analysis (paywall limited access to full articles; sampling documented in Sources)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

### Structure
**No prescribed template.** Authors "do not need to follow a template, but please ensure your heading levels are clear, and the sections clearly defined." This is a CRITICAL ENFORCED delta from the mgmt baseline (which prescribes standard structures by article type: quantitative/qualitative/theoretical/review templates). Feminist Review explicitly rejects structural templates. Sections should be organized around the substantive argument, not generic labels (Introduction/Methods/Results/Discussion).

### Length
**6,000 to 8,000 words total**, inclusive of main text, abstract, citations, footnotes, and references. This is ENFORCED and substantially tighter than typical mgmt journals (which run 10,000–15,000+ words). Open Space submissions: up to 3,000 words. Word count is non-negotiable; all components count against the limit.

### Abstract
**200–300 words, unstructured.** Placed between title and main body. No structured subsections (Background/Methods/Results/Implications). A single prose paragraph or flowing narrative acceptable. No citations in abstract. **ENFORCED DELTA:** mgmt baseline notes structured or unstructured are both used; Feminist Review explicitly specifies unstructured.

### Keywords
**Minimum 6 keywords, maximum as needed (no stated limit).** Keywords must be specific to the research topic and suitable for indexing/abstracting services. **Note:** Mgmt baseline does not mention keywords; this is a JOURNAL-SPECIFIC requirement.

### Citations
**SAGE Harvard reference style.** In-text: (Author, Year) or Author (Year). Multiple citations: alphabetical or chronological order. Et al. for three+ authors from first citation. **Field-level consistency:** matches mgmt baseline (APA-style citations are standard across management); SAGE Harvard is the specific variant here.

### Headings
Clear heading levels required; authors define their own hierarchies based on argument structure, not template. Headings should reflect substantive content, not generic labels. (E.g., "Colonial Legacies in Contemporary Feminist Practice" rather than "Theoretical Background.")

### Tables and Figures
No explicit guidelines provided in author instructions. Standard academic practice assumed.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Author anonymity must be maintained: "Any information that compromises the anonymity of the author(s) should be removed or anonymized and included on the Title Page instead."
- Manuscripts must be anonymized before submission.
- All authors must consent to submission in current form.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Article Structure and Templates
**ENFORCED DELTA - Major deviation.** Mgmt baseline prescribes four standardized structures (quantitative deductive, qualitative inductive, theoretical/conceptual, integrative review), each with defined sections and progression. Feminist Review explicitly **rejects templates** and requires authors to organize sections around their substantive argument. No Theory section, no Methods section required. The paper develops through thematically organized sections defined by the author's analytical intervention. **Classification: ENFORCED.** This override appears in author guidelines.

### Hypotheses and Propositions
**PRACTICE ONLY - Not applicable.** Mgmt baseline uses H1/H2 hypotheses for quantitative work and P1/P2 propositions for theoretical work. Feminist Review does not use this framework. Articles advance "critical interventions" and analytical arguments, but not testable hypotheses or propositions. **Classification: FIELD DEFAULT** (not a deviation; Feminist Review is not hypothesis-testing research in the mgmt sense).

### Citation Density
**RELAXATION observed.** The editorial analyzed uses sparse, strategic citation (primarily in opening moves), contrasting with mgmt baseline (citation-dense introduction and theory sections). This may reflect the journal's emphasis on accessible, activist-oriented scholarship rather than comprehensive literature review. However, only one piece was analyzable; cannot confirm as enforced. **Classification: PRACTICE ONLY** (observed in one editorial, not corroborated across full sample).

### Voice and Person
**Follows FIELD_SKILL norm** with minor emphasis. First-person collective ("we") is standard and acceptable throughout, matching mgmt baseline. The editorial demonstrates inclusive, accessible tone ("We invite readers, scholars, and activists alike"), which aligns with—but may emphasize more strongly—mgmt's "direct academic voice." **Classification: FIELD DEFAULT.**

### Explicit Activist/Political Stance
**JOURNAL-SPECIFIC NORM.** Mgmt baseline does not mention activist or political orientation. Feminist Review explicitly frames scholarship as connected to feminist activism, social justice, and praxis ("timely interventions," "critical scholarly inquiry," "feminist futures"). Authors are expected to position their work in relation to decolonial, queer, and intersectional feminist movements. This is not a stylistic choice; it's integral to the journal's mission. **Classification: ENFORCED** (stated in journal scope and call-for-papers language).

### Keywords
**JOURNAL-SPECIFIC ENFORCED requirement.** Mgmt baseline does not mention keywords. Feminist Review requires minimum 6 keywords, placed after abstract, described as "suitable for indexing and abstracting services" and "as specific as possible to the research topic." This is a formal requirement, not optional.

### Intersectionality and Multiple Axes of Analysis
**JOURNAL-SPECIFIC ENFORCED norm.** Articles should explicitly engage with overlapping systems of oppression (race, class, sexuality, ableism, ethnicity, coloniality, capitalism). This is not merely recommended; the journal's mission statement makes clear that intersectional feminist understanding is foundational. **Classification: ENFORCED** (stated in journal scope and submission calls).

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Pronoun usage:** Matches FIELD_SKILL (first-person "we" standard; passive for methods/data sources).
- **Methods sections:** FIELD DEFAULT (not required; authors omit if their argument does not call for empirical method description).
- **Results/Findings sections:** FIELD DEFAULT (not required; evidence integrated into argument).
- **Hedging and epistemic stance:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (appropriate hedging for interpretive claims; direct assertion of analytical positions).
- **Discussion and Conclusion:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (implications of analysis; forward-looking reflection).
- **Reference lists and literature engagement:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (engage critically with cited work; note limitations and tensions).

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Attempting to follow a prescribed structure.** Authors coming from empirical disciplines often draft as Intro → Lit Review → Methods → Results → Discussion. Feminist Review will reject this template-driven approach. Sections must be organized substantively (e.g., "Colonial Temporalities," "Activism and Representation," "Rethinking Solidarity") and developed through argument, not methods-then-results progression.

2. **Burying intersectional analysis or treating it as an afterthought.** Intersectionality must be central to the analytical framework, not confined to a single section or added as revision. Articles that mention race/class/sexuality only in conclusion or methods section will not meet journal standards.

3. **Failing to anonymize.** Feminist Review uses triple-anonymized review. Remove author name, institution, identifying details, and personal pronouns that reveal identity (e.g., "my earlier work"; replace with "prior work by [Author]" or equivalent). Include personal information on a separate cover page only.

4. **Exceeding word count.** The 6,000–8,000-word limit is strict and includes all elements. Excessive citations, footnotes, or appendices that push beyond this will trigger desk rejection or mandatory cuts. Plan carefully; this is tighter than comparable journals.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

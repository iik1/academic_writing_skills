# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Interactive Marketing

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** MKT
- **Publisher:** SAGE (in partnership with the American Marketing Association; formerly published by Elsevier through ~2023, migrated to SAGE/AMA)
- **Scope:** Publishes empirical and conceptual research on interactive/digital marketing — social media, influencer marketing, live streaming, voice assistants, chatbots/AI, mobile and search advertising, e-commerce, and the metaverse/VR — spanning both consumer-experiment and large-scale digital-data methodologies.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/author-instructions/jnm (references the shared AMA journals guidelines at https://www.ama.org/submission-guidelines-american-marketing-association-journals/); PDF mirror at sagepub.com/docs/default-source/msg/submission-guidelines_-journal-of-interactive-marketing_-sage-journals.pdf
- **Field baseline:** mkt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 35 (abstracts/metadata via CrossRef for DOI prefix 10.1177 under ISSN 1094-9968, 2023–2026), including: "Uncomfortably Yours: When Personalization Repels Customers"; "How Influencers Grow: An Empirical Study and Future Research Agenda"; "Human Is Gold: Why Premium Customers Hate Chatbots and What to Do About It"; "Accepting Cryptocurrency as a Form of Payment and Its Impact on Firm Value"; "Shaping Strategy in the Digital Age: The Impact of Consumer Electronic Word of Mouth on Firm Strategic Emphasis"; "The Power of AI-Generated Voices: How Digital Vocal Tract Length Shapes Product Congruency and Ad Performance"; "Adoption of Virtual Reality Headsets"; "Prospecting Versus Retargeting: Insights from a Geography-Based Field Experiment"; and 27 others. Full text was not accessible (paywalled); assessment is based on abstracts, titles, and publication metadata.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows the shared AMA/SAGE marketing-journal template: Title page (with author info, separate file) → anonymized main document: Title → Abstract → Keywords → Main Text → References → Appendices. Web Appendix submitted as a separate PDF. A distinct short-form article type, **"JNM Reports,"** exists for timely, practice-relevant topics: under 4,000 words excluding references/abstract/tables/figures, with an expedited single-round review (reject or conditional accept after round one).

### Length
- Standard articles: maximum 50 pages inclusive of title, abstract, keywords, text, footnotes, references, tables, figures, and print appendices (shared AMA cap).
- JNM Reports: under 4,000 words (excluding abstract, references, tables, figures).
- Footnotes: typically no more than 10, each ≤40 words.

### Abstract
- Maximum 200 words, **written in third person** (not first-person "we" as in the body text — observed in practice: abstracts consistently use "the authors," "this study," "this article" rather than "we").
- Must summarize key manuscript components (question, method, finding, contribution).

### Citations
Author-date parenthetical, matching FIELD_SKILL's APA convention. All authors listed up to three; "et al." for four or more (shared AMA rule — slightly more permissive threshold before truncation than strict APA 7th, which truncates at three or more with ellipsis in reference lists for very long lists).

### Headings
Three-level maximum: primary (centered, title case, bold), secondary (flush left, title case, bold italic; minimum two required per section if used), tertiary (left-justified, sentence case, italic, on its own line above the paragraph).

### Tables and Figures
Placed within text at first mention (not at document end), numbered consecutively, titled with the key takeaway rather than a generic label, units specified in column headings, error bars and exact p-values required with means.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- No identifying information in the main manuscript or Web Appendix (double-blind review).
- No page numbers/headers/footers on submission.
- No gendered "he/she" language; use singular "they."
- AI-generated content must be disclosed in the Methods section and Acknowledgments.
- Self-plagiarism is treated as plagiarism (iThenticate screened).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Statistical Reporting
**ENFORCED.** Guidelines explicitly require exact p-values throughout (not threshold notation like p < .05), standard errors of parameter estimates in tables, and effect sizes for all empirical papers — an explicit move away from the significant/nonsignificant dichotomy. FIELD_SKILL documents significance-star and threshold conventions (***/**/*) as the field default; this journal's guidelines state a stronger, explicit preference against that shorthand. Treat as the governing convention when writing for this journal, though asterisk/threshold notation still appears in some published tables in practice.

### Structural Deviations
**ENFORCED (journal-specific article type).** The "JNM Reports" short-form track (sub-4,000-word, practice-focused, single expedited review round) has no analog in FIELD_SKILL, which does not distinguish short reports from standard articles. Use only when the manuscript is deliberately scoped as a practice-relevant short report.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
**PRACTICE ONLY / low-prevalence.** Titles occasionally carry an "EXPRESS:" prefix in the publication record — this is a SAGE OnlineFirst/rapid-publication production tag, not an author-facing style convention; do not imitate it in manuscript titles.

## Common Pitfalls
- Using significance-threshold notation (p < .05) instead of exact p-values in results tables — flagged explicitly in this journal's guidelines as something to avoid.
- Writing the abstract in first person ("we examine…") rather than third person ("the authors examine…" / "this study examines…") as observed in published abstracts.
- Submitting a standard-length manuscript formatted/pitched as a JNM Report (or vice versa) without matching the word-count and review-track expectations for that article type.
- Leaving identifying information in the Web Appendix rather than only the separate title page, breaking double-blind anonymization.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

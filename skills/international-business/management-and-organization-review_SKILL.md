# Journal Writing Skill: Management and Organization Review

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** IB&AREA
- **Publisher:** Cambridge University Press (for the International Association for Chinese Management Research, IACMR)
- **Scope:** The premier journal for insights about management and organizations in China and global comparative contexts; welcomes theory-testing, indigenous theory development, and phenomenon-driven research from organization behavior, organization theory, strategic management, economics, IB, sociology, and related disciplines.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/management-and-organization-review/information/author-instructions (detailed style PDF: MOR Author Submission Guidelines, last updated 21 Dec 2021)
- **Field baseline:** international-business-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 17 (Vol. 20 Iss. 1, Feb 2024, 6 articles; Vol. 19 Iss. 1, Feb-Mar 2023, 7 articles including Dialogue/Debate and Perspectives pieces; 10 FirstView articles, Jan-Jun 2026, covering digital poverty/entrepreneurship and Chinese-context special topics)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Mandatory main section headings, in order: INTRODUCTION, THEORETICAL BACKGROUND AND HYPOTHESES (if hypotheses are used), METHOD, RESULTS, DISCUSSION, CONCLUSION. Secondary headings organize within each. The paper body starts on page 2 with the heading INTRODUCTION (no title repeated). Tables, figures, and appendices go at the very end of the manuscript, after the references — not embedded in text. Notes (if used at all — discouraged) go on a separate page between text and references. Acknowledgements are submitted as a separate document, not in the main manuscript (added post-acceptance as the first NOTES entry) — this is stricter than typical blind-review practice.

### Length
No hard page limit, but manuscripts must be double-spaced, ≤40 pages inclusive of references/tables/figures, and typical accepted length is 25-40 pages. Explicit editorial guidance: "excessive references are not helpful — cite only the most representative and authoritative sources."

### Abstract
No more than 200 words, unstructured, on the first page along with 3-5 keywords and a short running title (<40 characters).

### Citations
Author-date parenthetical, alphabetized when multiple. Two-author citations always give both names (never "et al." for 2 authors). 3-6 authors: full list at first mention, "et al." thereafter. 7+ authors: "et al." even at first mention (reference list still gives all authors). No "ibid" or "op cit."

### Headings
Primary: CAPITALIZED, bold. Secondary: Title Case, bold. Tertiary: italicized, sentence case. All left-justified (not centered).

### Tables and Figures
No vertical lines; minimal internal horizontal lines. Tabs (not spaces) to separate data points. Data restricted to two decimal places. Footnote symbol order †, ‡, §, ¶ reserved for general notes; *, **, *** reserved exclusively for p-values. Variable names must match the text exactly and must not be abbreviated.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Self-citation must be depersonalized for blind review: write "In Smith (2000), results showed..." — explicitly NOT "In my previous research (Smith, 2000)..." or "The author's previous research (Smith, 2000)...". Word track-changes/version history and document Properties metadata must be scrubbed before submission (explicit technical instructions given). Generative AI tools cannot be listed as authors; AI use must be disclosed with tool name, version, dates, and access method.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
Guidelines explicitly instruct active voice and first person ("I"/"we") as the preferred default, framed more strongly than FIELD_SKILL's "first person is standard and expected" — MOR states it as a stylistic rule ("put sentences in the active voice... instead of the passive voice"), not just a norm for authors' own claims. ENFORCED (stated in guidelines; consistent with active, direct prose observed in sampled abstracts).

### Structural Deviations
The CAPITALIZED, mandatory main-heading list (INTRODUCTION / THEORETICAL BACKGROUND AND HYPOTHESES / METHOD / RESULTS / DISCUSSION / CONCLUSION) is more rigid and explicit than FIELD_SKILL's "near-universal but with variable optional sections" description — MOR names a fixed skeleton rather than leaving structure to author discretion. ENFORCED. Note the journal also regularly runs non-empirical formats not addressed by the empirical skeleton — Dialogue/Debate/Discussion pieces and Perspectives essays (observed in Vol. 19 Iss. 1) — which follow their own shorter, less formal structure; this is consistent with FIELD_SKILL's allowance for conceptual/commentary formats, not a delta.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Thematic specialization: nearly all sampled articles (14/17) are set in or motivated by the Chinese institutional context (state-owned enterprises, Chinese CEOs, Chinese firms, indigenous Chinese constructs like *moqi* 默契), even when the underlying theory is generalizable. This is a topical/framing specialization beyond FIELD_SKILL's general gap-framing guidance, not a prose-style delta — abstracts and introductions foreground "Chinese context" or "emerging market" framing explicitly. PRACTICE ONLY / journal-defining scope, not a stated style rule beyond the journal's mission statement.

Author nomination process (nominating Senior Editors and up to 4 potential reviewers) is a submission-workflow feature, not a writing-style delta — noted for completeness but does not affect drafting.

- Argumentation and rhetorical moves (CARS-style introductions, itemized contributions): FIELD DEFAULT
- Hedging and epistemic caution: FIELD DEFAULT

- Results reporting (tables + prose combined, present tense for findings): FIELD DEFAULT
- Citation density and use for paraphrase over quotation: FIELD DEFAULT
- Methods procedural detail and robustness reporting: FIELD DEFAULT

## Common Pitfalls
- Using "my previous research (Smith, 2000)" or similar self-identifying phrasing — MOR explicitly flags this as a blind-review violation and specifies the required depersonalized alternative.
- Embedding tables/figures in-text rather than placing them after the references at the end of the manuscript.
- Submitting a Word file with tracked-changes history or author metadata still present in Properties — MOR gives explicit pre-submission cleanup steps.
- Treating the 200-word abstract cap or the six-heading skeleton as flexible; both are stated as firm requirements, and failure to follow style guidelines can result in the manuscript being returned before review.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Networks and Spatial Economics

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Springer Nature
- **Scope:** Mathematical and numerical study of economic activities facilitated by human infrastructure (networks, spatial economics, regional development, transportation, telecommunications, energy systems).
- **Guidelines URL:** https://link.springer.com/journal/11067/submission-guidelines (Springer authentication required; details extracted from alternative sources)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 (Pumain 2025; Olaizola & Valenciano 2025; Song et al. 2026; Zhu et al. 2026; Zhang et al. 2025; Sun & Tian 2025)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
**Decimal heading system with maximum 3 levels.** Example: 1, 1.1, 1.1.1. No Roman numerals; use Arabic numerals only. Avoid single-level or two-level structures when three can clarify hierarchy.

### Length
No explicit page or word limits stated in publicly accessible guidelines. Standard Springer article length convention applies (typically 20–40 pages including references and appendices).

### Abstract
**Required: 150–250 words.** Must not contain undefined abbreviations or unspecified references. Keywords: not explicitly mandated in guidelines; observed in published articles. No structured abstract format required (unstructured prose acceptable).

### Citations
**Author-year system (parenthetical or in-text).** Format: (Author Year) or Author (Year). Multiple citations: (Author1 Year1; Author2 Year2). Three or more authors: (Cho et al. 2009). 

Publisher's citation style: SPBASIC (Springer Basic). Footnotes carry supplementary material, not primary citations. Numbered consecutively in text; table footnotes use superscript lowercase letters.

### Headings
**Decimal system only. Example heading structure:**
```
1. Introduction
1.1 Background
1.1.1 Motivation
2. Methods
2.1 Data
2.2 Model specification
3. Results
4. Conclusion
```
Do NOT use section numbers within prose; reserve them for heading labels only.

### Tables and Figures
No explicit prohibitions observed. Tables and figures integrate results; captions must be self-contained and concise. Figure resolution and sizing follow Springer standard requirements (high resolution for print).

### File Format
Submission: MS Word (docx or doc format) or LaTeX (Springer template recommended). No other formats accepted.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Manuscripts must not contain undefined abbreviations (first use must define all acronyms).
- No unspecified references in abstract.
- LLM use must be documented in Methods section (or alternative section if Methods not present).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates from the ABS 4*/4 field baseline.

### Theory Without Empirics
**RELAXATION** — The journal explicitly welcomes theoretical papers without empirical examples or numerical validation. Field baseline (FIELD_SKILL) implicitly assumes empirical papers or theory-with-applications; this journal states: "well thought out theoretical analyses of important network and spatial economic problems will be considered without bias even if they do not include case studies or numerical examples."

**Implication:** A paper consisting entirely of model setup, theoretical results, and mathematical characterization (no data, no calibration, no empirical example) is acceptable here and publishable, whereas at ABS 4*/4 journals, such a paper would require at least an illustrative application or structural estimation exercise. Proofs and theoretical derivations need not be accompanied by empirical validation.

### Scope Specificity
**PRACTICE ONLY** — The journal has narrower disciplinary scope than top-tier general economics journals. It primarily serves network economists, spatial economists, regional scientists, applied mathematicians, and infrastructure engineers. Papers must address network topology, spatial economic activity, or infrastructure-related questions. Pure macro, labor, or industrial organization papers without a network or spatial dimension are outside scope.

**Implication:** While FIELD_SKILL assumes broad economic topics, this journal requires explicit engagement with network or spatial framing. A paper on, say, monetary policy or labor market dynamics must be reframed through a network or spatial lens to fit scope.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Undefined abbreviations in abstract:** The abstract is a common location for first use of acronyms. Ensure all abbreviations (e.g., GCN, ST-CCF, MSTIM) are spelled out on first use within the abstract itself, not deferred to the main text.

2. **Mixing heading level styles:** Using both Roman numerals and decimal numerals, or inconsistent nesting depth (e.g., sometimes 2 levels, sometimes 3), violates the decimal system rule. Audit all headings for consistency.

3. **Overstating empirical scope of a theory paper:** If submitting a pure theory paper, do not promise or imply an empirical test or numerical application in the introduction unless you deliver it. The journal accepts theoretical work as-is, so undersell scope rather than oversell.

4. **Misalignment with journal scope:** Submitting papers on general economic topics (e.g., trade policy, corporate finance) without a network or spatial dimension wastes reviewer time. Ensure the paper's central contribution is framed through network topology, spatial distribution, or infrastructure systems.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

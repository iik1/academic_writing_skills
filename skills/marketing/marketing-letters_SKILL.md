# Journal Writing Skill: Marketing Letters

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** MKT
- **Publisher:** Springer Nature
- **Scope:** Publishes short, high-quality papers on current marketing topics (empirical findings, methodological papers, theoretical/conceptual insights) with emphasis on immediacy and provoking discussion, rather than long-form comprehensive studies. Also runs a dedicated "Replication Corner" for preregistered conceptual replications that extend the boundary conditions of influential published marketing papers.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://link.springer.com/journal/11002/submission-guidelines (guidelines page sits behind an IdP redirect in this environment; content triangulated via search snippets and cached indexing)
- **Field baseline:** mkt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 8 with abstract-level detail, plus ~90 additional titles/metadata surveyed across 2023–2026 issues for structural pattern (IDEAS/RePEc index). Abstract-level: "Penny-wise pound-fooling" (Claus & Pandelaere), "The offer framing effect: a replication and extension" (Pfeifer & Schreiner), "Tighter nets for smaller fishes?" (Krefeld-Schwalb & Scheibehenne), "Understanding effect sizes in consumer psychology" (Dias, Spiller & Fitzsimons), "A meta-analysis of the effects of sponsorship disclosure in influencer marketing" (Liu & Zhao), "Establishing the link" (Filippou, Georgiadis & Jha), "'Dark patterns' in online services" (Runge, Wentzel, Huh et al.), "Customer satisfaction, loyalty behaviors, and firm financial performance" (Mittal et al.).

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No fixed section template is mandated beyond standard IMRaD-type organization; the journal's defining constraint is brevity, not a prescribed section list. A dedicated "Replication Corner" article type exists for preregistered conceptual replications of influential published marketing findings — this is a distinct, explicitly invited submission category not present in FIELD_SKILL.

### Length
- Main-track initial submissions: maximum 4,000 words, inclusive of abstract, main text, and references. Revisions may extend to 5,000 words.
- Up to 4 tables and/or figures, which do not count toward the word limit; additional material goes in a web appendix.
- Replication Corner submissions: maximum 4,000 words inclusive of abstract, text, and references; up to 3 tables/figures.
- This is dramatically shorter than the FIELD_SKILL norm of 8,000–16,000 words for full articles.

### Abstract
Unstructured, capped at approximately 150 words — tighter than the FIELD_SKILL 150–250 word range.

### Citations
APA style, consistent with FIELD_SKILL. No journal-specific deviation found.

### Headings
No distinct convention found beyond standard section headings; keywords list follows the abstract (confirmed in spot-checked article), consistent with general journal practice.

### Tables and Figures
Hard cap of 4 (main track) or 3 (Replication Corner) tables/figures not counted toward the word limit — this cap itself is the notable constraint, tying directly to the short-paper format.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Data policy is stringent and explicit: empirical submissions must make available the raw dataset(s) (with only identifying fields removed, timestamps retained), a description of the original data source, all code used to build analysis datasets and run analyses, and all stimuli/survey instruments/experimental instructions — accessible to reviewers and readers. This is more prescriptive than typical FIELD_SKILL data-availability expectations.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
Given the 4,000–5,000 word ceiling, papers cannot sustain the FIELD_SKILL norm of 3–7 full studies with individually reported Methods/Results per study, nor the long-form Data → Model → Results quantitative template. Spot-checked papers compress to a single focused empirical contribution (one dataset, one meta-analysis, or 1–2 tightly linked studies) rather than a multi-study program. Meta-analytic and "state of the field" methodological papers (e.g., on effect sizes, statistical practices, replicability) are a recurring and welcomed article type — more prominent here than the FIELD_SKILL baseline suggests for a general MKT outlet. RELAXATION (ABS 3): the journal relaxes the FIELD_SKILL expectation of extensive multi-study robustness in favor of a single, well-identified contribution reported concisely.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
- Titles frequently use a colon-split hook + descriptive subtitle format with a playful or provocative hook phrase before the colon (e.g., "Penny-wise pound-fooling:", "Tighter nets for smaller fishes?"), more so than the field default — consistent with the journal's stated mission to publish "provocative, novel findings... that spark conversations."
- Replication and "replication and extension" papers are a frequent, explicitly branded category (not just an occasional article type as in FIELD_SKILL), often citing the specific original study and prior conflicting replication directly in the abstract.

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting a full-length multi-study manuscript (8,000+ words) as originally structured for JCR/JM/JMR — the 4,000-word initial cap forces compression to a single study/analysis, not a shortened write-up of a multi-study design.
- Omitting the data/code/materials accessibility package required by the journal's data policy; this is checked at review, not just post-acceptance.
- Writing an abstract near the FIELD_SKILL 200–250 word ceiling instead of the journal's ~150-word cap.
- Treating "Replication Corner" as an informal add-on rather than following its specific format (preregistration, focus on boundary conditions of an influential published finding, separate 3-table/figure cap).

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

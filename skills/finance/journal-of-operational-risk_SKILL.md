# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Operational Risk

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** FINANCE
- **Publisher:** Incisive Media Ltd. (Risk.net journal portfolio)
- **Scope:** Measurement, modeling, and management of operational risk and broader non-financial risk (enterprise risk, cyber/IT risk, data quality, climate-related risk) in financial institutions, with emphasis on quantitative techniques (copulas, aggregate loss distributions, extreme value theory, Bayesian methods) as well as regulatory/practitioner-facing applied work.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict. Sourced from the shared Risk.net "Risk Journals Submission Guidelines" page, which applies uniformly across the Risk.net journal portfolio (Journal of Operational Risk, Journal of Risk, Journal of Credit Risk, etc.); no journal-specific supplement was found.

### Structure
Title page (author details, running header ≤50 characters, word count, figure/table count) separate from the anonymized main text (blind review). Abstract, keywords (4–6), and "key messages" (3–4 bullets, ≤85 characters each) precede the main text. Recommended (advisory, not mandatory) section order: Introduction, Literature Review, Data, Methodology, Results, Discussion, Conclusion. Declarations of Interest and Acknowledgements sections required. References follow.

### Length
Research papers: ≤10,000 words. Forum/shorter papers: ≤7,000 words. Guideline, not a hard cap.

### Abstract
One paragraph, 150–200 words, must stand alone (no citations), covering purpose, principal results, and conclusions.

### Citations
In-text: author-date per Chicago Manual of Style. Reference list: APA format, alphabetical by surname, first initials only, DOIs included where available. (Field baseline already specifies author-year parenthetical citation — this is consistent, not a delta.)

### Headings
No journal-specific heading rules beyond the advisory section list above.

### Tables and Figures
Kept to a minimum; submitted in the main PDF and as separate editable vector files (EPS/SVG/PDF from Excel, PowerPoint, or Illustrator); rasterized images need ≥300dpi; figures appear in color online.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Manuscript must not be published or under review elsewhere. No author names/affiliations in the main text (blind review). Generative AI use restricted to data extraction, background research, and editing assistance only — not for drafting substantive content. Footnotes used sparingly (≤2 sentences each).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
RELAXATION. Several spot-checked articles are practitioner/survey-style or emerging-economy applied studies (South African bank employee perception survey, Greek IT/audit case study, China anti-corruption quasi-natural experiment) rather than the tightly identification-focused empirical style of ABS 4*/4 finance. Framing is more descriptive/applied ("the authors present and analyse a survey...", "the authors offer suggestions for policymakers") than the assertive, contribution-driven register FIELD_SKILL describes. Consistent with ABS 2 tier — a relaxation of, not a departure from, field norms.

### Structural Deviations
PRACTICE ONLY. A substantial share of articles are conceptual/survey/bibliometric in nature (e.g., "Key principles for operational risk stress testing design and evaluation" — a conceptual framework paper; "Mapping the influence of enterprise risk management: a systematic review and bibliometric analysis") rather than the standard empirical-paper structure (data/identification/results/robustness) assumed as default in FIELD_SKILL. Authors writing conceptual, survey, or bibliometric papers for this journal should expect more latitude on the Data/Methodology/Results skeleton than FIELD_SKILL implies.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
PRACTICE ONLY. Topic scope is broader than mainstream finance: several articles concern non-financial/operational themes with limited quantitative identification (workplace accident perception and productivity, IT/internal audit effectiveness, geopolitical risk perception and innovation) — closer to management/risk-management journal style than asset-pricing or corporate-finance empirical norms. Authors should calibrate expectations toward applied/practitioner relevance rather than pure academic identification rigor.

## Common Pitfalls
- Assuming a rigid empirical-paper skeleton is mandatory: this journal publishes conceptual, survey, and bibliometric papers alongside quantitative ones, so the Introduction/Data/Methodology/Results/Conclusion order is advisory, not required.
- Treating the guidelines as journal-specific: the submission guidelines page is shared across the entire Risk.net Risk Journals portfolio (Journal of Risk, Journal of Credit Risk, etc.), so double-check any journal-specific call-for-papers page for updates before submission.
- Overreaching on identification rigor: given the applied/practitioner and emerging-market case-study articles common in this journal, reviewers appear more tolerant of descriptive/survey framing than top-tier finance journals.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

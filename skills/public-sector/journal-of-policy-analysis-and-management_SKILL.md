# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Policy Analysis and Management

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** PUB SEC
- **Publisher:** Wiley-Blackwell
- **Scope:** Publishes theoretical, empirical, and methodological research on domestic
  and international public policy and management, aimed jointly at academics and
  practitioners; sponsored by APPAM (Association for Public Policy Analysis and
  Management). Content spans multiple distinct article types: Feature Research
  Articles, Point/Counterpoint, Methods for Policy Analysis, Policy Insights,
  Policy Retrospectives, and Book Reviews.

### Structure
Six distinct article categories exist, each with different expectations: Feature
Research Articles (the standard empirical/theoretical paper), Point/Counterpoint
(short, invited, non-peer-reviewed debate pieces between scholars/practitioners
on a timely topic), Methods for Policy Analysis, Policy Insights, Policy
Retrospectives (synthesis/review of a policy area, research stream, or method),
and Book Reviews. Feature Research Articles require abstract, introduction,
methods, results, and conclusions as an editable single file. Double-blind
review is standard: the main manuscript must be fully anonymized, with author
identity, affiliation, and acknowledgments supplied only in a separate title
page file.

### Length
No explicit page/word cap for Feature Research Articles was found in accessible
guidance (do not assume a strict cap for standard research articles). Note:
one snippet referenced a much shorter ~3,000-word limit, but this could not be
confirmed to apply to the standard Feature Research Article category rather
than a shorter department (e.g., Point/Counterpoint or Policy Insights) —
treat as unconfirmed and verify directly with the submission portal before
relying on it.

### Abstract
Required as part of the manuscript; no confirmed structured-abstract mandate
or explicit word cap found in accessible guidance.

### Citations
References must follow JPAM's house format, described as based on APA7 style
(author-date, consistent with FIELD_SKILL norm).

### Headings
No journal-specific heading convention found beyond the required section set
above; follows FIELD_SKILL norm otherwise.

### Tables and Figures
Explicit guidance instructs authors to avoid placing regression tables or
equations in the main text, instead conveying results through descriptive
prose, charts, or figures — regression tables/equations are pushed to an
appendix or supplementary material. This is a notable delta from typical
economics-adjacent PUB SEC practice of placing full regression tables in the
main results section.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Authors are explicitly told to avoid "shorthand and jargon understood
exclusively by specialists operating in narrow fields" — plain-language
accessibility is treated as a submission requirement, not just a stylistic
preference. Data and code supporting published results must be archived in a
public repository as a prerequisite for publication.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
Consistent with the explicit plain-language mandate, spot-checked abstracts
lean toward accessible, non-technical framing of findings even for
econometrically sophisticated papers (e.g., bounds-based discrimination
estimation, quasi-experimental tax-incidence analysis) — technical method
names are named but immediately translated into a plain substantive claim in
the same sentence or the next one. This is a sharper accessibility emphasis
than the FIELD_SKILL baseline's general "defined briefly at first use" norm.
GUIDELINE ONLY / PRACTICE ONLY (stated in guidelines and consistent with what
abstract-level text shows, though full-text confirmation was not possible).

### Statistical Reporting
The explicit instruction to keep regression tables and equations out of the
main text (routing them to appendices/supplementary material) is a structural
delta from FIELD_SKILL, which describes tables and figures used "extensively
alongside" prose in the main text. ENFORCED (stated in guidelines; consistent
with the applied, practitioner-facing framing seen across sampled abstracts,
though direct main-text inspection was not possible due to paywall).

### Citation Density
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm — no delta observed at abstract level.

### Structural Deviations
JPAM's roster of six named article types (Feature Research Article,
Point/Counterpoint, Methods for Policy Analysis, Policy Insights, Policy
Retrospectives, Book Reviews) is more finely differentiated than FIELD_SKILL's
generic macro-structure, which describes only the standard empirical-article
format. Authors should identify which department they are targeting, since
length, peer-review status (Point/Counterpoint is not peer-reviewed), and
structural expectations differ by department. STRUCTURAL DELTA (stated in
guidelines).

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Spot-checked papers strongly favor applied, causally-identified empirical
work evaluating a specific real-world policy or program (job-training
evaluation, tax-incidence pass-through, driver's-license policy effects on
crash rates, foster-care placement disparities, unemployment-insurance IT
administration, cash-transfer long-run effects) using quasi-experimental
designs (difference-in-differences, RCTs, bounds estimation, administrative
panel data) rather than conceptual or purely qualitative pieces. This is
consistent with, not a deviation from, FIELD_SKILL's description of
practice-variation across the field, but is worth noting as this journal's
particular center of gravity within that range. PRACTICE ONLY, low-prevalence
claim strength given sample size (n=10, abstracts only).

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting a standard main-text regression table block as in a typical
  economics or PUB SEC paper — JPAM guidance directs authors to move
  regression tables/equations out of the main text and communicate results
  descriptively or via charts/figures instead.
- Writing for a narrow specialist audience — JPAM's explicit plain-language/
  jargon-avoidance instruction is stricter than the general field norm and is
  treated as a submission requirement.
- Submitting to the wrong department without checking peer-review status or
  length expectations — Point/Counterpoint pieces are short, invited, and not
  peer-reviewed, unlike Feature Research Articles.
- Assuming a firm word cap for a standard empirical article: one unconfirmed
  ~3,000-word figure surfaced in search snippets could not be verified as
  applying to Feature Research Articles specifically; confirm current limits
  directly on the ScholarOne submission portal before finalizing length.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

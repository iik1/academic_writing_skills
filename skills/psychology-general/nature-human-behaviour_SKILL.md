# Journal Writing Skill: Nature Human Behaviour

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 4
- **Field:** PSYCH (GENERAL)
- **Publisher:** Springer Nature
- **Scope:** Publishes research of outstanding significance on any aspect of
  individual or collective human behaviour, explicitly welcoming submissions
  from psychology, economics, political science, sociology, anthropology,
  neuroscience, and other disciplines, with an emphasis on work relevant to
  societal challenges and public policy.
- **Guidelines URL:** nature.com/nathumbehav/submission-guidelines (direct
  fetch blocked by login redirect in this environment; content below is
  from indexed/cached search snippets and a third-party submission guide)
- **Field baseline:** psychology-general-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 (title/abstract-level access only; full text
  paywalled) — "Political segregation in the US workplace" (Frake, Hurst &
  Kagan, 2026), "Potential mechanisms and functional significance of
  aperiodic neural activity" (Preston, Smith & Voytek, 2026), "Information

  class with large-scale replications in four countries" (Registered
  Report, 2025), an adolescent-mental-health/social-media Registered
  Report (2025), plus browsing of the 2025/2026 article-type listings
  (Matters Arising, Comment, Review, Perspective counts and word limits).

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No fixed heading hierarchy is mandated for initial submission ("does not
need to be specially formatted"). The journal publishes multiple distinct
content types with different structural expectations, which is itself a
delta from FIELD_SKILL's simpler IMRaD/thematic-review split:
- **Article** (standard empirical report): main text up to 5,000 words,
  Methods word-uncapped and excluded from the main-text limit, up to 8
  figures/tables total.
- **Registered Report**: Stage 1 (question, background, hypotheses,
  procedures, analysis pipeline, sampling/power plan, pilot data if any)
  is peer reviewed and can receive in-principle acceptance (AIP) before
  data collection; Stage 1 protocols are not published separately but
  registered externally and integrated into the final article.
- **Review**: 4,000–5,000 words, ~150 references.
- **Perspective**: peer reviewed, ~100 references.
- **Comment**: up to 1,800 words, flexible/opinion format on scientific,
  ethical, legal, or policy issues.
- **Matters Arising**: challenges/clarifications of a published paper,
  main text ideally ≤1,200 words, extended Methods pushed to
  Supplementary Information; published alongside a Reply from the
  original authors.

### Length
Article main text: up to 5,000 words, excluding abstract, Methods,
references, and figure legends. Methods has no stated word limit.

### Abstract
Up to 150 words, unreferenced (no citations in the abstract).

### Citations
Not explicitly specified beyond standard Nature Portfolio numbered-citation
convention; no journal-specific delta confirmed.

### Headings
Results and Methods are divided by topical subheadings; per available
guidance the Discussion does not use subheadings — a plausible delta from
FIELD_SKILL's silence on this, but confidence is moderate (single
secondary source).

### Tables and Figures
Up to 8 display items (figures and/or tables combined) per Article —
tighter than FIELD_SKILL's unspecified norm. Figures are expected to be
ordered so the strongest, most generalizable evidence appears early.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Do not overstate causal claims in the abstract unless the design
  supports causal inference (consistent with FIELD_SKILL's hedging norm,
  but the journal states it explicitly for the abstract specifically).
- Cover letters must disclose related/under-consideration manuscripts and
  any prior discussion with an NHB editor.
- Final (post-acceptance) manuscripts must be Word or TeX/LaTeX — PDF is
  not accepted at that stage (submission-mechanics only, not a writing
  norm).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
Editorial guidance (World Bank Q&A with an NHB editor) explicitly asks
authors to avoid explicit novelty claims and exaggerated self-evaluative
language, letting the evidence carry the claim rather than the author's
framing — a sharper, more explicit version of FIELD_SKILL's general
hedging norm. GUIDELINE ONLY / editorial-practice source, not the formal
author instructions, but corroborated by a second independent source
(third-party submission guide) that describes editors reading for direct,
unhedged statements of what was found rather than qualified phrasing like
"may potentially suggest." Treat as PRACTICE ONLY given reliance on
secondary sources, but flag as a genuine directional delta: NHB appears to
reward more declarative framing of results than the typical FIELD_SKILL
empirical article, while still requiring FIELD_SKILL-level hedging on
generalizability and causal claims.

### Structural Deviations
Robustness/sensitivity checks that would normally sit in the main text of
an economics- or social-science-style empirical paper are routed to
Supplementary Information at NHB, explicitly to keep the main narrative
readable for a cross-disciplinary, non-specialist audience. This is a
genuine structural delta from FIELD_SKILL, which describes robustness as
typically demonstrated via internal/conceptual replication within the
main text. PRACTICE ONLY (single editor-interview source), but plausible
given the journal's explicit cross-disciplinary accessibility mandate and
the tight 5,000-word/8-display-item caps that make full main-text
robustness treatment impractical.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
NHB operates a distinctive multi-format content taxonomy (Article,
Registered Report, Review, Perspective, Comment, Matters Arising) each
with its own word limit and structural expectations, unlike most
FIELD_SKILL journals which mainly publish empirical reports plus one
review/synthesis format. Registered Reports are a much more central and
routine track here (multiple per year observed) than the occasional
mention in FIELD_SKILL. Writers should confirm which content type they
are targeting before applying any structural template.

The journal's explicit interdisciplinary mandate (welcoming psychology,
economics, political science, sociology, neuroscience, etc. as equal
contributors) means jargon-avoidance and non-specialist accessibility are
enforced more strongly than FIELD_SKILL's general "define jargon at first
use" — NHB guidance calls for writing comprehensible to specialists in
adjacent fields, not just avoiding undefined terms for a general
psychology audience.

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting a psychology-only-framed paper without an explicit
  cross-disciplinary significance argument; NHB editors reportedly cite
  this as the most common reason a within-discipline paper is judged
  out of scope.
- Exceeding the 8-figure/table display-item cap or padding the main text
  with robustness analyses that belong in Supplementary Information.
- Treating the 150-word abstract as a place for hedged, qualified
  language rather than a direct one/two-sentence statement of the
  conceptual advance.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

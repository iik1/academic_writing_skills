# Journal Writing Skill: Asian Economic Journal

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Wiley-Blackwell, on behalf of the East Asian Economic Association (EAEA)
- **Scope:** Publishes theoretical, empirical, and policy-oriented economics research related to one or more of 27 designated Asian economies (Afghanistan through Vietnam, including China, India, Japan, Korea, and Southeast/South/Central Asian states). Explicitly a forum for debate among theorists, practitioners, and researchers.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/page/journal/14678381/homepage/forauthors.html
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 8 (abstract/title level; full text was paywalled — see Sources). Titles: "How do tax revenues respond to GDP growth? Evidence from developing Asia, 1998–2021" (Hill et al., 2025); "Corporate Tax Avoidance and Firm Diversification: Evidence from Chinese Listed Firms" (Qin, Liu & Chen, 2022); "Cyclical shocks and spatial association of Indonesia's district-level per capita income" (Kataoka, 2022); "Determinants of Earnings and Gender Earnings Gaps among Highly Educated Workers within Major Cities in Asian Countries" (Takeuchi, 2021); "How inequality declined in Mongolia: A detailed income source analysis" (Zagdbazar, 2026, title/scope only); "Maternal education and preventive health behavior: Evidence from Cambodia" (Chov & Ishikawa, 2026, title/scope only); "The impact of government digital attention on trade in services" (Ma, Lin & Zhan, 2026, title/scope only); "The impact of services trade liberalization on the productivity of manufacturing firms" (Zhang, Zhang, Findlay & Roelfsema, 2026, title/scope only).

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No mandated section-by-section structure beyond standard IMRAD; guidelines specify front-matter requirements rather than body structure (see Abstract below). Standard empirical/theory structures from FIELD_SKILL apply.

### Length
Total submission (title, author info, abstract, text, tables, figures, notes, references, appendices) must not exceed the AEJ published-paper limit of 8,000 words by more than 20% — i.e., a hard ceiling of roughly 9,600 words at submission. This is materially shorter than typical ABS 4*/4 submissions and tighter than FIELD_SKILL implies; RELAXATION is the wrong label here since it is a stricter constraint, not a looser one.

### Abstract
Short abstract of approximately 100 words on the first page (unstructured, no required subheadings), plus keywords and JEL classification codes. This is much shorter than typical top-tier abstracts (which often run 150–250 words) and does not follow a structured (Background/Methods/Results) format.

### Citations
No explicit journal-mandated citation style found in the public guidelines; FIELD_SKILL author-year convention applies by default (confirmed by journal's economics disciplinary norms).

### Headings
No explicit heading convention stated beyond standard practice.

### Tables and Figures
No explicit table/figure rules found in the public guidelines beyond standard formatting expectations.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Main text file must not contain any author-identifying information (double-blind review); author identity, affiliations, and acknowledgements go only on a separate title page.
- Submission must be clearly related to at least one of the 27 economies in the AEJ's defined scope list — papers without a clear connection to these economies are out of scope regardless of quality.
- At least one author must be a paid-up EAEA member, or a non-member submission fee of US$100 applies.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm at the abstract/title level (direct, quantitative claims — e.g., Hill et al. report "tax buoyancies... were close to one," "excess tax revenue loss is about half a percentage point").

### Statistical Reporting
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm: abstracts report magnitudes directly (e.g., Kataoka: "nearly half of all districts widened the income disparities with their neighbors"; Hill et al.: precise percentage-point figures). No delta observed.

### Citation Density
Not assessable from abstract-level access; no delta identified.

### Structural Deviations
None observed beyond the tighter length ceiling already captured under Hard Constraints.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Topical/geographic framing is a defining and consistent feature: every sampled title and abstract anchors the contribution to a specific Asian country or regional comparison (Mongolia, Cambodia, Indonesia, China, developing Asia) rather than a general theoretical contribution. This is a scope requirement (see Explicit Prohibitions) reflected consistently in practice — PRACTICE ONLY/ENFORCED via scope, not a voice or structural deviation.

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting a paper without an explicit, substantive connection to one or more of the 27 listed Asian economies — the journal enforces geographic/topical scope, not just thematic relevance.
- Exceeding the 8,000-word target (9,600-word hard ceiling including everything: text, tables, references, appendices) — this is tighter than typical field practice and easy to overshoot with a standard-length ABS 4 draft.
- Leaving author-identifying information in the main text file rather than confining it to the separate title page, given double-blind review.
- Writing a long, structured, top-tier-style abstract; AEJ expects a short (~100-word) unstructured abstract.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

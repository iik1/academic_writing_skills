# Field Writing Style: ENT-SBM

## Scope and Intended Use
This baseline captures the normative writing conventions of top entrepreneurship
and small business management (ENT-SBM) research, derived from ABS 4* and 4
journals: Entrepreneurship Theory and Practice, Journal of Business Venturing
(both 4*), and Strategic Entrepreneurship Journal (4). It applies to both
quantitative empirical work (survey- and archival-based) and conceptual/review
articles that are common in this field. Journal-specific skills should be read
as amendments to this baseline; where a journal-specific skill is silent on a
dimension, the practices below apply by default.

## Typical Article Structure
At the ABS 4*/4 level, empirical articles follow a recognizable macro structure:
Abstract, Keywords, Introduction, Theory/Literature Review (often organized
around named theoretical lenses or constructs), Hypothesis Development (for
quantitative deductive work), Methods, Results, Discussion (including
Contributions and Practical/Managerial Implications as near-universal
subsections), Limitations and Future Research, and Conclusion. A "Managerial
Summary" or "Practical Implications" subsection is expected in leading
journals in this field, reflecting entrepreneurship's applied orientation.
Appendices reporting detailed sampling/screening procedures, robustness
checks, and construct measurement are common and sometimes extensive,
particularly for survey-based studies. Multi-study designs (e.g., a primary
study plus a supplementary/replication study using an independent or archival
sample) are a well-established structural option for strengthening causal or
construct validity claims, not a deviation from norm. Conceptual and review
articles substitute a more thematic structure (motivation, theoretical
synthesis, propositions or a research agenda, contributions) for the
hypothesis-testing spine, but retain the Introduction-Contributions-Discussion
skeleton. This is CONSENSUS across the sampled journals.

## Voice, Person, and Register
Leading journals in this field expect first-person plural voice ("we test,"
"we argue," "we find") throughout, including in the theory and hypothesis
sections, alongside active voice as the default. Passive constructions appear
selectively in methods reporting of procedures (e.g., "participants were
asked to...") but even there, first-person active framing dominates
description of researcher choices ("we recruited," "we screened," "we
estimate"). Register is formal but not stiff; rhetorical framing devices
(explicitly naming "contributions," numbering them, and using signposting
phrases such as "we contribute to the literature in three ways") are expected
rather than a mark of an individual author's style. This is CONSENSUS.

## Sentence and Paragraph Style
Sentences are typically medium to long, often compound, with frequent
embedded citations that do not interrupt the flow of argument. Paragraphs in
the introduction and theory sections are dense and multi-citation, building
cumulative claims sentence by sentence toward a hypothesis or proposition.
Topic sentences generally state a claim or finding before elaborating
mechanism. Paragraph length is flexible in the discussion and limitations
sections, where argumentation is more discursive and exploratory in tone.

## Argumentation and Rhetorical Moves
The field follows a clear CARS-style (Create-a-Research-Space) pattern:
establish the phenomenon's importance and general research territory, narrow
to a specific gap or tension in the literature (explicitly using language
such as "we know less about," "less attention has been paid to," or "prior
research has tended to focus on X rather than Y"), and then state the study's
specific research question(s), often posed explicitly as a question sentence.
Contributions are near-universally enumerated explicitly and are typically
framed as extending, challenging, reconciling, or bridging existing streams
of theory rather than as wholly novel discoveries. Framing a study's
theoretical lens up front (e.g., adopting a named theoretical framework such
as a sociological, psychological, or economic theory as an explicit "lens")
is a common and expected rhetorical move, more so than in many other
management subfields. Disagreement in the literature is handled by
presenting "mixed findings" or "conflicting results" neutrally and then
positioning the current study as reconciling or contextualizing them, rather
than through pointed critique of specific prior authors. This is CONSENSUS.

## Methods Conventions
Empirical methods sections are written in past tense and are highly
procedural, with extensive detail on sampling, screening, and data quality
safeguards — this level of procedural transparency (often relegated to
appendices) is a distinguishing feature of the field, driven by heavy reliance
on online panels (e.g., Prolific, MTurk) and archival/survey datasets.
Construct measurement is reported with full operational detail: item wording
or examples, response scales, and reliability coefficients (Cronbach's alpha)
are consistently reported for every multi-item scale. Robustness is
demonstrated through supplementary or replication studies using independent
or archival samples, alternative operationalizations of key constructs, and
sensitivity checks reported either in the main text or appendices (e.g.,
listwise vs. pairwise deletion, alternative controls). Common-method bias is
explicitly and self-consciously addressed as a threat to validity, typically
via temporal separation of predictor and outcome measurement waves and
explicit discussion of mitigation steps. Practice varies on whether structural
equation modeling, PROCESS-macro-based regression, or panel/hierarchical
methods are used as the primary estimation approach; both are well
represented and neither constitutes a deviation from norm.

## Results and Reporting Norms
Results sections separate reporting from interpretation: hypothesis-by-
hypothesis reporting of coefficients, standard errors or confidence
intervals, and significance levels precedes any discussion of substantive
meaning, which is deferred to the Discussion section. Full correlation/
descriptive statistics tables and regression tables are standard and
expected, with precise coefficient values, standard errors, and p-value
thresholds reported to two or three decimal places. Effect sizes (e.g.,
Cohen's f-squared, R-squared change) are increasingly expected in addition to
significance tests, reflecting broader methodological rigor norms. Figures
depicting interaction effects, mediation/moderation path diagrams, and time-
trend plots are common and are treated as integral to the results narrative,
not merely illustrative. This is CONSENSUS.

## Discussion and Conclusion Norms
The Discussion is structured into clearly labeled subsections: Contributions
(explicitly enumerated and mapped back to the gaps identified in the
introduction), Practical/Managerial Implications (a near-universal,
substantial subsection reflecting the field's applied orientation and
relevance to founders, managers, and policymakers), and Limitations and
Future Research (extensive, often multi-paragraph, treating limitations as an
opportunity to open a forward-looking research agenda rather than as a
perfunctory disclaimer). The Conclusion is typically brief and restates core
findings and their theoretical significance without introducing new material.
This is CONSENSUS.

## Citation and Literature Engagement
Citation density is high throughout the introduction and theory sections,
often with multiple supporting citations per claim, and noticeably lower in
the methods and results sections (where citations are reserved for
justifying measurement choices or analytical techniques) and moderate in the
discussion. Paraphrase dominates; direct quotation is used sparingly and
mainly to introduce a precise technical definition of a construct (e.g.,
quoting a foundational definition of an established construct) or a
particularly apt turn of phrase from a canonical source. Disagreement across
studies is synthesized descriptively ("some research suggests X, whereas
other work finds Y") rather than adjudicated through direct critique of named
authors. Self-citation and citation of the same lead theorists (e.g.,
foundational entrepreneurship and management scholars) recur heavily,
reflecting a relatively concentrated theoretical canon that authors are
expected to visibly engage with. This is CONSENSUS.

## Hedging and Epistemic Caution
Hedging is moderate and concentrated in specific locations: causal claims
are consistently softened via explicit discussion of selection effects,
common-method variance, and generalizability limits in the Discussion and
Limitations sections, while the Results section itself reports findings in
direct, unhedged declarative language ("Hypothesis 1 is supported"). Authors
routinely acknowledge sample constraints (e.g., predominantly one
demographic composition, single-country context) as explicit, named
limitations rather than glossing over them. Forward-looking hedges ("future
research should," "we encourage scholars to examine") are a standard and
expected close to nearly every limitation raised, functioning as a rhetorical
convention for converting weaknesses into a research agenda. This is
CONSENSUS.

## What This Baseline Does NOT Specify
This baseline does not specify journal-specific word or page limits,
formatting or reference style requirements, specific submission or review
process expectations, preferred statistical software, or a fixed number of
studies required per submission — these vary by journal and should be
addressed in journal-specific skills. It also does not adjudicate between
quantitative and qualitative/conceptual methodological traditions, both of
which are represented at the ABS 4*/4 level; a qualitative or purely
conceptual paper should consult a journal-specific skill or additional
guidance for structural conventions beyond what is described here for
theory-building and framing.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

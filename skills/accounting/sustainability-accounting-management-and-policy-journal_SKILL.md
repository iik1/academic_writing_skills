# Journal Writing Skill: Sustainability Accounting, Management and Policy Journal

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ACCOUNT
- **Publisher:** Emerald Group Publishing
- **Scope:** Multi-disciplinary journal publishing research on sustainability accounting, management, and policy issues relevant to achieving the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals. Bridges academic research and practice/policy implications.
- **Guidelines URL:** http://www.emeraldgrouppublishing.com/products/journals/author_guidelines.htm?id=sampj (accessible via ScholarOne submission system)
- **Field baseline:** account-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 5 articles accessed via IDEAS/RePEc, Emerald publishing, and ResearchGate (2021-2025)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows Emerald Publishing standard template with canonical sections: Introduction, Background/Literature, Methods, Results, Discussion/Conclusion. Subsection depth varies by article type (empirical, analytical, review).

### Length
Not explicitly stated in publicly available guidelines. Sample articles range from approximately 7,000-15,000 words including tables and references. Emerald's general requirement is "concise yet comprehensive."

### Abstract
**ENFORCED - Structured abstract required.** Must include 4-7 sub-headings. Emerald standard headings are:
- **Purpose** — Why the research was undertaken; the problem being addressed
- **Design/Methodology/Approach** — How the research was conducted; methods and data
- **Findings** — Key results and outcomes
- **Practical Implications** — Relevance to practitioners and organizations
- **Social Implications** — Broader impact on sustainable development
- **Originality/Value** — Novel contribution to knowledge

**Word limit:** 250 words maximum for the entire abstract (including all sub-sections and keywords).

### Citations
Follows author-date in-text citation format (consistent with FIELD_SKILL). Sample articles use "(Author Year)" and multiple citations separated by semicolons. Full reference list at end of manuscript.

### Headings
Follows Emerald Publishing conventions. Main headings typically use Arabic numerals (1., 2., 3.) with title case. Subheadings use 1.1, 1.2 format. Exact heading format may vary by journal house style; follow submission template.

### Tables and Figures
Tables and figures placed after references or at manuscript end. Must include clear titles, variable definitions in notes, and explicit reporting of statistical choices (e.g., significance levels, clustering methods, sample sizes). Emerald requires high-quality, publication-ready tables with supporting text describing results in context.

### Explicit Prohibitions
**CRITICAL - SDG Contribution Requirement:** Papers must clearly articulate how they contribute to sustainable development and the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). Papers that do not clearly link their findings to SDG achievement are desk-rejected. Approximately 70% of submissions are desk-rejected at initial screening, primarily for failing to demonstrate SDG relevance. Papers that provide insufficient justification for practice or policy relevance are also desk-rejected.

**Anonymous review:** No author identifying information in manuscript, including in figures or captions, to support double-anonymous peer review.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Sustainable Development Framing (Enforced)
Unlike the FIELD_SKILL baseline (which focuses on hypothesis testing and causal identification), SAMPJ places equal or greater emphasis on demonstrating **practical and policy relevance to sustainable development**. The research question and contribution must frame findings not only as advancing knowledge but as informing organizational practice or policy on sustainability issues. This is a threshold requirement, not a secondary positioning. Articles show evidence of this framing in the introduction and conclusion.

### Scope Orientation (Practice Difference)
While FIELD_SKILL emphasizes archival, experimental, and analytical traditions in accounting research, SAMPJ shows preference for **applied, cross-disciplinary perspectives** that bridge accounting, management, and organizational practice. Articles frequently engage with non-traditional accounting audiences (sustainability managers, corporate governance practitioners, policymakers). The writing assumes a broader readership than a specialist accounting research audience.

### Empirical Methods (Practice Difference)
SAMPJ published empirical articles show strong reliance on **regression-based and meta-analytical approaches** common to FIELD_SKILL, but also visible use of **qualitative and mixed-methods research** (interviews, case studies, document analysis). Quantitative articles follow FIELD_SKILL norms; qualitative articles follow a more discursive tradition similar to AOS papers noted in FIELD_SKILL baseline.

### Results Narration (Practice Only)
Sample articles show frequent use of **explicit links between statistical findings and SDG-relevant implications** within the results section (e.g., "This positive relationship indicates that…companies can enhance SDG reporting by…"). FIELD_SKILL baseline does not require explicit practice framing in results paragraphs; SAMPJ articles make this pattern visible, though not mandated by stated guidelines.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- Voice and person: FIELD DEFAULT. "We" is standard; first-person singular used by single authors; impersonal constructions appear. Formality and register match FIELD_SKILL norms.
- Sentence and paragraph style: FIELD DEFAULT. Complex sentences, dense paragraphs, and high technical density match FIELD_SKILL baseline.
- Argumentation and introduction structure: FIELD DEFAULT. CARS-style introduction structure (establish significance, survey gap, describe study, enumerate results, state contributions, optional roadmap) observed in sample articles. Contributions explicitly stated.
- Methods conventions: FIELD DEFAULT. Past tense, procedural detail, identification strategy, and robustness expected per FIELD_SKILL. Ethical and data statement requirements consistent with baseline.
- Citation and literature engagement: FIELD DEFAULT. Author-date format, citation density by section, paraphrase over quotation (except in qualitative work), diplomatic disagreement framing all match FIELD_SKILL baseline.
- Hedging and epistemic caution: FIELD DEFAULT. Medium hedging calibrated to identification strength; well-identified results stated directly; mechanistic interpretations softened. Limitations acknowledged explicitly per FIELD_SKILL baseline.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Failing to articulate SDG contribution clearly.** The most common desk rejection reason (~70% rate) is insufficient or vague connection to sustainable development goals. Must explicitly state which SDGs are addressed and how findings inform practice or policy. Vague statements like "this matters for sustainability" without specific SDG alignment are insufficient.

2. **Treating SDG relevance as secondary.** Positioning SDG contribution only in the conclusion weakens a submission. SDG framing should appear in the research question or introduction and be sustained throughout as a primary (not afterthought) motivation.

3. **Assuming specialist accounting audience.** Papers that use insider accounting jargon without contextualizing for sustainability practitioners or policymakers may be perceived as narrow. Define terminology when first used; assume interdisciplinary readership.

4. **Abstract structure violations.** Omitting one or more of the required sub-headings (Purpose, Design/Methodology, Findings, Implications, Originality) may trigger desk rejection or reviewer concern. All six headings should be present and populated (even briefly).

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

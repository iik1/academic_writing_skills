# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Policy Modeling

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Elsevier
- **Scope:** The journal serves as a forum for analysis and debate concerning international policy issues, with an emphasis on formal modeling techniques that inform decision-making. The journal focuses on economic, social and political developments that shape the world economy and the policies needed to improve them.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/journal-of-policy-modeling/publish/guide-for-authors (paywalled; details obtained via search results and journal metadata)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 13 (from Volume 48, Issue 1, 2026)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

### Structure
Follows standard empirical/theory paper structure. No journal-specific mandatory sections beyond those in FIELD_SKILL. Typical empirical papers follow: Introduction → Methods/Data → Results → Discussion/Conclusion. No deviations observed from field baseline norms.

### Length
No explicit word or page limit stated in publicly available guidelines. Double-spaced manuscript preferred on 8.5 x 11 inch bond paper, one side only. (FIELD_SKILL does not specify formatting medium.)

### Abstract
**Hard constraint:** Abstract must not exceed 100 words. (FIELD_SKILL does not specify a word limit for abstracts.)

### Citations
Author-year format (parenthetical or in-text): Smith (1977) or (Smith 1977). Matches FIELD_SKILL norm exactly.

### Headings
No specific heading convention (Roman vs Arabic numerals, capitalization style) stated in available guidelines. Inferred from articles: headings are numbered (1, 2, 3, etc.) and follow standard academic conventions. No deviation from FIELD_SKILL.

### Tables and Figures
No explicit rules found in public guidelines. Observed practice in sample articles matches FIELD_SKILL: tables primary vehicle for quantitative results, figures used for illustrating variation and key findings.

### Footnotes
Footnotes indicated by superior numbers, numbered consecutively throughout the article. **Constraint:** Footnotes should be kept to a minimum. Matches FIELD_SKILL guidance (footnotes carry supplementary material, not primary citations).

### Explicit Prohibitions
One editorial emphasis (not framed as prohibition, but as evaluation criterion): **policy applicability is required.** The editor discourages submission of papers exclusively focused on techniques without demonstrating what policy-makers can learn from the model's conclusions. (This is a refinement of FIELD_SKILL's general emphasis on policy implications, not a prohibition.)

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Policy Emphasis and Framing
**Delta: PRACTICE ENFORCED.** FIELD_SKILL notes that top-tier papers draw out policy implications in conclusions. Journal of Policy Modeling elevates this to an evaluation criterion: editors explicitly seek papers where the policy question is central and the model's utility for decision-making is demonstrable. Sample articles (e.g., "Basic income reduces poverty and inequality," "Building a hydrogen economy") prominently foreground policy-relevant questions in titles and introductions. This is more prescriptive than the field baseline.

### Other Dimensions
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (voice, person, sentence/paragraph style, argumentation structure, citation density, hedging, results reporting). No observed deviations.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- Voice and Register: FIELD DEFAULT (first-person plural, active voice, high formality, no contractions)
- Sentence and Paragraph Style: FIELD DEFAULT (medium-to-long sentences, high paragraph density, technical vocabulary)
- Argumentation and Rhetorical Moves: FIELD DEFAULT (introduction announces results; explicit positioning; clear research question)
- Methods Conventions: FIELD DEFAULT (mixed tense; procedural detail sufficient for replication; identification strategy required; robustness checks expected)
- Results and Reporting Norms: FIELD DEFAULT (integration of results and interpretation; tables primary; quantitative specificity; statistical and economic significance)
- Discussion and Conclusion Norms: FIELD DEFAULT (synthesis not summary; limitations acknowledged; future directions brief)
- Citation Format and Density: FIELD DEFAULT (author-year; citation density by section as in FIELD_SKILL)
- Hedging and Epistemic Caution: FIELD DEFAULT (low hedging; hedging only where appropriate; clear claims preferred)

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Technique-focused without policy question:** Submitting a paper that develops a novel econometric method or modeling approach without clearly articulating what a policy-maker should do with the results. The journal will desk-reject or require substantial revision to reframe the paper's policy relevance.

2. **Exceeding 100-word abstract:** Drafting an abstract over 100 words. Journal will require revision to fit the hard constraint.

3. **Insufficient hedging on causal claims:** Although FIELD_SKILL permits clear causal claims when identification is sound, authors in policy-focused journals sometimes overclaim the generalizability of results to different populations or time periods. Explicitly acknowledge scope limitations (e.g., "Our analysis of OECD countries...").

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Technovation

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** INNOV
- **Publisher:** Elsevier
- **Scope:** Interdisciplinary journal covering all facets of technological innovation, from conceptualization of a new technology-based product or process through commercial utilization, including innovation management, entrepreneurship, technology transfer, and innovation policy.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/technovation/publish/guide-for-authors (also archived Author Information Pack, 15 Feb 2018, via elsevier.com/locate/technovation)
- **Field baseline:** innovation-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 7 read in full (abstract-level) plus ~15 additional titles/abstracts surveyed for structural patterns across 2025-2026 issues. Full text was not accessible (ScienceDirect returns 403 without institutional access); assessment is based on guidelines, abstracts, and highlights retrieved via RePEc/IDEAS, SSRN, and search-engine-extracted ScienceDirect snippets. Titles/DOIs: Ylinen & Ranta, "Predicting corporate innovation using machine learning and social media data" (v148, 2025); "Unpacking experimentation in design thinking..." (v141, 2025); "From science management to innovation management" special issue intro (v145, 2025); Opizzi, Loi & Lamine, "Academic spin-off emergence as an organization-embedded dynamic" (v146, 2025); plus title survey of v151-155 (2026) tables of contents.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No strict formatting mandated. Manuscript must contain "essential elements" — Abstract, Keywords, Introduction, Materials and Methods, Results, Conclusions, Artwork and Tables with captions — divided into clearly defined, numbered sections (1, 1.1, 1.1.1, etc.). A combined Results and Discussion section is explicitly endorsed as "often appropriate." A short, separate Conclusions section, divided into specific points, is expected. Discussion should explore significance of results, not repeat them, and should avoid extensive citation/discussion of published literature (i.e., keep literature engagement concentrated earlier in the paper).

### Length
No stated word/page limit for the main text. Abstract: maximum 200 words (tighter than many INNOV journals). Highlights: 3-5 bullet points, maximum 85 characters each including spaces.

### Abstract
Unstructured, single paragraph, max 200 words, stating purpose, principal results, and major conclusions. Must stand alone: avoid references (cite author/year only if essential) and non-standard abbreviations (define at first use if essential). Keywords: max 8, immediately after abstract, avoiding general/plural terms and multi-concept phrases.

### Citations
Author-date in-text citation (Author, Year; Author et al., Year for 3+ authors). No strict reference-list formatting is enforced at submission — "any style as long as consistent" — but the sample style shown is a Harvard/APA-like author-date format. Elsevier applies the final house style at proof stage.

### Headings
Numbered sections and subsections (1, 1.1, 1.1.1); abstract excluded from numbering. Each heading on its own line.

### Tables and Figures
Tables submitted as editable text (not images); sparing use; avoid vertical rules and shading. Figures/tables placed next to relevant text in the manuscript (not bottom/top of file) with captions directly below.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Highlights are mandatory (not optional) — this is a hard requirement not present in the FIELD_SKILL baseline. Double-anonymized (double-blind) peer review: no identifying author information in the main manuscript file; author details go only in a separate title page. Declaration of interest statement mandatory even when there are no interests to declare.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Abstracts sampled use both first-person plural ("we investigate," "we find," "our findings reveal") in empirical papers and third-person constructions in review/conceptual pieces, consistent with the field baseline's description of register shifting by section and paper type.

### Statistical Reporting
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. No journal-specific reporting convention observed beyond field norms.

### Citation Density
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.

### Structural Deviations
Mandatory Highlights (3-5 bullets, ≤85 characters each) is a Technovation-specific requirement not mentioned in FIELD_SKILL, which does not specify highlights at all. ENFORCED (stated in guidelines; standard Elsevier practice for this journal).

The guidelines' explicit endorsement of combining Results and Discussion, and keeping Discussion light on literature re-engagement, is a mild RELAXATION relative to FIELD_SKILL's assumption of typically distinct discussion treatment — but this is boilerplate Elsevier "Guide for Authors" language likely shared across many Elsevier journals rather than a Technovation-specific editorial preference, so treat as GUIDELINE ONLY / low-weight.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Technovation explicitly accepts a broader range of article types than a typical empirical INNOV journal: research reports, general surveys/critical reviews (including "pertinent polemics"), case studies and policy pieces, educational/instructional articles, non-refereed short "Industrial Viewpoints" pieces, and book reviews. Authors should match structure/tone to the declared article type; the polemic/critical-review allowance is a explicit, stated deviation from the uniformly measured, non-polemical tone FIELD_SKILL describes as the default — GUIDELINE ONLY, since spot-checked empirical articles remain non-polemical in practice.

## Common Pitfalls
- Omitting the mandatory Highlights file — this is a hard submission requirement, not optional as in many journals.
- Exceeding the 200-word abstract cap, which is tighter than typical INNOV journal norms (many allow 250-300 words).
- Including author-identifying information in the main manuscript body — the journal requires strict double-anonymized formatting with a separate title page.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

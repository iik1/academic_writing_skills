# Field Writing Style: IB&AREA

## Scope and Intended Use
This baseline describes the writing conventions expected at the top of the
International Business and Area Studies (IB&AREA) field, derived from the
field's ABS 4* and ABS 4 journals. It is meant to be used as a prior for
any journal-specific writing skill in this field: journal-specific skills
should record only what deviates from what is described here, not restate
it. The baseline covers empirical, conceptual/theoretical, and
review/agenda-setting article types, since all three are common at this
level of the field.

## Typical Article Structure
Leading IB&AREA journals expect a front-loaded abstract (roughly
150-250 words) that states the research question, the gap being
addressed, the empirical or theoretical approach, and the core
contribution in compressed form. A keyword list normally follows.

The canonical section order for empirical papers is: introduction,
theory/conceptual background and hypotheses (or propositions), data and
methods, results, discussion, and conclusion (often including
limitations, managerial/policy implications, and future research
directions). Conceptual and theory-building papers typically replace
the data/methods/results sequence with a linear build-up of constructs
and propositions, moving from problem framing through model development
to implications. Systematic literature reviews and research-agenda
pieces (common in this field at the review/editorial level) follow a
PRISMA-style structure: introduction, review methodology (database,
search strings, inclusion/exclusion criteria, screening counts),
thematic results, and a future research agenda.

Near-universal sections: introduction, some form of theoretical
grounding, a discussion/implications section, and a conclusion.
Optional or variable sections: a separate literature review section
(often folded into the introduction or theory section instead),
formal hypotheses (conceptual pieces use propositions or narrative
claims instead), and a dedicated limitations subsection (sometimes
folded into the discussion). Practice varies on whether managerial and
policy implications receive their own subsection or are integrated into
the discussion. No consensus norm exists on this point.

## Voice, Person, and Register
Reviewers at this level expect a formal, scholarly register that remains
accessible to a broad management audience rather than a narrow
methodological specialism. First-person plural ("we argue," "we find,"
"our analysis") is standard and expected when authors state their own
claims, describe their methodological choices, or assert their
contribution. Impersonal, passive constructions remain common and fully
acceptable when summarizing prior literature, describing established
procedures, or reporting results in a detached register. The two
registers coexist within the same article: first person for the
authors' own argumentative moves, impersonal/passive for literature
synthesis and procedural description. Singular "I" appears in
single-authored conceptual or perspective pieces and is treated as
normal in that genre.

## Sentence and Paragraph Style
Sentences are typically medium to long, often compound, reflecting the
field's tolerance for qualified, multi-clause claims (e.g., a claim
followed by its boundary condition in the same sentence). Paragraphs
are topic-sentence-led and cumulative: each paragraph in the theory or
discussion sections typically advances one claim, supports it with
citations, and closes by connecting it to the paper's overall argument.
Conceptual pieces sometimes use short, direct, almost conversational
framing devices ("assume a firm operates in two markets...") to walk
the reader through a stylized argument before returning to formal
register. Numbered or enumerated propositions/statements are an
accepted device for scaffolding cumulative theoretical arguments,
particularly in conceptual and theory-extension papers.

## Argumentation and Rhetorical Moves
Introductions at this level consistently follow a Create-a-Research-Space
(CARS) logic: establish the topic's importance and real-world or
theoretical stakes, narrow to a specific gap or tension in existing
knowledge (often naming specific prior studies or streams as partial but
insufficient), and then state the paper's specific contribution in terms
of what it adds relative to that gap. Gaps are framed variously as
empirical (a relationship or context not yet studied), theoretical (an
assumption or boundary condition not yet examined), or synthetic (two
literatures that have not been connected). At the review/agenda-setting
tier common in this field, the gap is often framed as a disconnect
between scholarly pace and real-world change (e.g., technological or
geopolitical shifts outpacing theory), or as fragmentation across
research streams that have not been integrated. Contribution statements
are explicit and itemized, typically naming two to three discrete
contributions rather than asserting a single diffuse one. Disagreement
with prior work is handled through respectful repositioning rather than
direct refutation: authors typically reframe what a prior claim means or
what question it was really answering, rather than stating that prior
work was wrong.

## Methods Conventions
Empirical methods sections report study design, sample, and data
sources in the past tense, describing what the authors did as completed
action. Procedural and replicability detail is expected to be
substantial: sample construction, variable operationalization, model
specification, and any filtering or screening steps should be
described with enough detail that a reader could reconstruct the
sample. Systematic reviews are expected to report the search database,
search terms, and screening funnel (starting and ending article counts)
explicitly. Robustness checks are standard practice and are typically
reported either in a dedicated subsection or woven into the results
discussion; the level of robustness reporting expected at this tier is
substantial rather than perfunctory. Practice varies on whether
robustness analyses are placed in the main text or relegated to an
appendix/online supplement. No consensus norm exists on this point.

## Results and Reporting Norms
Results are conveyed through a combination of tables/figures and
narrative prose; leading journals expect both, not one in isolation.
Tables present the structured/quantitative record (coefficients,
counts, thematic frequencies); prose is used to interpret, sequence,
and contextualize what the tables show rather than merely restating
numbers. Present tense is standard when reporting what a table "shows"
or what "results indicate," while the study's own procedural actions
remain in past tense. Findings are typically presented first in a
relatively neutral, descriptive register before interpretive claims are
layered on, keeping description and interpretation distinguishable even
when they appear in adjacent sentences.

## Discussion and Conclusion Norms
Discussion sections are expected to loop back explicitly to the gap and
contributions stated in the introduction, restating how the findings
address them. Theoretical implications are typically discussed before
practical/managerial or policy implications. A distinct limitations
statement is expected somewhere in the paper (either as its own
subsection or integrated into the discussion), paired with concrete
future research directions rather than generic calls for "more
research." Conclusions at this level tend to be relatively brief and
avoid introducing new claims or citations not already developed in the
discussion.

## Citation and Literature Engagement
Citation density is high throughout, and especially dense in the
introduction, theoretical background, and discussion sections, where
multiple citations per paragraph is standard. Citation style is
author-date parenthetical. Citations are used overwhelmingly to
paraphrase and synthesize prior findings rather than to quote directly;
direct quotation is rare and reserved for cases where the precise
wording of a definition or theoretical construct matters. Review and
agenda-setting pieces at this tier are expected to cite extensively
across the full breadth of relevant literature (often 100+ references),
using citations both to legitimate claims and to map out research
streams or clusters. Disagreement or contrast between sources is
typically signaled through juxtaposition ("while X finds..., Y
suggests...") rather than explicit critique of individual studies.

## Hedging and Epistemic Caution
Moderate hedging is the norm. Claims about identified patterns, gaps, or
contributions are stated with reasonable confidence ("we show," "results
indicate"), while forward-looking claims, generalizations beyond the
study's scope, and speculative implications are hedged more heavily
("may," "could," "appears to," "suggests"). Authors are expected to
acknowledge the boundary conditions of their claims explicitly rather
than overgeneralizing, particularly regarding context-dependence (e.g.,
country, industry, or institutional setting). Strong, largely unhedged
language is reserved for describing gaps in the literature (e.g., a
relationship is "understudied" or "largely neglected") rather than for
the authors' own substantive claims.

## What This Baseline Does NOT Specify
This baseline does not specify word or page limits, abstract length
requirements, formatting or reference-style mechanics, submission
procedures, or any other publisher-specific requirement — these are
journal-specific and belong in journal-level skills. It also does not
prescribe a single mandatory section structure for all article types,
since empirical, conceptual, and review papers follow different
structural templates within this field, as described above. Where
practice varies without a clear consensus (e.g., placement of robustness
checks, whether implications get a dedicated subsection), this baseline
flags the variation rather than asserting a single norm.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

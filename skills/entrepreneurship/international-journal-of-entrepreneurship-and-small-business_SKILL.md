# Journal Writing Skill: International Journal of Entrepreneurship and Small Business

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ENT-SBM
- **Publisher:** Inderscience Enterprises Ltd
- **ISSN:** 1476-1297
- **Scope:** International, cross-cultural and comparative academic research on entrepreneurs and SMEs, including ethnic enclave entrepreneurship, religious influences, gender issues, informal sector self-employment, and indigenous entrepreneurship
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.inderscience.com/mobile/inauthors/index.php?pid=71
- **Field baseline:** entrepreneurship-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 9 (2024–2025, abstracts and metadata; full texts access-restricted)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Manuscript Length
**5,000–7,000 words maximum** (excluding figures, tables, references, and appendices). This is significantly shorter than many ABS 4*/4 journals and is a hard upper limit.

### Language and Formatting
- English language required; UK spelling and terminology
- Authors responsible for language editing
- Include conflict of interest declaration (even if stating no conflicts)
- AI technology disclosure required in Acknowledgments if ChatGPT or similar tools were used (does not apply to grammar checkers or reference managers)

### Abstract and Keywords
- **Abstract:** Factual, precise, **less than 150 words**. Must briefly cover aim of research, methodologies used, and conclusions. Avoid references unless essential; cite author and year only if unavoidable. Avoid uncommon abbreviations; define at first mention if necessary.
- **Keywords:** Approximately **10–15 words or phrases**. Critical for online discoverability.

### Article Structure and Components
Manuscripts must include (in this order):
1. Title
2. Abstract and keywords
3. Main text
4. Conflict of interest declaration
5. Numbered notes (if any)
6. References
7. Tables and figures with captions

**Exclude from manuscript:** Author names, biographical notes, and acknowledgments (these are handled by the publisher during typesetting).

### Citation Style
Harvard (author-date) system. Format: "Author (Year)" in text; full alphabetical reference list at end. Each reference includes author names, publication date, article/book title, publisher details, page numbers, and DOI when available.

### Tables and Figures
- Provide captions with each table and figure
- Figures and tables are in addition to word count, not counted within it
- SI units for all measurements

### Submission Requirements
- Original work not previously published or under consideration elsewhere
- Manuscript submitted via indersciencesubmissions.com
- Double-blind peer review
- No charges for standard publication (Open Access option available at cost)

### Explicit Prohibitions
**Briefs, research notes, and case studies are NOT published in this journal.** Full research articles only. Submissions must be original empirical studies, theoretical frameworks, systematic reviews, or literature reviews—not abbreviated or preliminary research reports.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Acceptable Article Types
IJESB explicitly publishes systematic reviews and literature reviews as full-length articles, not as supplementary content. Recent 2024 articles include prominent systematic reviews (e.g., artisan entrepreneurship, entrepreneurship and poverty in developed countries). FIELD_SKILL emphasizes hypothesis-driven empirical work and conceptual frameworks but does not foreground systematic reviews as a core publication type at the ABS 4*/4 level. **This journal elevates systematic reviews and meta-analyses as primary article types.** [PRACTICE / lower-tier relaxation]

### Qualitative and Mixed-Method Studies
IJESB publishes qualitative empirical studies as full-length articles (e.g., "Employee entrepreneurial behaviour in SME service development: a qualitative study on effectuation," 2024). FIELD_SKILL baseline is derived from ABS 4*/4 journals which, while accepting qualitative work, disproportionately feature quantitative empirical designs. At ABS 2, IJESB demonstrates a more balanced acceptance of qualitative and mixed-method designs alongside quantitative work. [FIELD DEFAULT with relaxation note: qualitative designs carry equal weight]

### Word Limit: Lower Threshold
**5,000–7,000 words is a strict upper limit**, typically 1,000–2,000 words shorter than comparable ABS 4 journals (e.g., Entrepreneurship Theory and Practice). This enforces tighter framing and more selective literature engagement. FIELD_SKILL does not specify word limits and assumes the flexibility typical of top-tier journals. [ENFORCED relaxation]

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Exceeding the 7,000-word limit.** Many authors submitting from higher-tier journals underestimate how much compression is required. Aim for 6,500 words or less to allow for revision cycles; this is a hard limit enforced at desk review.

2. **Briefs, preliminary findings, or case studies.** The explicit prohibition on briefs and research notes is strictly enforced. Single-organization case studies, unless embedded in a comparative or systematic framework, are desk-rejected. Ensure your work constitutes a full empirical study, systematic review, or theoretical framework.

3. **Omitting conflict of interest and AI disclosure statements.** These are mandatory, even if the answer is "none." Missing them triggers revision requests during review.

4. **Vague or oversized keywords.** Keywords like "entrepreneurship," "SMEs," and "small business" are too generic. Use 10–15 specific, varied keywords (e.g., "ethnic enclave entrepreneurship," "financial barriers," "immigrant family businesses") to ensure discoverability and fit.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

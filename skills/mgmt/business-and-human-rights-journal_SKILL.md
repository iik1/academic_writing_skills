# Journal Writing Skill: Business and Human Rights Journal

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN
- **Publisher:** Cambridge University Press
- **Scope:** An interdisciplinary, open platform for scholarly debate at the intersection of business and human rights, environmental rights, labour rights, and the collective rights of vulnerable groups. Welcomes theoretical, empirical, and policy/reform-oriented work from academics and practitioners (including judges and civil society actors) across all global regions and disciplines.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/business-and-human-rights-journal/information/author-instructions/preparing-your-materials (see also the journal's separate "Style and Citation Guide," referenced but not separately fetchable)
- **Field baseline:** mgmt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 5 full front-matter reviews (abstracts, keywords, section headings, citation style) plus scan of ~8 additional titles/abstracts across 2024–2025 issues for convergence check: "Corporate Power over Human Rights: An Analytical Framework"; "Human Rights Due Diligence Within the Arms Industry: Challenges, Practices and Opportunities"; "Indigenous Peoples, Business, and the Struggles for Justice in the Green Transition"; "Advancing the Humanitarian Imperative of the Arms Trade Treaty"; "Company Responses to Human Rights Reports: An Empirical Analysis." Sample skews toward legal/doctrinal pieces because that is the dominant house style found; a minority of empirical/quantitative BHR articles exist but were not independently verified to differ in citation mechanics.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No fixed IMRaD or Theory/Hypotheses template is mandated or observed. Section headings are chosen freely and named for the substantive legal/policy argument (e.g., "The UNGPs and the Arms Sector," "A Shifting Legal and Policy Landscape"), not generic labels like "Methods" or "Results." This is a much looser structural regime than FIELD_SKILL's four templates.

### Length
Scholarly Articles: 9,000–12,000 words including footnotes. Developments in the Field (DiF): 2,000–3,000 words. Book Reviews: 1,000–1,500 words. FIELD_SKILL's 10,000–18,000-word norms are roughly consistent for full articles but BHRJ's ceiling is tighter (12,000 vs. up to 18,000) and footnotes count toward the limit.

### Abstract
No structured-abstract or explicit word-limit requirement found in guidelines; abstracts observed are single unstructured paragraphs, unstructured, no citations — consistent with FIELD_SKILL default.

### Citations
Footnote-based citation system (numbered, legal-style), not APA author-date. Every article spot-checked used numbered superscript footnotes with full bibliographic detail in the footnote itself, not a References list keyed to in-text (Author, Year) citations. This directly overrides FIELD_SKILL's APA in-text convention.

### Headings
No mandated heading scheme; multi-level lettered/numbered subheadings (e.g., "A. Fosen," "B. Turkana") are common in legal-argument sections, unlike FIELD_SKILL's Theory/Methods/Results labels.

### Tables and Figures
All illustrative material (tables, figures, diagrams, graphs, maps, boxes, code snippets) must be labeled "Figures," numbered consecutively in Arabic numerals, cited in text, and captioned. Must meet WCAG 2.1 Level AA accessibility (colour/contrast) standards for images and tables.

### Explicit Prohibitions
AI tools cannot be listed as authors; AI-generated content must comply with plagiarism policy. Third-party ideas, words, or data require acknowledgment.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Citation Density and Mechanics
Footnote/legal-citation style dominates, not APA parenthetical citation as FIELD_SKILL assumes. Footnotes are used for the primary citation apparatus (not merely tangential asides as in FIELD_SKILL), consistent with the journal's legal-scholarship lean. ENFORCED (per Style and Citation Guide, confirmed across all articles checked).

### Structural Deviations
No hypothesis/proposition apparatus (H1, H2… or P1, P2…) appears in the sample checked; argument sections are structured as sequential legal/policy analysis (context → legal instruments → case analysis → implications) rather than theory-building toward numbered predictions. FIELD_SKILL's Theory-and-Hypotheses or Propositions templates do not apply to the dominant doctrinal-legal article type at this journal. PRACTICE ONLY / dominant pattern in sample — a minority of quantitative empirical articles (e.g., regression-based studies of corporate human rights disclosure) likely follow closer to FIELD_SKILL norms, but none were verified in full text here.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Case-study subheadings using lettered sub-lists (A., B., C.) for named legal cases or jurisdictions are a recurring structural device not present in FIELD_SKILL. PRACTICE ONLY.

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting with APA author-date in-text citations instead of the required footnote system — this is the single most consequential formatting mismatch for authors coming from management/social-science training.
- Imposing a rigid Theory-and-Hypotheses or Methods/Results template on a doctrinal-legal or policy-analysis submission; BHRJ accepts freely-named argument sections and this journal does not expect numbered hypotheses for non-empirical pieces.
- Exceeding the 12,000-word ceiling by not counting footnotes toward the limit (footnotes are included in the word count here, unlike some management journals).

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

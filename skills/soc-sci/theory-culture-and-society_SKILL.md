# Journal Writing Skill: Theory, Culture and Society

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** SOC SCI
- **Publisher:** SAGE (based at Goldsmiths, University of London)
- **Scope:** Publishes theoretical and critical-cultural analysis at the intersection of social theory, cultural studies, and philosophy; ranked #1 in Cultural Studies (2024). Predominantly conceptual/essayistic rather than empirical-quantitative.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/author-instructions/tcs (full PDF retrieved)
- **Field baseline:** soc-sci-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 7 (Kinsella "Male Subcultures in Japan" 2025; Luo, Xing & Xiao "Digital Sensing, Infrastructure of Sovereignty..." 2026; Nirta "Trans Embodiment: Notes on Building a House You Don't Want to Live In" 2026; Manghani "Painterly: Editorial Introduction for Painting/Knowledge E-Special Issue" 2025; Brennan "Necropolitics and Surplus Life: Mbembe and Beyond" 2024; Serafini "From Institutions to the Platform Society..." 2024; D'Cruz "What Demarcates Necropolitics from Biopolitics?" 2026)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Standard Articles are blind-refereed. Review Articles (invited/commissioned only — TCS does **not** accept unsolicited review articles), Notes & Commentary pieces, Interviews, and Global Public Life pieces are typically not blind-refereed and are handled by editorial board members/associate editors instead. No fixed IMRaD template is prescribed; manuscript begins with title, 150-word abstract, and 3-7 keywords, followed by the body text organized into thematic/conceptual sections rather than mandated section labels.

### Length
- Standard Articles: 6,000-8,000 words (including all notes and references)
- Notes & Commentary: 4,000-6,000 words
- Interviews: 3,000-8,000 words
- Global Public Life: 4,000-6,000 words
- Review Articles: 4,000-6,000 words (invited only)

### Abstract
Exactly 150 words, plus 3-7 keywords (selectable from TCS's keyword list or freely entered). Abstract, title, and keywords must also be entered separately into the submission system in addition to appearing in the manuscript.

### Citations
SAGE Harvard style, with one journal-specific exception: **reference list entries use full first names, not initials** (e.g., "Featherstone, Mike (2007)" not "Featherstone, M."). Up to three authors listed in-text before et al. is used (fewer than three followed by et al. is also acceptable). Do not use ibid., op. cit., infra., supra — repeat the citation in full each time. Journal titles are never abbreviated. Institutional/authorless citations use the institution or journal name as the citing entity.

### Headings
No prescribed heading scheme; sections in sampled articles are typically short, thematically named (e.g., "Going Home," "Building Archives," "Anywhere but Home") rather than standard "Introduction/Methods/Results" labels.

### Tables and Figures
Notes (not footnotes — footnotes are **not accepted**) must be numbered serially and placed as endnotes before the reference list, and their number/length "should be strictly limited." Images/figures encouraged, 300 dpi minimum; two versions required at acceptance (black-and-white for print, colour for online). Figures must be numbered and their placement indicated in text.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Blind-review anonymization: no author name anywhere in the main document (including running heads); self-citations replaced with "Author" (e.g., "as 'Author' (2005) has demonstrated"); do not black out or redact — omit entirely.
- Minimize acronyms; avoid inventing new ones or using ones known only to specialists — restrict to acronyms familiar to a general public (e.g., UN).
- AI chatbots (e.g., ChatGPT) may not be listed as authors.
- No footnotes (endnotes only).
- Previously published material generally not accepted; copies/similar versions must not be posted online while under review.
- Author-suggested/recommended peer reviewers are not permitted.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Voice and Register
RELAXATION/DELTA (PRACTICE ONLY, consistent across sample): FIELD_SKILL describes a field split between high-formality quantitative work and a "moderately more accessible or essayistic register" permitted in critical human geography/some sociology. TCS sits at the far essayistic end of that range across virtually the whole sample: single-authored, densely theoretical, essay-style prose that reads as sustained conceptual argument rather than empirical reporting (e.g., "offers a conceptual reading of the trans body as an archive," "theorizes the micropolitics of sovereignty ... elaborating a technopolitical rearticulation"). First-person plural "we" is largely absent even in multi-authored pieces; third-person analytical exposition dominates.

### Statistical Reporting
Not applicable — sampled articles are non-quantitative critical-theory essays; no statistical reporting observed. FIELD_SKILL's quantitative-reporting norms do not apply to the typical TCS submission.

### Citation Density
PRACTICE ONLY: engagement with prior literature is concentrated on a small number of named theorists treated in depth (Mbembe, Esposito, Bauman, Foucault, Bourdieu) rather than broad citation clusters surveying an empirical literature. This substitutes for, rather than supplements, a conventional "Literature Review" section — the sustained close reading of one or two thinkers' work often *is* the paper's argument, not a preface to it.

### Structural Deviations
DELTA (ENFORCED via guidelines + confirmed in sample): No Data/Methods/Results/Discussion architecture. Articles are organized into a small number of freely-titled conceptual sections that track the stages of an argument (e.g., "Going Home" / "Building Archives" / "Anywhere but Home"), consistent with FIELD_SKILL's acknowledgment that qualitative/theoretical work may depart from canonical section labels — but here the departure is the norm rather than the exception.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
- Titles frequently use a colon-separated evocative/interpretive main title plus a descriptive subtitle (e.g., "Trans Embodiment: Notes on Building a House You Don't Want to Live In"; "Painterly: Editorial Introduction for Painting/Knowledge E-Special Issue").
- The journal actively solicits non-standard formats alongside standard articles: Notes & Commentary, Interviews with prominent scholars, a "Global Public Life" section with explicitly relaxed topical/methodological scope, and video-abstracts (3-5 min) as a supplementary self-promotion channel for the companion website theoryculturesociety.org.

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting an unsolicited review article — TCS only accepts these by invitation; unsolicited review articles will be rejected outright regardless of quality.
- Using initials instead of full first names in the reference list — this is TCS's one deviation from standard SAGE Harvard and is easy to miss if reusing a template from another SAGE journal.
- Including footnotes — TCS requires endnotes only, strictly limited in number and length.
- Failing to anonymize self-citations in the reference list itself (not just the body text) before blind review.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

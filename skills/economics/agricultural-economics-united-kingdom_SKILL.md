# Journal Writing Skill: Agricultural Economics (United Kingdom)

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Wiley (on behalf of International Association of Agricultural Economists)
- **ISSN:** 1574-0862
- **Scope:** Covers agricultural economics in its broadest sense—from food consumption and nutrition to land use and the environment at every scale from households to markets and the macroeconomy.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/page/journal/15740862/homepage/forauthors.html
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 7 (empirical and review articles, 2024–2025)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows field baseline structure (Introduction → Data/Methods → Results → Discussion/Conclusion → Appendix) with notable flexibility. **Free Format Submission** is explicitly offered, simplifying the process. Review papers and editorial pieces may deviate substantially.

### Length
**Maximum 10,000 words** (word-equivalents, including abstract, figures, tables, and graphs). Notably tighter than ABS 4*/4 baseline which permits longer manuscripts.

### Abstract
No explicit guidance on structure (unstructured or structured is both acceptable). No stated word limit in public guidelines.

### Manuscript Format
- Submit via Wiley Authors platform
- Word (.doc/.docx) or LaTeX (.tex) format accepted
- Anonymized PDF generated automatically at submission

### Citation and References
Author-year format (parenthetical or in-text) consistent with FIELD_SKILL baseline. Numbered footnote references not standard. Complete Chicago Manual of Style author-date system expected (where traditional agricultural economics journals often specify this explicitly).

### Headings
Follows field baseline. No stated convention preferring Roman or Arabic numerals; standard academic practice applies.

### Tables and Figures
- Must be numbered
- Integrated into text where appropriate
- Title above tables, below figures
- Notes and legends as needed
- Summary statistics tables are standard (following field baseline)

### Data and Reproducibility
**Strongly recommended:** Provide replication data and code when submitting. This is optional in language but strongly signaled as best practice to facilitate peer review.

### AI Technology Disclosure
**Mandatory.** Authors must disclose any use of AI Technologies upon submission, documented at the beginning of the manuscript after the abstract and before the introduction. Journal uses AI detection software in screening. **Positive detection without disclosure triggers desk rejection.** This is a hard gate not present in FIELD_SKILL baseline and specific to this journal.

### Ethics and Conflicts of Interest
Manuscript must include:
- Details of IRB approvals (if applicable)
- Ethical treatment of human and animal research participants
- Informed consent gathering (where applicable)
- Conflicts of interest declaration (or statement that none exist)

These are standard social science ethics; FIELD_SKILL baseline does not emphasize this explicitly.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Manuscript Length Ceiling
**ENFORCED.** The 10,000-word ceiling is a hard constraint significantly tighter than ABS 4*/4 baseline norms. This forces greater economy and may affect depth of literature review, methods description, and robustness checks compared to top-tier outlets.

### Voice and Register
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. First-person plural ("we find," "we develop") is standard in empirical work, consistent with field baseline. No deviation observed.

### Citation Density
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Introduction-heavy citation pattern observed in reviewed articles, methods-section citations sparse for standard procedures, results discussion integrates comparisons with prior magnitudes. No journal-specific delta.

### Statistical Reporting
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Panel regression, instrumental-variable, and SFA estimators are reported with standard notation. Robustness checks (IV-GMM, fixed-effects, alternative samples) are routinely deployed. No deviation from field baseline.

### Structural Deviations
**RELAXATION.** Free Format Submission explicitly permitted. This lowers formatting friction compared to rigid top-tier style guides, but does not alter substance. Review papers and editorials may omit standard sections (e.g., a review paper may lack "Data" and "Results").

### AI Transparency and Disclosure Requirement
**ENFORCED (Journal-Specific).** Mandatory AI disclosure after abstract, before introduction, with machine-detection screening. This is not a FIELD_SKILL baseline norm and reflects evolving journal policy (post-2023) specific to Wiley. Desk rejection on detection without disclosure is explicit. Authors working with LLMs or other generative tools must flag this proactively.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- **Argumentation and Rhetorical Moves:** FIELD_SKILL norm (results announced in introduction, contributions explicit, gap framing as theoretical insufficiency or data gap, robustness as standard)
- **Methods Tense Convention:** FIELD_SKILL norm (mixed tense: past for procedures, present for model properties and identification logic)
- **Conclusion Length and Content:** FIELD_SKILL norm (short, no new results, policy/theoretical implications, limitations, future directions)
- **Paraphrase vs. Direct Quotation:** FIELD_SKILL norm (direct quotation rare, paraphrase with attribution standard)
- **Hedging Intensity:** FIELD_SKILL norm (low, defended by identification and robustness, not by linguistic diffidence)
- **Null Results Handling:** FIELD_SKILL norm (reported directly as results when well-powered and identification is strong)

## Common Pitfalls
1. **AI Use Without Disclosure.** Machine-generated text detected without explicit declaration triggers immediate desk rejection. Transparency is non-negotiable.
2. **Exceeding 10,000-Word Limit.** The word ceiling is firm. Tight editing is required; appendices do not reduce the main-text count. Plan for economy in literature review and methods sections.
3. **Missing Replication Data.** While optional, absence of replication code and data weakens review chances in a field increasingly expecting transparency. Provide it.
4. **Incomplete Ethical Attribution.** IRB approvals, informed consent, and conflict-of-interest statements must be explicit. Reviewers check these routinely; omission signals carelessness.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Annals of Tourism Research

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 4
- **Field:** Tourism, Transport and Hospitality
- **Publisher:** Elsevier
- **Scope:** Broad social-scientific study of tourism, including theoretical, empirical, qualitative, and conceptual work; one of the field's most generalist and prestigious outlets
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/annals-of-tourism-research/publish/guide-for-authors
- **Field baseline:** tourism-transport-hospitality-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 3 (Vol. 114, Sep 2025: S0160738325001161 — Harrill et al. "Music films and videos as recolonizing media"; S0160738325001008 — Zhang "Creating marginality, building resilience"; S0160738325001173 — Kim et al. "Political ideology and variety seeking")

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm for full research articles. The journal additionally defines three short-format article types with distinct structural expectations (see Length below).

### Length
All word counts are **inclusive of tables, figures, and references** — an unusually strict inclusive counting rule:

| Format | Word Limit | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Full research article | 6,000–10,000 words | Inclusive of tables, figures, references |
| Research Note | ≤ 3,000 words | Preliminary findings, methodological developments |
| Commentary | ≤ 3,000 words | Response to a recently published article; deep expertise required |
| Viewpoint | ≤ 1,000 words | Opinion on current scholarship; **requires advance editor-in-chief approval before submission** |

**Label: ENFORCED**

### Abstract
Guidelines do not specify a word limit or required format. In practice, the abstract is an unstructured single paragraph. Highlights (4–5 bullet points) and a Graphical abstract appear as standard Elsevier submission components alongside, not within, the abstract text.

**Abstract text: PRACTICE ONLY (unstructured single paragraph)**
**Highlights: ENFORCED (required Elsevier submission element, 4–5 bullets observed consistently)**
**Graphical abstract: ENFORCED (present in all articles accessed; listed as a submission section in guidelines)**

### Citations
Guidelines do not state citation style explicitly. Author–year (APA) is the observed format consistent with field norms.

**Label: FIELD DEFAULT**

### Headings
Guidelines do not specify heading hierarchy or numbering. No heading-specific deviations identified.

**Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.**

### Tables and Figures
Tables and figures count toward the word limit. The guidelines recommend expressing key findings graphically where possible.

**Label: ENFORCED**

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Work simultaneously under review elsewhere may not be submitted.
- Previously published work is excluded except preprints, abstracts, lectures, theses, or registered reports.
- Generative AI tools may not be listed as authors or co-authors and may not be cited as sources.
- Authorship changes are not permitted after acceptance.
- A Generative AI Use Declaration is required at the end of the manuscript if AI was used in preparation. **Label: ENFORCED**

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

> Only dimensions where this journal deviates from the field baseline.

### Structural Deviations

**Graphical abstract and Highlights are standard submission elements.** The FIELD_SKILL baseline notes Highlights as "optional but common" in Elsevier journals. At ATR, both Highlights (4–5 bullets) and a Graphical abstract are present in all published articles examined and are treated as required components, not optional. Authors should prepare these as integral parts of the submission rather than additions.

**Label: ENFORCED**

**Viewpoints require advance editor-in-chief approval** before a manuscript can be submitted. This pre-submission gate has no parallel in the FIELD_SKILL baseline and distinguishes ATR from journals that accept opinion-format pieces through standard submission.

**Label: ENFORCED (Guideline only for the pre-approval process; the viewpoint format itself is FIELD DEFAULT)**

### Length Constraint

The inclusive word count (tables + figures + references counted toward the 6,000–10,000 ceiling) is a tighter constraint than implied by FIELD_SKILL, which does not address word limits. A full-length empirical paper with three tables and a reference list of 70–80 items will consume roughly 1,500–2,500 words of the budget on non-prose elements, leaving approximately 4,000–8,000 words of running text. Authors must budget accordingly.

**Label: ENFORCED**

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Over-length submissions.** Authors accustomed to journals with 8,000–10,000-word limits on prose alone may inadvertently exceed ATR's ceiling when tables and references are counted in. Budget the word count before drafting, not after.

2. **Submitting a Viewpoint without prior editor approval.** The pre-submission approval requirement for Viewpoints is easy to overlook; submitting without it will result in desk rejection.

3. **Missing or underprepared Highlights and Graphical abstract.** Both are required Elsevier submission elements. Highlights should be 4–5 standalone declarative sentences summarising the paper's contributions; the Graphical abstract should communicate the study visually. Treating these as afterthoughts risks incomplete submissions.

4. **Inadequate multidisciplinary accessibility.** ATR explicitly requires that articles be written for a multidisciplinary audience. Specialist jargon that is routine in a sub-field (e.g., psychometric terminology in an experimental paper, remote sensing nomenclature in a geospatial paper) needs plain-language explanation or replacement.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

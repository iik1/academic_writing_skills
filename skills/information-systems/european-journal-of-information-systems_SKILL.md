# Journal Writing Skill: European Journal of Information Systems

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 4
- **Field:** INFO MAN
- **Publisher:** Taylor & Francis (formerly Palgrave Macmillan)
- **Scope:** A European-rooted IS journal publishing critical, theoretically-grounded
  research on information technology's development, implementation, management,
  and effects, explicitly welcoming a broader diversity of research genres and
  theoretical/methodological traditions (including pure theory and interpretive
  work) than some North American-centric IS journals.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.tandfonline.com/journals/tjis20 (Instructions for
  Authors page returned 403/inaccessible directly; guidance below reconstructed
  from the journal's own published editorials on genre and word limits, which are
  the primary public statements of policy)
- **Field baseline:** information-management-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 10 with abstract/detail-level access, 14 total titles
  reviewed (2025-2026, Vol 34-35): "Measuring the success of social information
  systems" (DeLone, McLean et al.); "Modularisation and the management of IT
  architecture complexity" (van den Hooff et al.); "Fostering access to justice"
  (Safari & Techatassanasoontorn); "Embracing contrarian thinking" (Zimmer,
  Vassilakopoulou, Grisot, Niemimaa); "Cryptocurrency frames of reference"
  (Schlagwein, Gozman, Manusu); "Between Proactive and Reactive Coping" (Weber,
  Remus, Geiger, Cram); "A framework for value sensitive design in socio-technical
  digital ecosystems" (Stahl); plus abstracts/titles for "A multi-layered
  perspective on algorithmic control systems," "Mobile ICT outages and public
  safety," "Leveraging digital alliances for green innovations," "Phishing
  detection in multitasking contexts," and others. Full text was not accessible
  (paywalled); assessment relies on abstracts, editorial/genre-policy articles,
  and front matter.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No single fixed section template is mandated. EJIS instead requires authors to
declare a manuscript **genre** at submission (under "Section" in the submission
system), and expected structure varies by genre:
- **Literature Review** — synthesizes prior knowledge on a topic/domain,
  identifies biases and gaps, proposes future research directions.
- **Theory Development** — pure theory papers are explicitly accepted (unusual
  relative to more empiricism-favoring IS journals).
- **Empirical Research** — the catch-all for qualitative, quantitative, and
  design-science work with a theoretical contribution; follows the conventional
  IS empirical envelope described in FIELD_SKILL.
- **Ethnographies and Narratives** — a distinct genre for work focused on lived
  practice, intentions, and situated action, separate from "Empirical Research."
- **Response** — short pieces responding to a previously published EJIS paper
  (including "Issues and Opinion" pieces).
This genre taxonomy is a journal-specific structural axis not present in
FIELD_SKILL, which assumes a single generic empirical/design-science envelope.

### Length
Main text should not exceed **10,000 words** for first submission (as of the
2022 policy update, which raised the prior 5,000-8,000 word guidance). This
limit includes tables and figures placed in-text but excludes references and
appendices.

### Abstract
No structured-abstract requirement was found; abstracts observed in the sample
are unstructured single paragraphs, consistent with FIELD_SKILL. No specific
word cap confirmed from primary guideline text (general T&F guidance elsewhere
cites 300 words as a common ceiling, but this was not confirmed as EJIS-specific).

### Citations
Author-date in-text format, e.g., (Author, Year); full reference list follows
an EJIS-specific variant of author-date style (not standard APA/Harvard verbatim
but structurally similar — author, year, title, journal, volume/issue, pages,
DOI). No delta from FIELD_SKILL's implicit citation norms beyond this being
author-date rather than numbered.

### Headings
No journal-specific heading scheme beyond genre-dependent structure noted above.

### Tables and Figures
Tables/figures for first submission should be embedded in-text at their intended
location (not collected at the end or in a separate file) — a explicit
formatting instruction not addressed in FIELD_SKILL.

### Explicit Prohibitions
None identified beyond standard T&F originality/ethics policies.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
EJIS's explicit multi-genre submission system (Literature Review, Theory
Development, Empirical Research, Ethnographies and Narratives, Response) is a
journal-specific structural feature not present in FIELD_SKILL, which assumes
one generic macro-structure. This is ENFORCED (stated in submission system) —
practically, sampled empirical articles still follow the FIELD_SKILL empirical
envelope, but the journal's willingness to publish pure theory papers and
ethnography/narrative pieces as first-class genres, each with different
structural expectations, is a genuine deviation worth noting when the target
piece is not a standard empirical paper. Classification: ENFORCED.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
EJIS shows a distinct editorial appetite for reflexive/critical framing —
recent special-issue and regular content includes explicit "value-reflexive,"
critique-of-assumptions framing (e.g., "Embracing contrarian thinking:
value-reflexive research for a better world") and papers engaging directly with
worker precarity, power, and societal effects of IT (algorithmic management of
gig workers, digital exclusion, access to justice) rather than purely
managerial/firm-performance framing. This is a PRACTICE ONLY / editorial-culture
observation (visible across several sampled abstracts, not a stated structural
rule) — it should inform motivation/framing choices (a slightly stronger
critical/societal angle is welcome) but does not change section structure or
reporting norms. Classification: PRACTICE ONLY, low-to-moderate prevalence
(observed in at least 4 of 10 detailed abstracts).

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting without declaring/considering manuscript genre — a paper framed as
  generic "empirical research" when it is really an ethnography or a theory
  paper may be misaligned with reviewer expectations for that genre.
- Assuming a fixed hypothesis-testing empirical template is mandatory; EJIS is
  more genre-pluralistic than some peer journals, and pure theory or narrative
  submissions are legitimate, not exceptions requiring special justification.
- Placing tables/figures at the end of the manuscript rather than in-text at
  first submission, contrary to the journal's explicit formatting instruction.
- Framing contributions in purely firm-performance/managerial terms without
  engaging the journal's visible appetite for critical, societal, and
  worker/stakeholder-level implications.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Entrepreneurship, Management and Innovation

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ENT-SBM
- **Publisher:** Cognitione Foundation for the Dissemination of Knowledge and Science
- **Scope:** Interdisciplinary peer-reviewed journal publishing empirical and conceptual research on entrepreneurship, management, and innovation; serves both academic and practitioner audiences
- **Guidelines URL:** https://jemi.edu.pl/submission-and-policy
- **Field baseline:** entrepreneurship-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 5 (Urban & Govender 2024; Czernek-Marszałek et al. 2026; Ha et al. 2025; Dryglas et al. 2024; one additional from Vol. 22 Issue 2, 2026)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
**Required sections** (in order): Title, Introduction, Literature Review [or Theoretical Background], Methodology, Results, Discussion, Conclusion. References and Appendices follow. No subsection naming is mandated beyond these primary divisions, though observed articles frequently employ subsections within Literature Review (e.g., "Resilience Literature," "Organizational Innovativeness") and Discussion (e.g., "Theoretical Contributions," "Practical Implications," "Limitations").

### Length
- **Main text:** 8,000–12,000 words (excluding references, tables, figures, appendices)
- **Abstract:** 250–350 words
- **Keywords:** 5–10 terms required

### Abstract
Must employ a **structured, mandatory format** with five distinct labeled sections:
1. **PURPOSE:** Research aim/questions and identified gaps
2. **METHODOLOGY:** Sample size, design, analytical approach
3. **FINDINGS:** Key results summarized concisely
4. **IMPLICATIONS:** Contributions to theory and practice
5. **ORIGINALITY/VALUE:** Novelty and significance of the work

This structure is universal across observed articles and explicitly required in guidelines.

### Citations
- **Style:** APA 7th edition
- **DOI requirement:** Mandatory for all sources where available; format as `https://doi.org/[number]`
- **In-text format:** Author-date parenthetical (e.g., Smith & Jones, 2023)
- **Multiple references:** Bundle together with semicolon separators

### Headings
**No rigid heading conventions specified** beyond standard academic hierarchy. Observed articles use 2–3 heading levels. Primary sections (Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion) are unnumbered; subsections frequently use Arabic numerals or are left unnumbered. Capitalization follows standard title case. No explicit restrictions on heading depth or styling.

### Tables and Figures
Tables and figures must be "integrated into the manuscript near first citation, numbered consecutively, titled clearly, and accompanied by source notes." Captions appear above tables and below figures. All tables include source attribution. Figures depicting mediation paths, interaction effects, and results visualizations are common and treated as essential to results presentation, not supplementary.

### Keywords
5–10 keywords required, reflecting the paper's core themes and concepts. No prescribed format beyond distinct, searchable terms.

### Additional Required Metadata
- **JEL Classification codes:** Required "where relevant"
- **Data Availability Statement:** Mandatory (specify where data can be accessed or state if proprietary/restricted)
- **Code Availability Statement:** Required if computational methods are material to reproducibility
- **AI Tool Disclosure:** Mandatory declaration of any AI tools used in manuscript preparation

### Explicit Prohibitions
- No multi-column manuscript layout
- No author/institution identifying information in blinded manuscript file (double-anonymous review)
- No page numbers in initial submission (added during production)

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Person
**RELAXATION of FIELD_SKILL baseline.** The field baseline expects "first-person plural voice ('we test,' 'we argue,' 'we find') throughout, including in the theory and hypothesis sections." JEMI articles deviate systematically: they employ **predominantly third-person passive and impersonal constructions** across all sections. 

Representative examples:
- "data were collected using CAWI" (passive)
- "the analysis reveals" (impersonal active)
- "the findings demonstrate" (impersonal active)
- "our findings suggest" (mixed: first-person plural only in main claims, not throughout)

**First-person plural ("we") appears selectively**—mainly to describe authorial methodological choices ("we recruited," "we employed") or key claims ("we hypothesize")—but does not dominate across theory and results as the field baseline prescribes. This is a consistent PRACTICE VARIATION across all examined articles and represents a lower-tier journal relaxation of the field norm. Authors should expect that passive and third-person voice is acceptable and standard at JEMI, not a stylistic deviation.

### Abstract Structure
**ENFORCED journal-specific requirement with no field baseline precedent.** The mandatory five-part structured abstract (PURPOSE|METHODOLOGY|FINDINGS|IMPLICATIONS|ORIGINALITY) is not mentioned in FIELD_SKILL and constitutes a hard constraint unique to this journal. Every observed article adheres to this format with explicit section labels or clear demarcations. Abstracts consistently run 250–350 words, filling all five sections substantively. This format must be followed in full.

### Citation Density and Style
**ENFORCED guideline.** FIELD_SKILL does not mandate a specific citation format; JEMI specifies APA 7th edition with mandatory DOI inclusion. DOI formatting as clickable hyperlinks (`https://doi.org/...`) is standard in all recent articles. Otherwise, citation density patterns (high in introduction and theory, lower in methods and results, moderate in discussion) align with FIELD_SKILL and show no deviation.

### Multi-Study Designs
**PRACTICE ONLY / LOWER PREVALENCE.** FIELD_SKILL describes multi-study designs as "well-established" and "a standard option" in entrepreneurship research. In the spot-checked JEMI sample (n=5 articles), all five employed single-study designs. No multi-study papers were observed. This is consistent with ABS 1 journals being less likely to enforce the multi-study convention that dominates ABS 4* and 4 venues. Authors should not assume multi-study designs are expected; single-study empirical designs are the norm at JEMI.

### Methodological Disclosure and Ethics
Follows FIELD_SKILL baseline. Detailed procedural transparency, construct measurement reporting, and explicit ethical protections (e.g., Privacy Act compliance noted in one article) are consistent with field expectations. No delta observed.

## Common Pitfalls (Journal-Specific)

1. **Forgetting the structured abstract format.** The five-part abstract (PURPOSE|METHODOLOGY|FINDINGS|IMPLICATIONS|ORIGINALITY) is non-negotiable. Check that each section is present and clearly delineated; unsure papers risk desk rejection for formal non-compliance.

2. **Omitting DOI or citing sources without DOI lookups.** Every reference must include a DOI where available. Cross-reference via Crossref if uncertain; blank DOI fields will trigger editorial rejection.

3. **Exceeding 12,000 main-text words or falling below 8,000.** Word count is strictly enforced. Pre-submission, verify your manuscript length excluding references and appendices falls within range; papers substantially over/under are returned to authors.

4. **Missing AI tool disclosure.** If any AI tool (ChatGPT, Claude, etc.) was used in any phase of manuscript preparation—even for brainstorming or editing—it must be explicitly disclosed in a footnote or dedicated statement. Failure to disclose creates reputational and ethical risk.

5. **Unmarked data/code availability statements.** JEMI requires explicit statements on data and code provenance. Do not assume "available upon request" suffices; specify repository URL or proprietary/restricted status clearly. Missing statements delay acceptance.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

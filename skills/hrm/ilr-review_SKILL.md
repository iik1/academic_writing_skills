# Journal Writing Skill: ILR Review

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** HRM&EMP
- **Publisher:** SAGE (for Cornell University ILR School)
- **Scope:** Interdisciplinary journal on all aspects of the employment relationship, spanning labor economics, HR/employment relations, and labor law/policy; strong emphasis on public-policy and firm-level relevance.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/pb-assets/cmscontent/ILR/2020_ILRR_Manuscript-Style-Guide-for-Authors_06-02-20.pdf (Manuscript Style Guide for Authors); https://journals.sagepub.com/author-instructions/ilr
- **Field baseline:** hrm-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 13 (abstracts/front matter via IDEAS/RePEc, SAGE abstract pages, and working-paper mirrors: Baktash & Jirjahn "Are Managers More Machiavellian..."; Coglianese & Price "Income in the Off-Season"; Modestino et al. "No Longer Qualified?"; Tan, Luka & Mazo "Unlikely Organizers"; Petrucci, Hinkel & Dunn "Timely Construction"; Ouyang & Batt "Which Firms Offer Better Jobs..."; Sullivan & To "Job Dispersion and Compensating Wage Differentials"; Qian & Fan "Working from Home and Affective Well-Being..."; Bartosch, Nicklich & Jackson "Employee Voice and Corporate Governance"; Nelson & Wilmers "Earnings Effects of Direct Worker Voice in Production"; Gruber "The Labor Market Impacts of Fair Work Legislation"; Di Giacomo & Lerch "Robots and Non-Participation..."; Muffert & Riphahn "Long-Run Career Outcomes of Multiple Job Holding"; plus Blau "Gender Inequality in the Labor Market: Continuing Progress?")

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No "Introduction" subhead is permitted, and headings/subheadings are not numbered at all (contrast the common numbered-section convention elsewhere). The introduction must be brief (approximately three to five paragraphs), must not present an outline/"map" of the paper, and must not anticipate findings or conclusions. Standard body order (Methods, Results, Discussion, Conclusion) is otherwise unconstrained but headings must appear regularly (do not go long stretches without one). No more than three heading levels are allowed, and second-/third-level headings must be used in sets of two or more (never a single subordinate heading).

### Length
10,000-word maximum, inclusive of text, tables, figures, footnotes, references, and appendices (supplemental online material does not count). Cover letter must justify any excess length.

### Abstract
150-word maximum (title capped at 50 words). First sentence states data, method, and purpose; two or three further sentences state key findings/conclusions/implications. Must be written in third person ("The authors investigate...") — note this differs from the body text's mandated first person. Language must be accessible to readers with no statistics background.

### Citations
In-text author-date parenthetical style, e.g., (Smith 2004). Multiple citations are separated by semicolons and ordered **chronologically, not alphabetically**: (Smith 1999; Anderson 2005; Frank 2011, 2013). Up to four author names are spelled out at first mention, then "et al."; for five or more authors, use "et al." even at first occurrence. Every reference-list entry must be cited in text and vice versa.

### Headings
Three levels only. Level 1: Title Style, bold, centered. Level 2: Title Style, bold, flush left. Level 3: Title Style, italic, not bold. No numbering of any heading level.

### Tables and Figures
Placed at the end of the manuscript (not inline), with in-text placement markers like "[[Table 1 near here]]". Must be self-contained with full column headers (including the first/label column). Significance asterisks are standardized: * p<.10, ** p<.05, *** p<.01. Size capped at 5"x8" portrait or 8"x5" landscape. Tables must be built in Word's native table feature (not pasted as images) and must remain editable. Numbers reported to no more than three decimal places unless finer precision is meaningful.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Do not thank or credit the editor or referees in acknowledgments. Do not include an author-disclaimer statement (e.g., "errors are our own") — the journal runs a blanket disclaimer itself. Avoid footnotes and in-text citations in the introduction and conclusion; put non-essential citations in the literature review instead. Do not use endnotes (footnotes only, used sparingly, for technical/explanatory content). Avoid a detailed "more research is needed" discussion in the conclusion. Do not use "he"/"she" exclusively or mix singular/plural ("an individual...their").

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
ENFORCED beyond FIELD_SKILL's "permitted and increasingly common" framing: the guidelines explicitly mandate active voice and first person throughout the body ("we compiled data," not "data were compiled"), giving a worked example contrasting the two. The abstract, however, is required to be third person — a body/abstract voice split not present in FIELD_SKILL. Confirmed in spot-checked abstracts (e.g., Blau; Di Giacomo & Lerch; Baktash & Jirjahn all use "the authors" or third-person framing in abstract while body/discussion excerpts use first person).

### Citation Density
RELAXATION/RESTRUCTURING of citation placement rather than density: the introduction and conclusion are expected to be nearly citation-free, with citation weight concentrated in the literature review — a stricter partitioning than FIELD_SKILL's general "citation density highest in intro/lit review."

### Structural Deviations
ENFORCED: ban on an "Introduction" heading and on any heading numbering; three-heading-level cap with defined bold/italic formatting; mandatory chronological (not alphabetical) ordering of multiple in-text citations. These are house-style specifics FIELD_SKILL leaves unspecified.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Optional but encouraged: explicit numbered hypothesis statements (H1, H1a) set off in a block, in roman type — common practice signal for quantitative submissions, though not stated as mandatory for all papers. Firm-level and public-policy implications are explicitly flagged as a priority interest for the conclusion, sharpening FIELD_SKILL's general "practical implications" expectation toward economics-adjacent policy framing specifically (consistent with observed article topics: minimum wage/scheduling law effects, robots and labor supply, project labor agreements).

## Common Pitfalls
- Including a subhead literally titled "Introduction," or numbering headings/subheadings — both explicitly banned.
- Alphabetizing multiple in-text citations instead of ordering them chronologically.
- Writing the abstract in first person ("We investigate...") instead of the required third person, or exceeding the 150-word cap.
- Thanking editors/reviewers or adding an author-disclaimer statement in acknowledgments — both explicitly discouraged.
- Loading the introduction or conclusion with citations/footnotes instead of reserving them for the literature review.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

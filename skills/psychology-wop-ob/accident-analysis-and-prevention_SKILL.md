# Journal Writing Skill: Accident Analysis and Prevention

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** PSYCH (WOP-OB)
- **Publisher:** Elsevier
- **Scope:** Covers accidental injury and damage broadly, with a heavy emphasis on transportation (road, rail) safety — human, environmental, and vehicular factors in crash occurrence and severity, countermeasure design and evaluation, biomechanics of injury, and statistical/computational modeling of accident data. In practice the journal is dominated by traffic-safety engineering, human-factors, and machine-learning/statistical-modeling papers rather than organizational or workplace psychology.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/accident-analysis-and-prevention/publish/guide-for-authors (ScienceDirect guide-for-authors page; full text blocked by 403 in this environment, supplemented by secondary summaries)
- **Field baseline:** psychology-wop-ob-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 10 with full abstract/keyword review, ~25 additional titles scanned for topical/temporal consistency (PMIDs 42378747, 42378746, 42378745, 42372571, 42365749, 42335681, 42320389, 42296798, 36580764, 36592490; years 2022 and 2026)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No structured/sectioned abstract format is enforced (see Abstract below). Beyond that, no journal-specific mandated section list was found distinct from standard IMRaD; secondary sources reference a "structured or extended abstract per article-type guidelines" but this is not confirmed by actual published abstracts (see Observed Deltas).

### Length
Secondary sources cite an informal target of roughly 8-15 manuscript pages for research papers/reviews; not confirmed as a hard cap on the official guide-for-authors page (inaccessible). Treat as approximate practice, not enforced rule.

### Abstract
No structured abstract with labeled subheadings (Background/Methods/Results/Conclusions). All spot-checked abstracts across 2022-2026 are single-paragraph free narrative.

### Citations
No enforced reference style at submission; the journal accepts any consistent style with author names, source titles, year, volume, and pagination, and encourages DOI inclusion. This is looser than a fixed house style.

### Headings
No distinct convention found beyond standard practice; not confirmed via guide-for-authors due to access block.

### Tables and Figures
No journal-specific rules identified beyond standard Elsevier artwork/illustration-service instructions; nothing distinct from FIELD_SKILL norms confirmed.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Standard Elsevier publishing-ethics requirements apply (plagiarism, competing-interest declaration, ethics/IRB statement for human-subjects work, generative-AI-use declaration, data availability statement). No prohibitions specific to writing style or content beyond these standard editorial-integrity requirements.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
This journal's dominant article type is transportation-safety engineering/human-factors/applied-statistics research, not organizational psychology. Voice is more purely technical-engineering than FIELD_SKILL's psychology register: heavier use of technical/methodological nouns (framework, model, algorithm) as grammatical subjects ("This paper constructs...", "The framework flagged...", "The model successfully reproduced...") alongside the first-person-plural pattern FIELD_SKILL describes. PRACTICE ONLY — driven by subject matter (engineering/ML) rather than a stated journal rule, but consistent enough across the sample to note as a delta from psychology-journal voice.

### Statistical Reporting
FIELD_SKILL's expectation of APA-style precise inferential statistics (exact test statistics, df, p-values, CIs) in the abstract is largely absent here. Abstracts instead report substantive/practical quantities (percentages, counts, model performance descriptors, e.g. "141 high-priority locations (1.03%)", "reduced priority scores by 6.1%") rather than significance-test statistics, reflecting the journal's applied/engineering-modeling orientation rather than hypothesis-testing psychology. PRACTICE ONLY, but consistent across the sample — RELAXATION of the FIELD_SKILL abstract-reporting norm, appropriate given this is an ABS 3 journal with a different disciplinary center of gravity within the field's scope.

### Structural Deviations
Abstracts are consistently unstructured single paragraphs with no explicit hypothesis statements or labeled Background/Methods/Results/Conclusions — a RELAXATION of FIELD_SKILL's expectation of structured/semi-structured abstracts and explicit numbered hypotheses. This held across all 10 abstracts read, spanning 2022-2026, so it is treated as a stable journal norm rather than low-prevalence noise.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Many articles are single-study, data-driven modeling papers (e.g., crash-severity prediction, risk-hotspot frameworks) rather than the multi-study hypothesis-testing packages FIELD_SKILL describes as increasingly favored at the ABS 4*/4 tier. This is a RELAXATION consistent with the ABS 3 rating — single-study, applied/methodological contributions are the norm rather than the exception here.

## Common Pitfalls
- Do not submit a structured abstract with labeled subheadings — this journal's published abstracts are consistently unstructured single paragraphs, unlike some other applied/health journals.
- Do not force a psychology-style numbered-hypothesis abstract; report the modeling/data approach and substantive findings (rates, percentages, performance gains) rather than inferential test statistics in the abstract.
- Single-study, methods-driven contributions (a new model, framework, or dataset application) are acceptable and common; a multi-study package is not required for this ABS 3 outlet.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

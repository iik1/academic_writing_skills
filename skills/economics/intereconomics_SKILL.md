# Journal Writing Skill: Intereconomics

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Sciendo (De Gruyter Group); formerly Springer. Published with ZBW – Leibniz Information Centre for Economics and the Centre for European Policy Studies (CEPS).
- **Scope:** Bimonthly review of European economic and social policy; publishes short policy-oriented pieces (editorial, forum, articles, "Letter from America") on economic policy issues in or affecting Europe. Not a standard research-article outlet in the ABS 4/4* IMRAD sense.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.intereconomics.eu/notes-for-authors.html and https://www.intereconomics.eu/submissions.html
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 12 (guideline page; two issue tables of contents, 2025 No. 1 and 2026 No. 2; full-text review of "Should Non-EU Countries Be Able to Join the EU Emissions Trading System?" by Andritzky & Hesse and "The Current Economic Situation in Germany in the Context of Previous Crises" by Grömling; abstract/metadata-level review of six further Editorial/Forum/Articles pieces via PMC open-access mirrors: "Redesigning EU Fiscal Rules After COVID-19," "De-Globalisation — A New World Order?," "Next Generation EU," "The Cost of Growing Older," plus two additional forum pieces)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Articles must **not be divided into numbered chapters or parts**. The journal's three (plus one) content categories each carry a different length and purpose: Editorial, Forum (multiple short contributions offering different perspectives on one policy problem), Articles (the main research-adjacent policy pieces), and the occasional "Letter from America" column.

### Length
- Editorial: ~1,300 words
- Forum contribution: 2,500–4,000 words
- Articles: 3,000–5,000 words (25,000–35,000 characters including spaces, including footnotes)
- Letter from America: ~1,300 words

This is dramatically shorter than a typical ABS 4/4* empirical or theory paper.

### Abstract
No structured or dedicated abstract is required or used. Articles open directly with a substantive framing paragraph that functions as an implicit summary of the argument and its stakes.

### Citations
APA-style author-date citations, given in a reference list. Footnotes are used for supplementary remarks, marked with superior numbers after punctuation, listed at the foot of the page — not for primary citations.

### Headings
Section headings are short, descriptive, and topical (e.g., "The development dilemma," "Overcoming reservations") rather than functional/generic (e.g., "Introduction," "Results," "Conclusion"). No numbering of sections. A final section is often titled "Summary" rather than "Conclusion."

### Tables and Figures
Submitted as separate, independently editable files (Excel, EPS, or similar) — not embedded as flattened images. Numbered sequentially by order of appearance in text.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Manuscripts must be original, unpublished, and not under review elsewhere. Mandatory plagiarism screening (Docoloc). Non-native English speakers are explicitly asked to have the manuscript reviewed by a native speaker before submission. British English preferred.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
Intereconomics pieces do not follow the empirical or theory IMRAD architecture in FIELD_SKILL at all: no separate Data, Empirical Strategy/Identification, or Model Setup sections, no standalone Related Literature section, no formal abstract, and no numbered sections. This is a RELAXATION appropriate to the journal's format (short policy commentary, ABS 1) rather than a redefinition of field norms — full research papers with these features are simply not what this outlet publishes. ENFORCED (stated in guidelines: "not divided into numbered chapters or parts"; confirmed across all sampled pieces).

### Voice and Register
Register is more accessible and less technically dense than FIELD_SKILL's ABS 4/4* baseline: policy argumentation is foregrounded over method exposition, and jargon is introduced with more contextual explanation for a mixed academic/policymaker readership. First-person plural "we" is not the dominant convention observed; single-author think-piece voice and third-person analytical framing are common alongside "we." PRACTICE ONLY / RELAXATION — consistent with the shorter, policy-brief format rather than a stated journal rule.

### Statistical Reporting
Where empirical claims appear (e.g., growth-rate comparisons, crisis-recovery paths), they are stated with quantitative specificity similar to FIELD_SKILL, but without full econometric apparatus (no standard-error reporting, robustness sections, or identification-strategy subsections) — consistent with the shorter article format rather than a distinct reporting norm. FIELD DEFAULT adapted to length constraint.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Articles frequently close with a section literally titled "Summary" rather than "Conclusion," and this closing section is brief, restating the policy implication rather than revisiting a results section. PRACTICE ONLY, low-prevalence-but-consistent across sampled Articles-section pieces.

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting a full IMRAD structure with numbered sections and a standalone Data/Methods/Results architecture — the journal explicitly rejects numbered chapters/parts and expects short, topically-headed sections instead.
- Writing to ABS 4/4* length (8,000+ words) — Intereconomics articles cap at 5,000 words including footnotes; Forum pieces are shorter still (2,500–4,000).
- Omitting a native-English review pass — the guidelines explicitly ask non-native speakers to have the manuscript checked before submission.
- Treating tables/figures as flattened images rather than submitting editable source files (Excel/EPS), which the guidelines require.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

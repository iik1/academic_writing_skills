# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Applied Corporate Finance

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** FINANCE
- **Publisher:** Wiley-Blackwell (for the Financial Management Association / Morgan Stanley)
- **Scope:** Quarterly journal (est. 1988) bridging academic finance research and corporate practice — risk management, capital structure, governance, and strategy — aimed at senior corporate policymakers as well as academics. Ceased publication as of December 31, 2025; archive remains on Wiley Online Library.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/page/journal/17456622/homepage/forauthors.html (partially accessible; key excerpts below via search cache)
- **Field baseline:** finance-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 9 (titles/metadata; full text inaccessible behind Wiley paywall for all but front-matter). Sample: "Rethinking Risk Management" (Stulz, 1996/2022 reprint, full text obtained via author's own hosted PDF metadata + search-engine summary); "Corporate Culture in a New Era: Views from the C-Suite" (Graham, Grennan, Harvey, Rajgopal, 2023); "The Financial Cost of Carbon" (Bolton, Halem, Kacperczyk, 2022); "Globalization, Corporate Finance, and the Cost of Capital" (Stulz, 2022); "Enterprise Risk Management: Theory and Practice" (Nocco & Stulz, 2022); "Depositor Discipline and the Banking Panic of 2023" (Kupiec, 2023, roundtable-adjacent); Vanderbilt University roundtable on the capital structure puzzle (2023); American Enterprise Institute roundtable, "Was Milton Friedman Right about Shareholder Capitalism?" (2021); "Making Economics More Useful" (Bhidé). This is a good-faith, thinner-than-ideal sample: Wiley's platform returned HTTP 402 on nearly every full-text request, and no third-party full-text mirror beyond figure-only PDFs was accessible. Findings below rely on titles, RePEc/search-engine abstract summaries, and the journal's public guidelines snippet rather than close reading of full articles.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No mandatory numbered-section template is enforced; the introductory section carries no heading ("Introduction" is implicit, unlabeled). Subsequent sections use free-form topical headers rather than a fixed empirical-paper skeleton (Data/Results/Robustness). Many issues are built around **roundtable transcripts** (multi-participant discussion format, sometimes with a moderator) or **case studies** rather than a standard empirical-paper structure — this is a recurring, non-random format for a large share of content, not an occasional exception.

### Length
No explicit word/page limit found in accessible guideline text; historical practice (via title/issue patterns) suggests articles run shorter and more essayistic than ABS 4/4* empirical papers, closer to 15-25 published pages including any executive summary and references.

### Abstract
Cover-page abstract of **150-200 words**, unstructured single paragraph, submitted with the manuscript (per Wiley guidelines cache). Distinctively, the journal also publishes a standalone **"Executive Summaries"** item per issue — a separate front-matter piece compiling short summaries of all articles in that issue for a practitioner readership. This is a journal-level packaging convention, not a per-article abstract requirement beyond the standard cover-page abstract.

### Citations
Guidelines indicate the journal historically accepted **multiple reference formats** (examples given for journal articles, book chapters, and periodicals), i.e., less rigidly standardized than top-tier finance journals, though author-year in-text citation remains consistent with FIELD_SKILL.

### Headings
Section headers (where used) are specified as **left-justified, 14-pt, title case, bold, small caps** — a specific typographic convention stated in guidelines, distinct from generic FIELD_SKILL Arabic-numeral sectioning (no numbering requirement stated).

### Tables and Figures
Tables numbered consecutively in Arabic numerals (guideline-stated); no further table-density or design requirement found beyond FIELD_SKILL defaults.

### Explicit Prohibitions
None identified from accessible guideline text.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
Markedly more accessible and less densely technical than the ABS 4*/4 FIELD_SKILL baseline. Titles and available summaries ("Rethinking Risk Management," "Globalization, Corporate Finance, and the Cost of Capital," "Making Economics More Useful") signal prescriptive, argument-driven essays addressed to corporate practitioners and boards, not econometric identification-first prose. Authors write to persuade a management audience of a framework or recommendation, not primarily to establish causal identification. PRACTICE ONLY / pattern-level, based on title and summary evidence rather than full-text reading of the sample.

### Structural Deviations
Roundtable/panel-discussion transcripts and case studies constitute a substantial, recurring share of published content (observed in at least 5 of 9 sampled items: Vanderbilt roundtable, AEI Friedman roundtable, Kupiec banking-panic piece with roundtable participants, plus multiple additional roundtables noted in the broader table of contents search). This is a genre absent from FIELD_SKILL's structural norms entirely. PRACTICE ONLY, but high-prevalence enough to flag as a defining journal feature rather than a rare exception.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Per-issue "Executive Summaries" front-matter item, condensing all articles in an issue into short, non-technical summaries for a time-pressed executive readership — a packaging convention not seen in FIELD_SKILL journals. GUIDELINE-adjacent / observed practice (appears as a titled item in the table of contents across multiple issues).

## Common Pitfalls
- Treating this as a standard empirical-identification journal: many accepted pieces are argumentative essays, case studies, or multi-party roundtable transcripts rather than hypothesis-testing empirical papers — do not force a Data/Results/Robustness skeleton if the piece is essay- or discussion-format.
- Under-writing for a practitioner audience: FIELD_SKILL's technical-density norm (unexplained jargon, heavy identification discussion) is likely too dense for this outlet's C-suite/board readership; prioritize clear, prescriptive framing over methodological throat-clearing.
- Ignoring the flexible reference format: unlike top-tier finance journals with a single fixed citation style, this journal's guidelines show tolerance for multiple reference formats — do not assume a single rigid style is enforced.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

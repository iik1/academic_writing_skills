# Journal Writing Skill: Economic and Social Review

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Economic and Social Studies (journal of the Irish Economic Association)
- **Scope:** Ireland's leading journal for economics and applied social science; welcomes economics, sociology, political science, and cognate disciplines, with explicit preference for research relevant to Irish, UK, or European economic and social policy. Maintains a dedicated "Policy Articles" section (same peer review as regular articles) for applied policy work.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.esr.ie/about/submissions (accessed directly)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 11 (abstracts/titles/front-matter): Firm-Level Attitudes and Actions to the "Twin Transition" Challenges (Kren & Lawless, 2024); Catholic-Protestant Wage Differentials in Northern Ireland 2011 (Gillespie et al., 2024); A Cross Country Perspective on Irish Enterprise Investment (Gargan et al., 2024); Do Social Resources Moderate the Negative Association Between Financial Hardship and Life Satisfaction in Ireland? (Walsh, 2024); Childcare in Ireland: Usage, Affordability and Incentives to Work (Doorley et al., 2023); Young People Not in Employment, Education or Training (NEET) (Redmond & McFadden, 2023); The Short-Term Distributional Impact of Pension Auto-enrolment (Keane et al., 2023); Personal Assistance Services for Disabled People in Ireland (McCoy, 2023); Household Consumption and the Housing Net Worth Channel in Ireland (Cronin & McQuinn, 2023); Assessing the Impact of Mobility on COVID-19 (O'Donoghue et al., 2023); Why are Disability Rates for Older Working-Age Adults in Northern Ireland So High? (Devlin et al., 2023). Full text was largely inaccessible (paywalled or binary PDF); assessment relies on abstracts, titles, and front-matter recovered via RePEc/IDEAS and search-engine snippets.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Cover page (title, author names, affiliations) submitted separately from the main manuscript for blind review; the main manuscript file carries the title only, with no author-identifying information. No other mandated section structure beyond this separation.

### Length
Main papers: 4,000–9,000 words (longer requires justification). Notes and review articles: maximum 3,000 words including footnotes, references, and illustrations. This is materially shorter than the unstated but implicitly longer norm at ABS 4*/4 outlets.

### Abstract
Maximum 100 words, presenting the paper's significance and main conclusions. This is a hard numeric cap, tighter than typical field practice.

### Citations
Author-year in-text, e.g. "Walsh (1981, p. 36)" or "(Geary, 1927, p. 197)." References alphabetical at the end; journal titles not abbreviated; books list city and publisher; journal articles include volume, issue, and pages.

### Headings
No specific mandated heading scheme beyond standard practice; equations numbered consecutively in parentheses on the right-hand margin.

### Tables and Figures
Placed within the text at the appropriate point (not collected at the end). Titled and numbered consecutively in Arabic numerals; must be self-explanatory; graphs must be reproduction quality.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Must not be previously published or under review elsewhere. All identifying markers must be stripped from the main submission file to preserve blind review. Underlining prohibited in favour of italics (except URLs).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm on formality and technical density, but the sample skews noticeably toward applied/descriptive framing rather than identification-driven framing — several titles and abstracts foreground institutional description and policy context (e.g., "Personal Assistance Services for Disabled People in Ireland," "Young People Not in Employment, Education or Training (NEET): Concepts, Consequences and Policy Approaches") ahead of, or in place of, a causal-estimate headline. PRACTICE ONLY.

### Statistical Reporting
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm where quantitative work appears (e.g., Gillespie et al. report differentials as "no larger than 1.4 log per cent and statistically insignificant" — precise, field-standard reporting). Note the journal also publishes qualitative and mixed-methods work (e.g., McCoy 2023, a two-step qualitative/survey design) for which FIELD_SKILL's quantitative-reporting norms do not apply. FIELD DEFAULT for quantitative papers; scope is simply broader than FIELD_SKILL contemplates.

### Citation Density
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm; no observed delta.

### Structural Deviations
GUIDELINE ONLY / RELAXATION: the 4,000–9,000 word cap and 100-word abstract are firm, stated constraints well below what ABS 4*/4 practice implies. This is an ABS-tier relaxation of length, not a structural redefinition — introduction/body/conclusion architecture is not otherwise altered by the guidelines or contradicted in the sample.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
PRACTICE ONLY: a minority of titles use a quoted phrase drawn from qualitative fieldwork as a subtitle (e.g., McCoy 2023: "...'they meet the criteria for supports but we don't have the resources to provide the services'"). This is a low-prevalence pattern (observed in 1 of 11 articles), consistent with the journal's acceptance of qualitative/mixed-methods work, not a stated or dominant convention.

## Common Pitfalls
- Exceeding the 100-word abstract cap or the 4,000–9,000 word body limit — these are stated hard limits, not soft targets.
- Leaving author names, affiliations, or other identifying information in the main manuscript file rather than the separate cover page, breaking blind review.
- Assuming a strict quantitative-causal framing is required; the journal's remit explicitly includes qualitative and mixed-methods policy research, particularly in the Policy Articles section.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

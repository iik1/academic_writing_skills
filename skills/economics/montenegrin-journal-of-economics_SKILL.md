# Journal Writing Skill: Montenegrin Journal of Economics

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Economic Laboratory for Transition Research (ELIT), Podgorica, Montenegro
- **Scope:** International quarterly journal accepting theoretical, empirical, and applicative articles from all areas of economics; broad regional/transition-economics orientation but not restricted to it.
- **Guidelines URL:** http://www.mnje.com/ (submission-guidelines content accessible via site; a standalone "instructions for authors" PDF exists at mnje.com but returned 404 on direct fetch — guideline text below reconstructed from the site's author-guidance page, accessed successfully)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Required elements, in order: title, abstract, keywords, introduction, body (subtitled sections, unspecified naming), conclusion, bibliography (references arranged alphabetically by author surname). No mandated Methods/Results/Discussion labels — journal leaves internal section naming to authors beyond this skeleton.

### Length
4,000–5,000 words, maximum 32,000 characters including spaces. This is a hard ceiling far below FIELD_SKILL's unstated (effectively unlimited) length norm for ABS 4*/4 papers.

### Abstract
Structured requirement: 150–200 words. FIELD_SKILL does not specify abstract length; this is journal-specific.

### Citations
Guidelines do not specify an in-text citation style beyond requiring a bibliography sorted alphabetically by author. No numbered/footnote system is indicated. Treat as consistent with FIELD_SKILL's author-year default absent contrary evidence.

### Headings
No mandated heading scheme beyond title/abstract/keywords/introduction/subtitled body/conclusion/bibliography. Numbering style (Roman/Arabic) not specified — follow FIELD_SKILL default.

### Tables and Figures
No journal-specific rules found in accessible guidance beyond general formatting (A4, Franklin Gothic Book 11pt for the whole manuscript). No explicit table/figure numbering or notes convention specified — follow FIELD_SKILL default.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Maximum 10% plagiarism similarity, checked with anti-plagiarism software.
- Authors are explicitly stated to bear sole responsibility for the "authenticity of data, facts, quotations and other information."
- Manuscripts must be in English (author guidance additionally asks authors from Montenegro and neighboring countries to also submit a version in their native language, but the published/reviewed article is English-only).
- Formatting mandate: Word document, A4, Franklin Gothic Book, 11pt — a publisher-level typesetting requirement outside FIELD_SKILL's scope but binding here.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
Sample shows more variable and occasionally looser prose than the ABS 4*/4 baseline, consistent with a large non-native-English, internationally distributed author base (authors from Tunisia, Ukraine, Slovakia, Mexico, Russia, Montenegro in this sample). Register is still formal and technical, but sentence construction is sometimes less economical than FIELD_SKILL describes, and a couple of sampled abstracts (e.g., Kravchenko, Sidorov & Draskovic 2021) lean toward broader, essayistic framing (systemic risk, "neo-catastrophes," cosmopolitan politics) rather than the tight empirical-claim-first register FIELD_SKILL documents. PRACTICE ONLY / low-prevalence — not universal across the sample, and not a stated journal norm, so do not treat as a redefinition of voice; most sampled abstracts (Dahmani et al., Durcova, Jencova et al.) are conventional quantitative-empirical abstracts matching FIELD_SKILL register.

### Structural Deviations
Topical range is broader and more heterogeneous than a typical ABS 4*/4 field journal — conventional panel/VAR/DSGE empirical papers sit alongside more conceptual/policy-essay pieces in the same issues. RELAXATION of FIELD_SKILL's implicit expectation of a fairly uniform empirical or theoretical article type; the journal's stated scope ("theoretical, empirical and applicative articles from all areas of Economics") explicitly licenses this breadth. Not a new norm, just a wider tent at this ABS tier.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Short, hard word ceiling (4,000–5,000 words) compresses the FIELD_SKILL introduction/literature/robustness architecture. Given the length constraint, expect thinner standalone literature-review and robustness sections than FIELD_SKILL describes as near-universal at the ABS 4*/4 level — GUIDELINE-driven RELAXATION, consistent with the word limit rather than a separately observed stylistic choice (full text was not accessible to directly confirm section-by-section compression, so this is inferred from the stated length ceiling and should be treated cautiously).

## Common Pitfalls
- Exceeding the 4,000–5,000 word / 32,000-character ceiling — this is a hard submission constraint, not a soft target as at ABS 4*/4 outlets.
- Submitting an abstract outside the 150–200 word band.
- Wrong font/format (must be Franklin Gothic Book, 11pt, A4) — a typesetting requirement that has no analogue in FIELD_SKILL and is easy to overlook when adapting a manuscript drafted for a top-tier outlet.
- Bibliography not alphabetized by author — journal states this explicitly as a formatting requirement.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

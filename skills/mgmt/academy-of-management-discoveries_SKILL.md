# Journal Writing Skill: Academy of Management Discoveries

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN
- **Publisher:** Academy of Management
- **Scope:** Publishes phenomenon-driven empirical research guided by abduction and induction — studies that surface novel or emerging phenomena, unusual empirical patterns, or theory-inconsistent relationships that existing theory does not adequately explain.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.aom.org/publications/journals/publishing-with-aom/author-and-reviewer-resources/author-resources/submitting-to-discoveries/
- **Field baseline:** mgmt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 direct (title/abstract/editorial level: "Can you outsmart the robot?", "Backstage matters: Collective energy and information sharing on global teams", "The Growth Mindset at Work", plus editorial statements "AMD—Clarifying What We Are about and Where We Are Going," "Publishing in the Academy of Management Journals," "Engaging Practitioners in Empirical Exploration") plus repeated, consistent editorial-policy statements on the journal's mission surfaced across ~10 additional search hits. Full text of journals.aom.org is paywalled/blocked (403); relied on abstracts, editorials, and the publisher's own style-guide description of what AMD papers must look like.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Introduction grounds the phenomenon in a specific case/instance, describes context, and states the specific research question(s) and why current theory fails to explain the phenomenon. Findings section presents empirical exploration culminating in one or more "discoveries" (new boundary conditions, questioned mechanisms, theory-inconsistent relationships). Discussion offers post-hoc, data-driven explanation for where "extant theory falls short." No standalone Theory-and-Hypotheses section.

### Length
Traditional papers: 15–40 pages excluding references/tables/figures/appendices. Registered Reports: max 25 pages. Discoveries-through-Prose: no fixed limit. Commentaries: 2–3 pages. Guideposts: up to 1,000 words.

### Abstract
Unstructured, maximum 200 words. Must briefly describe the discovery presented and its potential implications (not a generic summary of question/method/finding).

### Citations
Follows AOM's general Style Guide (APA-based); no journal-specific deviation identified beyond FIELD_SKILL.

### Headings
No distinctive heading conventions beyond the phenomenon-forward structure above (i.e., no "Theory and Hypotheses" heading; "Findings" is standard).

### Tables and Figures
No AMD-specific table/figure rules found beyond general AOM formatting (12-pt Times New Roman, double-spaced, 1-inch margins).

### Explicit Prohibitions
Manuscripts using theory-driven hypotheses/predictions as the organizing device are discouraged — AMD papers "should not have hypotheses or theory-driven predictions." No prior/concurrent submission elsewhere; no reuse of data reported in another manuscript without disclosure in the cover letter; all author-identifying information must be removed before submission. Manuscripts not complying with the AOM Style Guide are returned without review.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Structural Deviations
FIELD_SKILL's "quantitative deductive" template (Theory and Hypotheses → Methods → Results, with H1/H2 hypothesis testing) does not apply here — ENFORCED. AMD explicitly replaces the hypothesis-testing logic with a phenomenon-first, research-question-driven structure even when methods are quantitative. This is closer to FIELD_SKILL's "qualitative inductive" template but applies regardless of method (quantitative, qualitative, or mixed): ground phenomenon → state research question → explore data → report discoveries → offer post-hoc theoretical explanation. Confirmed consistently across editorial statements and multiple article framings.

### Voice and Register
Editorial guidance stresses papers should be "written as simply and concisely as possible" and "accessible to the Academy's wide-ranging readership," with an explicit stated goal of preserving "the voice of the authors, not that of the reviewers or editors" — GUIDELINE ONLY (stated in guidelines; a mild relaxation toward plainer, less jargon-heavy prose than typical for ABS 3-4 management outlets). Consistent with ABS 3 RELAXATION of field norms rather than a stricter requirement.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Explicit rejection of confirmatory hypothesis testing as the central claim form — ENFORCED. Where FIELD_SKILL treats "propositions vs. hypotheses" as determined by empirical vs. conceptual status, AMD adds a third case: empirical papers that use research questions instead of either, because the phenomenon is pre-theory.

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting a manuscript organized around numbered hypotheses (H1, H2…) — AMD editors and guidelines treat this as a structural mismatch with the journal's phenomenon-first identity, even if the empirical methods are otherwise rigorous.
- Framing the introduction as a literature-gap argument rather than a phenomenon-grounded case; AMD introductions should open with the compelling empirical case/instance itself, not a review of what theory says.
- Abstract exceeding 200 words or reading as a generic structured summary rather than a description of the discovery and its implications.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: The Journal of Retirement

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Portfolio Management Research (PM Research)
- **Scope:** Portfolio management, retirement income planning, pension design, behavioral finance, policy, financial literacy, and practitioner-academic research at the intersection of retirement science and investment practice.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://jor.pm-research.com/authors (behind login; see PMR submission guidelines)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 12 (2018-2025, mixed issues)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
- Initial submission does not require specific format or style
- Final acceptance requires conformance to journal's stylistic and mechanical specifications
- No explicit requirement for the canonical FIELD_SKILL section order (Introduction–Conclusion); empirical papers frequently follow that pattern, but not mandated

### Length
- No stated word or page limit in public guidelines

### Abstract
- Not specified in accessible guidelines; abstracts typically appear in published articles

### Citations
- Not explicitly specified beyond ethical requirement for citations to all work "that originated with other authors"
- Author-year format observed in articles (consistent with FIELD_SKILL)

### Headings
- Up to three levels of headings permitted
- Top-level headings: bold uppercase
- Second-level headings: bold and indented
- Third level present in articles but format not detailed in accessible guidelines

### Tables and Figures
- **Tables:** Use 9-point Times New Roman or larger; numbers aligned by decimal point; same decimal-point count for same number types; centered headings, columns, and numbers; identical language in repeated appearances; prepare in native MS Word format (not Excel exports or pictures)
- **Charts/Figures:** Consistent capitalization and abbreviations throughout paper; all axes and lines labeled clearly and consistently; prepare within MS Word when possible; if imported from other software, provide duplicate in separate MS Excel tab with underlying data; use color encouraged if legible in black-and-white reproduction
- **Exhibits:** Color permitted; must be distinguishable in all color-blindness modes and in black-and-white print

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Work presented as original must be original
- Text originating with other authors must be quoted with quotation marks or cited
- Articles must provide sufficient detail and raw data for professionals in the field to replicate findings and conclusions

### Language and Tone
- Journal explicitly prefers "a more readable style" than generic academic journals
- High ethical bar for replication and transparency

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
**RELAXATION (not a stated constraint, but evidenced in practice):** While FIELD_SKILL describes "first-person plural" as near-universal in top-tier economics, the Journal of Retirement sample shows greater use of passive voice and third-person framing, particularly in methods and results sections. Articles mix "we" and passive constructions more liberally than ABS 4*/4 norms. Example observed: "Tables are presented in..." alongside "we constructed...". This reflects a lower-tier journal's slightly less stringent voice norm, though "we" remains common in introduction and conclusion.

### Citation Density
**RELAXATION:** FIELD_SKILL specifies low-to-medium citation density in results sections. The Journal of Retirement articles show medium-to-high citation density throughout, particularly in the introduction, methods, and discussion sections. Authors more frequently cite prior empirical benchmarks within results tables and comparative sections, reflecting the journal's practitioner-academic hybrid audience. Supporting evidence: Blanchett & Kaplan, Munnell et al., Biggs & Shapiro all cite research findings within results narratives rather than deferring comparison to prose alone.

### Structural Deviations
**PRACTICE ONLY (observed, not mandated):** Articles frequently include a standalone "Practical Implications" or "Discussion" section distinct from results, rather than integrating discussion into results paragraphs as FIELD_SKILL implies is near-universal. This reflects the journal's applied emphasis. Additionally, several articles include brief "Key Takeaways" or boxed summaries of actionable findings—a convention not present in ABS 4*/4 outlets. Example: Fisch & Turner article includes a call-out box listing three policy recommendations.

### Empirical Reporting Style
**PRACTICE ONLY:** Articles in this sample show slightly more narrative explanation of results than FIELD_SKILL suggests is standard at top tier. Rather than assuming readers will interpret a table independently, authors more frequently gloss key findings in prose: "As shown in Table 2, partial annuitization in combination with planned withdrawals is generally the best strategy, with a success rate of 91% under baseline assumptions." FIELD_SKILL allows this but does not mark it as typical; here, it is routine.

### Audience Orientation
**ENFORCED (implicit in guidelines and observed):** Unlike the purely academic orientation of FIELD_SKILL, the Journal of Retirement explicitly targets a dual audience of financial professionals and scholars. This is evidenced by:
1. Practical policy recommendations accompanying empirical findings (Biggs & Shapiro, Littlewood)
2. Case-study framing (Littlewood's consulting perspective)
3. Presentation of findings in terms actionable for practitioners (e.g., "Retirees diversifying with solar energy gain greater financial security")
4. Commentary sections addressing workplace policy (emergency savings programs)

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Over-emphasizing practitioner guidance without empirical grounding:** The journal values applied insight, but does not accept prescriptions without supporting analysis. Ensure policy recommendations are anchored in results or prior research, not assertion.

2. **Neglecting legibility of tables in black-and-white:** Color exhibits are encouraged but must remain readable when printed in grayscale. Test all figures under B&W conversion before submission.

3. **Using non-native formats for tables and charts:** Submitting tables as Excel screenshots or importing charts as static images (rather than preparing in MS Word with embedded data) will require revision at final acceptance stage. Build tables and charts in Word from the start.

4. **Unclear applicability to retirement domain:** Even though the journal covers portfolio theory, pension design, and behavioral economics broadly, ensure the practical relevance to retirement income planning or pension systems is explicit. A purely theoretical contribution without retirement context may face alignment concerns.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

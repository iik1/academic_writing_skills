# Journal Writing Skill: Scottish Journal of Political Economy

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Wiley-Blackwell (on behalf of Scottish Economic Society)
- **Scope:** Quarterly journal covering microeconomics, macroeconomics, labor, monetary and industrial economics, international trade, finance, and applied econometrics. Welcomes both full-length articles and short notes.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/page/journal/14679485/homepage/forauthors.html (paywall-protected)
- **Format:** Online-only (since January 2022)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 1 full metadata (Gibbard 2025); 6+ article titles and scopes from IDEAS/RePEc; access limitations prevented full-text analysis. See Sources section.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
- Two submission types accepted: (1) full-length articles; (2) short notes
- Section structure follows standard FIELD_SKILL norms (Introduction, Methods/Data, Results, Conclusion). No mandatory sections specified beyond this baseline.

### Length
- Full-length articles: maximum 7,500 words (inclusive of all text)
- Short notes: maximum 2,000 words
- Overlong papers returned without consideration
- **DELTA: RELAXATION** — FIELD_SKILL implies longer manuscripts are acceptable at ABS 4* level. SJPE's 7,500-word ceiling is significantly tighter than top-tier norms, reflecting the ABS 2 status and online-only format.

### Abstract
- Required: unstructured abstract (format not specified in accessible guidelines)
- Maximum 100 words
- **DELTA: RELAXATION** — FIELD_SKILL does not specify abstract length. At 100 words, this is conservative relative to field practice at higher tiers.

### Keywords and Classification
- Required: list of keywords, each maximum 20 characters
- Required: Journal of Economic Literature (JEL) classification code(s)

### Citations
- Citation style: Author-year (Harvard/parenthetical format)
- Reference list: alphabetical order, double-spaced, separate sheet
- Journal titles: set out in full
- Books: publisher, publisher's town, and year of publication required
- **DELTA: CONFORMANCE** — Follows FIELD_SKILL norm for author-year citations. No deviation from field baseline.

### Headings
- Section numbering convention not specified in accessible guidelines
- **DELTA: FIELD DEFAULT** — No stated convention; likely follows FIELD_SKILL (Roman or Arabic numerals acceptable).

### Tables and Figures
- Can be submitted either embedded in main document or as separate files
- Notes for tables/figures should explain variable definitions, standard error clustering, and sample restrictions
- **DELTA: FIELD DEFAULT** — Aligns with FIELD_SKILL practice.

### Mathematical Content
- Full mathematical workings required for justification of each step (stated explicitly)
- Authors should conform to conventions in recent SJPE articles
- **DELTA: ENFORCEMENT** — Stated requirement; slightly more explicit than FIELD_SKILL about completeness of derivations.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Overlong papers (exceeding 7,500 words for articles, 2,000 for notes) returned without peer review consideration
- No other explicit prohibitions stated in accessible guidelines

### Submission Requirements
- Format: Microsoft Word (.doc/.docx) or LaTeX (.tex)
- Platform: Wiley Authors portal
- CRediT (Contribution Roles Taxonomy) information required during submission
- Peer review: single-blind
- Data/replication requirements: not specified in accessible guidelines

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Manuscript Length as a Design Constraint
The 7,500-word ceiling for full articles is substantially shorter than the typical working papers targeting ABS 4* outlets (which often run 12,000–18,000 words). This is a **RELAXATION** that shapes composition. Authors must compress exposition relative to field baseline.

### Short Notes as a Distinct Category
SJPE explicitly accepts short notes (≤2,000 words). This is a recognized format in economics but not emphasized in FIELD_SKILL. Short notes permit rapid publication of empirical findings, policy responses, or comments without the full development expected in articles. This is **PRACTICE COMMON IN TIER 2** but not a primary FIELD_SKILL focus.

### Abstract Length Specification
FIELD_SKILL does not stipulate abstract length. SJPE's 100-word maximum is conservative (some top-tier journals permit 150–200 words). This is a **RELAXATION** and implies tighter summarization discipline.

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Voice and person:** FIELD DEFAULT (first-person plural "we" is expected, consistent with FIELD_SKILL baseline)
- **Citation density and format:** FIELD DEFAULT (author-year citations, medium-to-low density in results/conclusion, aligns with ECON field norms)
- **Sentence and paragraph style:** FIELD DEFAULT (medium-to-long sentences, paragraph density, technical vocabulary density follow FIELD_SKILL)
- **Argumentation structure:** FIELD DEFAULT (introduction with motivating hook, research question, approach, preview of results, contributions, and roadmap expected; follows FIELD_SKILL)
- **Methods and identification:** FIELD DEFAULT (empirical strategy stated clearly; identification assumptions required and defended for causal claims)
- **Results reporting:** FIELD DEFAULT (tables primary vehicle for quantitative results; prose highlights key magnitudes; economic significance alongside statistical significance expected)
- **Discussion and conclusion norms:** FIELD DEFAULT (conclusion shorter than introduction, genuine synthesis, limitations acknowledged, brief future directions)
- **Hedging:** FIELD DEFAULT (low overall hedging intensity; clear claims preferred; hedging used only where justified by external validity, mechanism vs. reduced-form, or data limitations)
- **Tense conventions:** FIELD DEFAULT (past tense for data/procedures, present tense for model properties and theoretical results)

## Common Pitfalls
> Submission errors observed or likely given journal norms.

1. **Exceeding word limits:** Submitting articles >7,500 or short notes >2,000 words will result in desk rejection without peer review. Ruthless compression required—more than typical field practice.

2. **Underspecifying abstract:** At 100 words, every word counts. Avoid redundancy with introduction; focus on the gap, method, and quantified main finding. Vague directional claims are insufficient.

3. **Incomplete mathematical exposition:** If the paper contains theoretical results, include full derivations in appendix. Do not abbreviate with "see supplementary material" if the logic is non-standard.

4. **Omitting JEL codes:** Journal requires explicit JEL classification(s). This is not optional and may cause processing delays if missing.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

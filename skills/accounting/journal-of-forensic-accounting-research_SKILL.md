# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Forensic Accounting Research

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ACCOUNT
- **Publisher:** American Accounting Association
- **Scope:** Promotes excellence in forensic accounting research, teaching, and practice. Covers both fraud and non-fraud forensic accounting research, including expert witness testimony, valuation, individual damages (forensic economics), and commercial damages.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://aaahq.org/Research/Journals/Journal-of-Forensic-Accounting-Research/Guide-for-Authors
- **Field baseline:** account-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 8 article titles and abstracts retrieved from publications.aaahq.org/jfar (full text paywalled; access limitation documented below)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Canonical sections for empirical papers: Abstract, Introduction, Hypothesis Development/Theory, Data and Research Design/Methods, Results, Additional/Robustness Analysis, Discussion and Conclusion, References.

### Length
**Maximum 55 pages total** including references, figures, tables, and appendices. This is the AAA standard for general journals (FIELD_SKILL does not specify a page limit).

### Abstract
**Maximum 150 words**. Should concisely convey topic, methods, and findings. Appears on its own page before article text. **AI Disclosure Statement required:** Must immediately follow the abstract and precede the body text, specifying all generative AI tools used during manuscript preparation and research phases. This is **ENFORCED** — not mentioned in FIELD_SKILL.

### Citations
Author-date format (Chicago Manual of Style, 16th ed.). In-text: "(Author Year)" or "Author (Year)". Works with 3-5 authors: "(Jones, Brown, and Lee 2019)"; 6+ authors: "(Wilson et al. 2015)". Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.

### Headings
Four hierarchical levels following AAA convention:
1. **Centered, bold, all capitals** (Roman numerals: I, II, III)
2. **Flush left, bold, mixed case** (Roman numeral subsections: I.A, I.B)
3. **Flush left, bold, italic, mixed case**
4. **Paragraph indent, bold, lowercase**

Matches FIELD_SKILL norm. Webster's Collegiate Dictionary for spelling.

### Tables and Figures
- Arabic numbering with descriptive titles (FIELD_SKILL norm)
- Tables created in Word, Excel, or LaTeX (not keyboard spacing)
- Figures as separate files: EPS, PS, TIFF, PDF, JPEG, or PNG
- Line art: 600 dpi, black/white bitmap; halftones: 264 dpi, grayscale
- Alt-text for accessibility required (emerging best practice, not FIELD_SKILL baseline)

### Formatting
- **Font:** 12-point Times New Roman
- **Spacing:** Double-spaced
- **Margins:** 1-inch all sides
- **Page numbering:** Sequential
Matches FIELD_SKILL defaults.

### Explicit Prohibitions & Requirements
- **Plagiarism screening:** All submissions undergo CrossCheck (iThenticate) plagiarism detection — **ENFORCED HARD CONSTRAINT** not in FIELD_SKILL.
- **ORCID required:** All submitting authors must provide a valid ORCID identifier at submission — **ENFORCED HARD CONSTRAINT**.
- **Human subjects approval:** Studies involving human subjects (participants in lab experiments, survey respondents, interview subjects, or field interventions) require IRB approval documentation at submission with a compliance statement in the published manuscript. Standard in accounting research (mentioned in FIELD_SKILL methods section), but AAA journals enforce this explicitly.
- **Conflict of Interest:** Completed Author Conflict of Interest (COI) form mandatory. Co-author COI confirmations and mentor/mentee COI forms (using AAA template) required.
- **Replication materials:** Upon conditional acceptance, authors must provide a Data Description Disclosures list and all computer code used for analysis, sufficient for independent replication of findings. **ENFORCED HARD CONSTRAINT** — this is emerging practice in accounting but not universal in FIELD_SKILL baseline (which specifies data availability as "increasingly required").

### Submission Process
- Submissions via Editorial Manager at https://www.editorialmanager.com/aaajfar
- Title page submitted separately to maintain double-blind anonymity
- Double-blind peer review (minimum two reviewers + associate editor)
- Non-refundable submission fees: $75 (AAA members), $100 (non-members)
- Revisions returned to original reviewers; detailed responses to each comment required

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Acceptable Methodologies and Scope
JFAR explicitly welcomes both fraud and non-fraud forensic accounting research. The editorial policy states: "Appropriate methodologies for discovery-based research include, but are not limited to, laboratory studies, surveys, theory-based analyses, case studies, critical analyses, and studies using archival data." This is **slightly broader** than the quantitative-dominant tradition in FIELD_SKILL, which notes AOS as a qualitative outlier. JFAR encourages **balanced coverage** across forensic practice, basic research, and education — signaling openness to applied/practitioner-oriented work alongside traditional empirical research. This is a **GUIDELINE PRACTICE** (stated but not verified in article sample due to access limitations).

### Empirical Standards for Nonsignificant Results
The editorial policy explicitly states: "Studies in which the hypothesized effects are not found (i.e., studies that have 'nonsignificant' results) will be considered if they have been rigorously conducted, are theoretically sound, and demonstrate high construct validity, internal validity, and statistical power." This is a **PRACTICE EMPHASIS** absent from FIELD_SKILL. It signals tolerance for null results if methodologically rigorous, reducing publication bias. Not a formal constraint, but a signal of editorial values.

### AI Disclosure Requirement
**ENFORCED HARD CONSTRAINT** not in FIELD_SKILL. Authors must provide an "AI disclosure statement" immediately after the abstract, specifying all generative AI tools used during preparation and research phases, and comply with AAA authorship policies on AI use. This reflects AAA's 2024 guidance and is not yet a baseline field norm.

### Replication Code and Data Disclosure
Mandatory upon conditional acceptance (not routine in FIELD_SKILL baseline, which notes data availability as "increasingly required"). Authors must provide:
- Data Description Disclosures list
- All computer code used for analysis
- Sufficient detail for independent replication

This is **ENFORCED** and represents a strong commitment to transparency beyond the FIELD_SKILL baseline.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- **Voice and person:** FIELD_SKILL norm applies (first-person plural "we"; active voice dominant; high formality; confidence calibration to identification strength).
- **Sentence and paragraph style:** FIELD_SKILL norm (medium-to-long sentences; dense paragraphs; high technical density).
- **Argumentation and rhetorical moves:** FIELD_SKILL norm (CARS-based introduction; explicit gap framing; contribution positioning; roadmap optional).
- **Methods conventions:** FIELD_SKILL norm (past tense; high procedural detail; identification strategy emphasis; robustness expected; ethics/data statements required).
- **Results and reporting norms:** FIELD_SKILL norm (tables primary; prose interprets; statistical precision; parallel organization to hypotheses; null results not suppressed).
- **Discussion and conclusion norms:** FIELD_SKILL norm (merged or separate; restatement of question; summary of findings; explicit contributions; explicit limitations; future research optional; confident but not overreaching tone).
- **Citation and literature engagement:** FIELD_SKILL norm (author-date format; high density in introduction/hypothesis/conclusion; medium in methods/results; paraphrase norm; diplomatic disagreement framing).
- **Hedging and epistemic caution:** FIELD_SKILL norm (medium overall; direct verbs for well-identified results; softened language for interpretations; limitations acknowledgment expected; no hedging on regression coefficients or methodological claims).

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Missing AI disclosure statement** — Even if no AI tools were used, many journals now expect an explicit statement to that effect. JFAR requires this to appear immediately after the abstract, before body text.

2. **Incomplete replication materials** — Upon conditional acceptance, authors must provide not just data, but also all code used for analysis and a detailed Data Description Disclosures list. Submitting at acceptance without full replication code may delay publication.

3. **Insufficient construct validity or statistical power** — JFAR explicitly welcomes null results *if* the study is methodologically rigorous. Conversely, a nonsignificant result in a study with low power or unclear construct validity will be rejected. Authors should pre-specify power analyses and clearly justify construct measures, especially in experimental or survey designs.

4. **Overlooking forensic-practice grounding** — JFAR balances basic research with practice and education. Papers that are purely theoretical or disconnected from forensic accounting practice (valuation, damages, expert witness work, fraud detection) are less aligned with the journal's stated scope. Explicitly anchor empirical or theoretical contributions to forensic accounting's applied domains.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

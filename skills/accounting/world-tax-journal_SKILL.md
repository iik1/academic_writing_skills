# Journal Writing Skill: World Tax Journal

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ACCOUNT (Tax/International Taxation)
- **Publisher:** International Bureau of Fiscal Documentation (IBFD)
- **Scope:** Peer-reviewed scientific research in international, comparative, and regional taxation. Publishes tax law articles and welcomes interdisciplinary contributions from economics, accounting, and behavioral science addressing international taxation. Single-jurisdiction submissions rejected unless demonstrating equal application to other jurisdictions or clear international relevance.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.ibfd.org/authors-contributors/journal-articles-and-opinion-pieces
- **Field baseline:** account-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** Limited (4 comparable tax/international law journal articles; full WTJ articles paywalled; analysis based on guidelines and comparable journals)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

### Manuscript Length
**Enforced:** 10,000–28,000 words preferred; editorial discretion applied. This is notably narrower than many ACCOUNT field journals and strictly enforced at submission.

### Structure & Numbering
**Enforced:** Use consecutive Arabic numerals throughout (1, 2, 2.1, 2.1.1). Limited to four levels of numbered headings. Do not use auto-numbered headings in Word; enter numbers manually. Sections must start with **Introduction** and end with **Conclusion** (FIELD_SKILL permits merged sections; WTJ requires both Introduction and Conclusion as explicit structural bookends).

### Abstract
**Enforced:** Exactly 150 words maximum (approximately 1,000 characters including spaces). No undefined abbreviations or unspecified references permitted. Must stand alone without prior context. **Note:** This is slightly longer than the ~150-word FIELD_SKILL norm but the constraint is explicit and firmer here.

### Table of Contents
**Enforced:** Required at submission (unusual for ACCOUNT journals). Appears to list all numbered section headings and subsections. Not a standard FIELD_SKILL requirement.

### Headings
**Enforced:** Use fixed heading numbers; do not allow Word auto-numbering. Keep titles concise. Number consistently.

### Citations & Footnotes
**Enforced:** Chicago Manual of Style, 18th edition, Notes and Bibliography system (full footnotes, not author-date parenthetical). First mention requires full citation details; subsequent mentions use shortened format. A bibliography is required for editorial purposes but footnotes are the primary citation vehicle. **Delta:** FIELD_SKILL specifies author-date in-text citations; WTJ uses footnotes exclusively.

### Format & Formatting
**Enforced:** 
- Submit as Word file (.docx) using normal style with built-in bold/italic formatting (not manual formatting)
- British spelling (Oxford English Dictionary, 11th edition)
- Use italics for emphasis, not bold
- Lists: Use hyphens or numbered format (1), (2), (3)—no bullet points
- Cross-references: Cite by section/table/figure number, not page number

### Language
**Enforced:** Clear and correct English. IBFD conducts language review; authors may be asked to improve English or offered assistance.

### Anonymization
**Enforced:** Submitted manuscript must be anonymized for blind review.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Citation Format
**Delta: ENFORCED CONFLICT**
- **FIELD_SKILL:** Author-date in-text citations (parenthetical), with multiple citations separated by semicolons, alphabetically or chronologically ordered.
- **WTJ:** Chicago Manual of Style 18th edition, Notes and Bibliography (footnotes throughout). First mention full citation; subsequent mentions shortened. Bibliography required.
- **Practice:** This is a fundamental citation paradigm difference driven by the journal's legal/humanities tradition (Chicago Notes-Bib is standard in law). Accounting field journals typically use author-date. **Impact: HIGH.** Authors from ACCOUNT field background must completely restructure citations.

### Structural Framing
**Delta: GUIDELINE ONLY** (stated but optional in practice)
- **WTJ explicit requirement:** Introduction and Conclusion as distinct labeled sections, each with its own heading number.
- **FIELD_SKILL:** Permits merged "Discussion and Conclusion" sections; does not mandate standalone Introduction heading.
- **Implication:** WTJ structure is slightly more rigid. Articles must clearly delineate these sections.

### Spelling and Emphasis
**Delta: RELAXATION**
- **FIELD_SKILL:** Does not specify spelling convention (typically assumes APA/US English in US-based journals).
- **WTJ:** Explicitly requires British spelling (Oxford 11th edition) and forbids bold for emphasis (italics only).
- **Practice:** Lower-tier journals sometimes relax formatting precision; WTJ applies this consistently.

### Lists and Formatting
**Delta: RELAXATION**
- **FIELD_SKILL:** Does not specify list formatting conventions.
- **WTJ:** Numbered format (1), (2), (3) or hyphens; no bullet points.
- **Practice:** Reflects legal writing convention.

### Article Scope
**Delta: RELAXATION** (relative to some ACCOUNT journals)
- **WTJ accepts:** Single-jurisdiction submissions if demonstrating equal or similar application to other jurisdictions **or** clear international relevance to an international audience.
- **Implication:** Lower barrier than some higher-tier journals; acceptable for focused jurisdictional studies provided international angle is explicit.

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Voice and person:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. First-person plural ("we") dominates; active voice throughout.
- **Formality and register:** Clinical, precise register consistent with FIELD_SKILL accounting field norms.
- **Sentence and paragraph structure:** Dense, complex sentences with embedded clauses; medium-to-long length sentences match FIELD_SKILL.
- **Argumentation structure:** Introduction, hypothesis/theory development, methods, results, discussion/conclusion architecture stable with FIELD_SKILL.
- **Methods conventions:** Procedural detail, past tense, identification strategy discussion, robustness checks follow FIELD_SKILL norms (where applicable in tax law context).
- **Hedging and epistemic caution:** Medium level; well-identified claims stated directly; mechanistic interpretations softened with "suggest," "indicate," "consistent with." Matches FIELD_SKILL.
- **Discussion and Conclusion:** Merged or separate both acceptable; contributions explicitly stated; limitations acknowledged. Consistent with FIELD_SKILL.

## Common Pitfalls for ACCOUNT-Field Authors

1. **Citation format inertia:** Author-date citations are deeply embedded in ACCOUNT training. WTJ's Chicago Notes-Bib footnotes require complete rewriting of citations and text flow. Reviewers immediately flag incomplete citations and author-date format as non-compliance.

2. **Abstract overfitting:** The 150-word limit is firm. Abstracts with abbreviations or forward references to footnoted material are desk-rejected. Ensure the abstract is self-contained and defined.

3. **Missing table of contents:** This is a submission requirement, not optional. Omission triggers desk rejection or major revision requests.

4. **Word count inflation:** The 28,000-word ceiling is tighter than some top-tier ACCOUNT journals, which allow 35,000+. Manuscripts exceeding this without editorial pre-approval face rejection or demand compression.

5. **Ambiguous section numbering:** Auto-numbered headings in Word can drift during editing. Reviewers expect fixed, manually entered numbers. This is flagged as sloppy manuscript preparation.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Russian Journal of Economics

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Non-profit partnership "Voprosy Ekonomiki" (open access via Pensoft/ARPHA platform)
- **Scope:** Quarterly open-access journal publishing high-quality research in all fields of economics related to policy issues. Original research on the Russian economy, economic policy, and institutional reform is prominent, though the journal welcomes submissions in all areas of applied and theoretical economics, especially those with policy implications.
- **Review:** Double-blind peer review; expected ~4 weeks from submission to publication
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.econorus.org/enguidelines.phtml
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 4 (section structures and metadata from https://rujec.org; full text access limited by paywall; abstracts and guidelines extracted from journal website)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
- Sections must be numbered with decimal notation permitted (e.g., 2.1, 3.2)
- Required front matter: author names, institution affiliations, abstract, keywords (5-8), JEL classification codes
- Standard ordering: Title → Authors/Affiliations → Abstract → Keywords → JEL Codes → Numbered sections → References
- Optional types: Research article, Review article, Book review
- Appendices are permitted and expected for supplementary material

### Length
- **Full articles:** Maximum 40,000 printed characters (including spaces) — approximately 6,000–8,000 words depending on formatting
- **Notes/Comments/Critical bibliographic reviews:** Maximum 20,000 characters
- No minimum word count specified

### Abstract
- **Required:** 150–200 words in both Russian and English
- **Format:** Unstructured prose (not explicitly structured with headings such as Objective, Methods, Results, Conclusion)
- **Must include:** Keywords (5–8 words and word expressions) and JEL classification codes below the abstract

### Citations
- **Format:** Author-date (in-text), following Harvard style conventions
- **In-text examples:** "Ivanov (1999), p. 72" or "Sidorov, Petrov (2003), Chapter 3"
- **Reference list:** Alphabetically ordered at end of article; Russian-language sources listed before foreign-language sources
- **General practice:** Paraphrase is standard; direct quotations are rare

### Headings
- Sections of the article must be numbered
- Two-level heading hierarchy is permissible (primary section, subsection with decimal notation)
- No specification of heading typeface or size (follows publisher defaults)

### Tables and Figures
- **Placement:** Tables and figures placed in document at logical points (not necessarily at end)
- **Numbering and captions:** "Table 1. [Title above]" or "Image 1. [Title above]"
- **Data sources:** Must appear beneath each table
- **Figure captions:** Optional but recommended

### Format Specifications
- **File format:** .doc (Microsoft Word) or .tex (TeX) acceptable
- **Font:** Times New Roman, size 12
- **PDF:** Required for peer review (anonymous; no author identification)

### Explicit Prohibitions
- No specification of explicit language/tone prohibitions in available guidelines
- Double-blind review requires anonymous submission (author names removed from PDF)

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Citation Format: Character Constraints Override English Default
**ENFORCED.** FIELD_SKILL specifies author-year citation (parenthetical or in-text) as near-universal in economics. RJE guidelines confirm this is the norm ("Ivanov (1999)"), BUT with one constraint: 40,000 printed characters is significantly shorter than the typical ABS 4*/4 economics paper (which commonly exceed 10,000 words or ~60,000 characters). This hard constraint on length means that citation density and literature engagement must adapt proportionally. A paper targeting 6,000–8,000 words cannot sustain the introduction-heavy literature review expected in FIELD_SKILL at the ABS 4*/4 level. **Expect proportionally fewer citations per section** — not a stylistic deviation, but a mechanical consequence of the length limit.

### Abstract Length
**RELAXATION.** FIELD_SKILL makes no statement about abstract length (it is outside FIELD_SKILL's scope). RJE specifies 150–200 words in both Russian and English. This is shorter than the typical ABS 4* abstract (which often exceed 250 words) but aligns with many ABS 2–3 journals. No conflict with FIELD_SKILL; documents a journal-specific constraint.

### Structure
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Articles observed (via section headings: Introduction, Methods/Scope, Results, Discussion, Conclusion) align with the empirical paper structure described in FIELD_SKILL. Numbered sections are standard in both FIELD_SKILL and RJE practice.

### Voice and Register
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. No explicit requirement stated in guidelines; section headings visible in sampled articles use neutral, direct language consistent with FIELD_SKILL's expectation of "we" and active voice.

### Statistical Reporting
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. No deviations observed or specified.

### Citation Density by Section
See "Citation Format" delta above. Overall citation density is likely lower due to length constraint, but section-level norms (high in introduction, low in conclusion) are expected to hold proportionally.

### Hedging and Epistemic Caution
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. No explicit guidelines; expected to match FIELD_SKILL's emphasis on clear claims defended through methods and robustness, not verbal hedging.

### Conclusion Length Relative to Introduction
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. FIELD_SKILL specifies conclusion is shorter than introduction; RJE's 40,000-character limit applies to entire paper, enforcing this proportionality automatically.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Exceeding character limit due to literature engagement.** The 40,000-character ceiling (roughly 6,000–8,000 words) is significantly tighter than leading economics journals. Writers accustomed to ABS 4*/4 standards often produce introduction-heavy drafts with full literature reviews that exceed this limit. **Mitigation:** Plan to compress the introduction and subordinate literature survey to a core gap-and-contribution statement within ~800–1000 words. Integrate minimal prior-work citations into relevant sections rather than clustering them in a standalone Related Literature section (which is easier to cut than dispersed citations).

2. **Non-compliance with Russian/English bilingual abstract requirement.** Submissions in English may omit the Russian abstract, delaying review or triggering desk rejection. **Mitigation:** If submitting in English, provide a professional Russian translation of the abstract (or arrange for journal translation service). If the journal offers translation support, confirm coverage of this cost in advance.

3. **Failure to include JEL classification codes.** Guidelines explicitly require JEL codes below the abstract; these are often overlooked by authors unfamiliar with the journal. **Mitigation:** Assign 3–5 primary JEL codes before submission (e.g., C38 for econometric methods, E24 for labour economics). The journal's scope statement and sample articles indicate these are mandatory, not optional.

4. **Anonymous PDF not truly anonymous.** The guidelines require an anonymous PDF for peer review. Authors sometimes fail to remove author names from headers, footers, metadata, or figure captions. **Mitigation:** Use Word's "Track Changes" > "Final" mode or export PDF from a guest account, then check all text, metadata, and image properties for identifying information.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: China Journal of Accounting Studies

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ACCOUNT
- **Publisher:** Taylor & Francis
- **Scope:** Peer-reviewed open access journal and the official international research journal of the Accounting Society of China. Accepts empirical, analytical, experimental, and qualitative case research on accounting, finance, auditing, corporate governance, and financial management across China and emerging markets.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.tandfonline.com/journals/rcja20 (access restricted; extracted from public sources)
- **Field baseline:** account-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 4 (abstracts and metadata from 2025 articles: "Unified tax incentive policies, tax burden fairness, and corporate investment efficiency"; "Business-finance integration, tax avoidance and tax risk"; "Can state-owned equity participation promote private enterprises' fulfilment of social responsibilities?"; "Is the pilot policy for carbon emission trading beneficial for ESG disclosure?")

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

### Structure
**ENFORCED** — Recommended organization (not strictly enforced at submission): title page, abstract, keywords, introduction, methods/hypothesis development, results, discussion, acknowledgments, conflict of interest statement, references, appendices, tables, and figures. Consistent with FIELD_SKILL baseline but no explicit section-numbering scheme enforced.

### Length
**ENFORCED** — Typical paper should not exceed 9,000 words inclusive of abstract, tables, references, figure/table captions, footnotes, and endnotes. This represents a tighter constraint than many FIELD_SKILL journals (e.g., 20,000+ in top-tier).

### Abstract
**ENFORCED** — Unstructured abstract of approximately 150 words (not structured with labeled subsections). Followed by 4–6 keywords. No keyword format specification provided; standard FIELD_SKILL keyword practice applies.

### Language and Spelling
**ENFORCED** — British English spelling throughout (e.g., "organisation," "analyse"). American English is not acceptable. Single quotation marks used except where nested quotations appear. Long quotations (40+ words) should be indented without quotation marks.

### Citations
**ENFORCED** — Author-date format per Paperpile: (Author, Year) for single/two authors, (Author et al., Year) for three+. Bibliography format: Last Name, Initial(s). (Year). Article title. *Journal Title*, Volume(Issue), page range. No strict reference formatting requirements at submission; essential elements (authors, titles, year, publication info) suffice for review. Digital Object Identifiers (DOIs) encouraged.

### AI Disclosure
**ENFORCED** — Authors must declare use of generative AI and AI-assisted technologies in manuscript preparation by adding a new section entitled "Declaration of Generative AI and AI-assisted technologies in the writing process" at the end of the manuscript, before the References list.

### File Format at Submission
**RELAXATION** — Single Word or PDF file accepted for refereeing; full formatting enforced only at revision/acceptance stage. This contrasts with some FIELD_SKILL journals requiring camera-ready copy on submission.

### Article Processing Charges
**FIELD DEFAULT** — No publication charges. The Accounting Society of China covers all APCs; authors face no cost for open access publication.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Word Limit Strictness
**ENFORCED** — The 9,000-word cap (inclusive) is a material constraint not present in top-tier field baseline. FIELD_SKILL journals typically allow 25,000–40,000 words. This ABS 1 rating reflects lower-tier norms. Authors must be surgical with hypothesis count, results reporting, and discussion depth.

### Abstract Format (Unstructured)
**ENFORCED** — Unstructured abstract (150 words) differs from FIELD_SKILL journals, which often default to unstructured as well. No delta in practice, but explicitly enforced here where some journals are flexible.

### Spelling Consistency Requirement
**ENFORCED** — Explicit British spelling mandate. FIELD_SKILL baseline does not mandate either variant; top-tier journals typically accommodate either if consistent. This journal enforces one throughout.

### Publication Timeline and Open Access Model
**FIELD DEFAULT** — Full open access publishing (no paywall) and zero APC for authors is increasingly standard at FIELD_SKILL level (e.g., China Journal of Accounting Research, also open access) so not a unique delta. Included for clarity.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Exceeding word limit.** The 9,000-word maximum (inclusive of all elements) is strictly enforced. Papers exceeding this are desk-rejected. Common mistakes: overly long hypothesis sections (field baseline norm allows extended development; here, hypothesis development must be tightly written), verbose results narratives, and lengthy robustness sections. Plan for ~150 words abstract, ~800–1000 words introduction, ~1200–1400 words hypothesis/theory, ~1500–1800 words methods, ~2500–3000 words results, ~1200–1400 words discussion, ~600–800 words references. Numbers are approximate; precision in planning is required.

2. **American English or mixed spelling.** British spelling is mandatory. Reviewers flag "organisation" vs. "organization" inconsistencies and may desk-reject if pervasive.

3. **Missing AI disclosure.** Omission of the "Declaration of Generative AI and AI-assisted technologies in the writing process" section (if AI was used in any preparation stage) is a procedural ground for desk rejection or delays.

4. **Over-formatting at submission.** Submitting a fully formatted, camera-ready manuscript at the submission stage is unnecessary and may slow processing. A plain single Word/PDF file is preferred; formatting is applied in-house only at revision.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

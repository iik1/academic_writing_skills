# Journal Writing Skill: Management Accounting Frontiers

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ACCOUNT
- **Publisher:** Institute of Certified Management Accountants (CMA Australia)
- **Scope:** Theoretical and empirical research on management accounting, broadly defined. Publishes research articles, literature reviews, and normative pieces intended for both academics and practitioners.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://cmaaustralia.edu.au/publications/maf/maf-guide-for-authors/ (access restricted; guidelines available on request)
- **Field baseline:** account-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 (Nasca et al. 2024, Laing 2018, Liyanage & Gooneratne 2021, So 2020, Sheehan 2024, Sridharan & Tse editorials 2019–2023)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Numbered sections (Arabic numerals: 1, 2, 3, etc.). Observed structure in articles:
- Section 1: Introduction
- Section 2: Conceptual foundations / prior research / background
- Section 3: Hypotheses or research questions (or methodology in non-hypothesis-driven papers)
- Section 4: Data and methodology / research design
- Section 5: Results / findings
- Section 6: Discussion and implications, limitations, future research

**ABS 1 note:** Structure relaxation from FIELD_SKILL (less emphasis on mechanical hypothesis enumeration in intro; discussion and conclusion are merged as standard, not optional).

### Length
Not explicitly specified in accessible guidelines. Observed article lengths: 12–50 pages including tables, references, and appendices. No stated maximum word count found; assume standard journal range (6,000–10,000 words for shorter pieces; 12,000–15,000 for empirical studies).

### Abstract
Format not explicitly stated. Articles appear to include a brief abstract (likely 100–150 words, unstructured) followed by keywords. No JEL codes observed in spot-checked samples.

### Citations
**Harvard style (author-date) exclusively.** In-text: "(Author Year)" or "Author (Year)". References list at end of paper. Only works cited in the text are included. Multiple citations separated by semicolons. If author name is mentioned in text, surname need not be repeated in citation.

### Headings
**Numbered section headings:** Use Arabic numerals (1. Introduction, 2. Background, etc.). Subsections use decimal notation (e.g., 2.1, 2.2). Heading case follows journal template (title case typical).

### Tables and Figures
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Tables carry panel labels, variable definitions in notes, and explicit reporting of statistical clustering or other methodological choices. Placed after text or at end of manuscript.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Submission implies content has not been published elsewhere or is under consideration by another journal.
- All submissions undergo double-blind peer review.
- Contact editor-in-chief for submission: [email protected]

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Manuscript Length and Scope
**RELAXATION — ABS 1 norm:** Accepts shorter, more applied pieces (12–50 pages total, including all apparatus) compared to FIELD_SKILL baseline (typical ABS 4*/4 archival papers run 40–60+ pages). This reflects the journal's mixed academic/practitioner audience and lower-tier status.

### Section Structure and Formalism
**STRUCTURAL DEVIATION — PRACTICE ONLY:** While FIELD_SKILL encodes flexible section ordering (particularly for theoretical/analytical papers), MAF consistently uses numbered sections (1, 2, 3, etc.), suggesting a stricter structural template. However, specific section naming is not rigidly enforced across article types (conceptual papers may use different section 2–3 headings than empirical studies).

### Audience and Tone
**RELAXATION — ABS 1 characteristic:** Writing is expected to remain "rigorous yet preferably intelligible to a wide audience of academics and, where appropriate, practitioners and graduate students." This is less stringent than FIELD_SKILL baseline, which assumes a specialist accounting researcher as primary audience. MAF explicitly permits a somewhat more accessible register when discussing implications for practice.

### Voice and Confidence Calibration
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm with no observed deviation. First-person plural ("we") dominates; well-identified findings warrant direct declarative statements; mechanistic interpretations use hedging.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Over-specializing the writing for academics:** The journal explicitly welcomes practitioner-oriented implications and examples. Avoid writing as if the reader is a technical specialist in accounting methodology; illustrate applied relevance.

2. **Rigid section naming:** While numbered sections are expected, exact section titles (e.g., "Hypothesis Development" vs. "Conceptual Foundations") vary by article type. Review published articles in your subfield within the journal to match conventions.

3. **Omitting limitations:** Reviewers expect an explicit limitations paragraph. Understatement is noticed negatively. Name specific threats to validity: sample composition, construct validity concerns, generalizability bounds, temporal scope, etc.

4. **Incorrect reference format in Harvard style:** Ensure author names, dates, and page numbers are consistent. Journals sometimes enforce strict alphabetical ordering and specific punctuation (e.g., commas vs. semicolons). Review the "Guide for Authors" for current house style before submission.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: China Economic Review

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Elsevier
- **Scope:** Original research works on the economy of China and its relation to the world economy; quantitative and analytical papers dealing with institutional change, policy and performance of the Chinese economy; comparative studies of China's development with other countries encouraged. Official journal of The Chinese Economists Society.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/china-economic-review/publish/guide-for-authors (paywalled; extracted via public search methods)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 10 articles from IDEAS/RePEc Volume 98 (2026), including: Imports of key capital goods and firms' span of production stages (Zhang et al.); Vocational high school to higher education transitions (Rao et al.); Grade retention abolition impacts (Liang & Zhang); Political shocks and semiconductor spillovers (Cai et al.); Green M&A and green exports (Long & Fan); Trade war consequences (Cheng et al.); SME bankruptcy reforms (Di & Mou); Haze and crime (Han et al.); Housing price administrative policy (Qiu & Wang); Labor ideology under tech shocks (Ding et al.)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Numbered sections and subsections required: 1, 1.1, 1.1.1 (subsections), then 1.2, etc. Follows FIELD_SKILL norm of Introduction, substantive body sections, and Conclusion, with optional Background, Methods/Empirical Strategy, Results, and Robustness sections. No journal-specified deviations from FIELD_SKILL structure observed.

### Length
No explicit word limit stated in accessible guidelines. Abstract limit is stated as 250 words (see below).

### Abstract
**ENFORCED, GUIDELINE-SPECIFIC:** Mandatory and distinct from article body. Maximum 250 words (FIELD_SKILL does not specify a word limit for abstracts). Must be concise and factual, standing alone—often presented separately from the article. Should briefly state: purpose of research, principal results, major conclusions. Avoid references where possible; if essential, cite author(s) and year(s) only. Avoid non-standard or uncommon abbreviations; define all abbreviations at first mention.

### Keywords
**ENFORCED, GUIDELINE-SPECIFIC:** 1 to 7 keywords required. Single-word keywords only—no multi-word phrases using "and" or "of". Avoid abbreviations unless firmly established in the field.

### Article Highlights
**ENFORCED, GUIDELINE-SPECIFIC:** Required at submission. 3 to 5 bullet points, each maximum 85 characters including spaces. Captures key takeaways in compressed form.

### Author Biography
**GUIDELINE ONLY:** Maximum 100 words per author. Submitted alongside manuscript.

### Citations
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Author-year (name and year in parentheses) format; e.g., (Wang & Yuan, 2014) for two authors; (Sobol et al., 2000) for three or more. Bibliography references follow journal title in italics, volume, issue, and page numbers: "Robinson, C. P. (2004). Revisiting the Baruch Plan. *Nature*, 432(7016), 441–442." No journal-specific deviations from FIELD_SKILL citation density or paraphrase norms observed in sample.

### Headings
Numbered headings per structure requirement above. FIELD_SKILL norm (Roman or Arabic numerals both acceptable) applies. No journal-specific heading-naming conventions observed.

### Tables and Figures
**ENFORCED, TECHNICAL:** Editable source files required—not PDF. Tables and figures in appendices use separate numbering: Table A.1, Fig. A.1, etc. Standard FIELD_SKILL norms on prose-table boundary, economic significance reporting, and table notes apply.

### File Format and Submission
**ENFORCED, TECHNICAL:** Editable source files only: .doc/.docx (single-column layout) or .tex. PDF is not acceptable as a source file. All figures, tables, and text graphics must be in editable format. Submission includes one designated corresponding author with email, postal address, and phone numbers. Nonrefundable submission fee: USD $100 (reduced for Chinese Economists Society members).

### Review Process
**ENFORCED:** Double-blind (anonymized) review. Editorial assessment for suitability precedes external expert review (minimum one reviewer).

### Explicit Prohibitions
No language/tone or writing-style prohibitions stated in accessible guidelines beyond standard academic integrity norms.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Relaxations vs. FIELD_SKILL
**Abstract.** FIELD_SKILL does not specify a word limit for abstracts; China Economic Review mandates 250 words maximum. This is a constraint not typical of FIELD_SKILL norms.

**Submission overhead.** FIELD_SKILL does not address submission fees or article highlights. China Economic Review requires both: a USD $100 nonrefundable submission fee and 3–5 mandatory article highlights (85 characters each). This is a RELAXATION compared to top-tier norms (ABS 4*/4 journals do not typically charge submission fees or mandate highlights in such compressed form).

**Citation format specification.** FIELD_SKILL states author-year is "near-universal" in ECON; China Economic Review makes this explicit in guidelines and codifies the two-author vs. three+ author notation (FIELD_SKILL does not detail this). This is GUIDELINE-SPECIFIC but not a delta in practice.

### No Deltas Observed
**Voice, person, and register:** Spot-check of 10 recent article titles and available abstracts shows no deviation from FIELD_SKILL first-person plural, active voice, or formality norms. Empirical papers in the sample (Imports of key capital goods, Haze and crime, Housing prices, Vocational education) follow FIELD_SKILL structure and descriptive language.

**Statistical reporting:** No deviation from FIELD_SKILL norms on quantitative specificity, economic vs. statistical significance, or null-result handling observed in available abstracts.

**Citation density:** Spot-check abstracts show FIELD_SKILL-aligned citation density (medium to high in introduction-like context, low in abstract summary).

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Exceeding 250-word abstract limit.** Abstracts longer than 250 words will be rejected at desk review stage. Discipline required.

2. **Failing to include article highlights.** The 3–5 bullet points (max 85 characters each) are mandatory for acceptance. This is an overhead not present in FIELD_SKILL norms and easy to overlook.

3. **PDF as source file.** Submitting PDF instead of .doc/.docx or .tex will be rejected; editable source files are non-negotiable.

4. **Multi-word keywords.** Keywords must be single words; "institutional change" would be rejected in favor of "institutions" and "change" as separate keywords (if within the 1–7 limit).

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

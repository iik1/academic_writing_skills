# Journal Writing Skill: South Asian Journal of Human Resources Management

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** HRM&EMP
- **Publisher:** SAGE Publications Ltd
- **Scope:** Peer-reviewed scholarly outlet for publications on human resource management in and out of South Asia (Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan, Sri Lanka). The journal is the only outlet focusing exclusively on HRM in South Asia.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/author-instructions/hrm
- **Field baseline:** hrm-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 8 (via search results, abstracts, and article summaries from 2024-2025 issues; full-text access limited by paywall but front-matter, abstracts, and publication metadata examined)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Research articles follow standard IMRaD structure: introduction, literature review (may be merged with introduction or integrated with theory development), methods, results/findings, discussion, and conclusion. Acknowledgements, ethics statements, and author contribution statements are standard back matter. The journal accepts conceptual/theoretical papers, empirical research papers (quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-methods), research notes, interviews, case studies, and book reviews.

### Length
- **Research articles:** 6,000–9,000 words
- **Commentaries, essays, research notes, case studies:** 2,000–5,000 words
- **Book reviews:** 1,000–2,000 words
- Word counts exclude abstract and reference list (inferred standard SAGE practice)

### Abstract
Unstructured abstract of 150–200 words. Must concisely state: purpose of research, country of study, methodology, theory used, major findings, and conclusions. Placed between title and main body.

### Keywords
4–6 keywords required. Placed after abstract.

### Citations
**Citation style:** APA. Every in-text citation must have a corresponding reference list entry and vice versa; identical spelling and year required for matching. See FIELD_SKILL baseline for paragraph-level citation density and engagement norms; this journal does not specify deviations.

### Headings
Manuscript must have clear heading levels. No specific style requirements stated in guidelines; inferred to follow FIELD_SKILL conventions (numbered or hierarchical structure acceptable).

### Tables and Figures
- Table number and caption appear on the same line above the table. Table number in bold, caption unbold, both title case.
- Column headings in title case, unbold.
- Row headings in sentence case, unbold.
- No bold or special characters in table cells unless explained; if present, author must provide explanation of significance.
- Figures must be 300 dpi resolution.
- **Placement:** All tables and figures presented together at end of article (not inline). Number tables and figures separately (Table 1, Figure 1, etc.), not by reference or placement. All tables and figures must be cited in text.
- Source attribution required for all tables and figures regardless of permission status.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Manuscripts must not be previously published or under review elsewhere.
- Self-identifying citations in anonymised review should be either removed or left blank.
- Preprint citations are discouraged; authors should update citations to peer-reviewed versions when available.

### Contextualization Requirement (Enforced)
Research must demonstrate "sufficient effort to embed or develop key constructs within the work, employment, management, organizational context or specific human resource issues in South Asia" and must be contextualized within one or more South Asian countries (Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan, Sri Lanka). Theoretically developed hypotheses/propositions required; significant discussion of HR policy/practice insights and implications for practitioners required.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### South Asian Contextualization (ENFORCED)
This journal explicitly requires that research be contextualized within South Asia and display "sufficient effort to embed or develop key constructs within the work, employment, management, organizational context or specific human resource issues in South Asia." This is a mandatory gate-keeping criterion not mentioned in FIELD_SKILL. Submissions framed as generic HRM research without explicit South Asian grounding are desk-rejected. This is a RELAXATION in the sense that it removes the journal from generic HRM positioning, but enforcement is strict.

### Abstract Structure (ENFORCED)
FIELD_SKILL does not specify abstract format. This journal mandates unstructured, 150–200 word abstracts. The specificity (unstructured, not structured; specific word band) is journal-specific and must be observed.

### Citation Style Specificity (GUIDELINE ONLY)
FIELD_SKILL does not specify citation style. This journal specifies APA. The wider field permits Harvard and other variants; this journal does not. APA is ENFORCED in submission and compliance is verified by copy-editing staff pre-publication.

### Word Limits (ENFORCED)
FIELD_SKILL does not specify word limits. This journal specifies 6,000–9,000 for research articles. Manuscripts exceeding this range are desk-rejected. This is more restrictive than many ABS 4-level journals (which often permit longer papers) and is ABS 1-appropriate (lower-tier journals are more prescriptive about length to manage editorial load).

### Table and Figure Placement (ENFORCED)
FIELD_SKILL does not specify table/figure placement. This journal mandates end-of-article placement (not inline). This is a notable delta from modern journal practice (most accept inline figures) and reflects an older house style. Compliance is checked during copyediting.

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Typical article structure (IMRaD framework):** FIELD DEFAULT. Journal follows standard HRM framing: introduction, lit review, methods, results, discussion, conclusion. No deviation from baseline.
- **Voice and register:** FIELD DEFAULT. Formal, impersonal register with first-person plural permitted in introduction/methods/discussion. Narrative and interpretive prose tolerated. No evidence of journal-specific voice preferences in abstracts or available prose.
- **Sentence and paragraph style:** FIELD DEFAULT. Medium to long sentences, frequent subordination for qualification and citation support. Signposting language common at section transitions. No evidence of deviation.
- **Argumentation (CARS-like structure, explicit contribution statements):** FIELD DEFAULT. Introductions establish problem, gap, and contribution; explicit statements of contribution observed in available abstracts.
- **Methods conventions:** FIELD DEFAULT. Quantitative: sample, measures, estimation strategy, robustness checks. Qualitative: sampling, data collection, analytic approach, reflexivity. Ethical approval statements observed. No deviation.
- **Results and reporting norms:** FIELD DEFAULT. Quantitative papers narrate table findings in prose; qualitative papers present thematic findings with illustrative quotes. Null findings reported straightforwardly. No journal-specific deviation observed.
- **Discussion and conclusion norms:** FIELD DEFAULT. Discussion revisits contributions, distinguishes theoretical from practical implications, closes with limitations and future directions. Practical implications emphasized. No journal-specific deviation.
- **Citation density and engagement patterns:** FIELD DEFAULT. Highest in intro/lit review, lowest in results, rising again in discussion. Paraphrase preferred over direct quotation of prior scholarship. Disagreement framed diplomatically. No evidence of journal deviation.
- **Hedging and epistemic caution:** FIELD DEFAULT. Moderate to high hedging, especially in discussion/conclusion. Causal language qualified in observational designs. Qualitative papers hedge generalizability explicitly. No journal-specific deviation observed.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Weak South Asian grounding:** Submitting HRM research framed as globally applicable without explicit contextualization within one or more South Asian countries. The journal emphasizes unique contextual challenges and opportunities in South Asia (labour-intensive manufacturing, informal-sector employment, emerging economies, regulatory heterogeneity). Generic "we tested AMO theory in a sample of 200 Indian employees" without discussion of Indian labour-market context, regulatory environment, or regional HR practice differences is likely to receive a desk reject or major revise.

2. **Abstract length and format violations:** Submitting structured abstracts (background/methods/results/conclusion sections) or abstracts exceeding 200 words. The journal specifies unstructured narrative format at 150–200 words. Automated systems may flag overlength or over-structured abstracts for desk rejection.

3. **Inline tables and figures:** Placing tables and figures within body text rather than at the end. The journal house style is end-of-article placement. Copyeditors will move all figures to the end, which can disrupt draft review feedback. Author-formatted inline figures signal unfamiliarity with journal requirements.

4. **Citation style inconsistency:** Mixing Harvard and APA in-text formats, using et al. in italics, or retaining author footnote citations (e.g., "ibid.") instead of full author-year format in APA. The journal enforces strict APA compliance during copyediting, and reviewer assessment of manuscripts begins with spotcheck of formatting. Non-compliance signals lower professionalism and may bias reviewer perception.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

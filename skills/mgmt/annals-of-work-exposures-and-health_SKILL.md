# Journal Writing Skill: Annals of Work Exposures and Health

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN (note: this is a discipline mismatch — the journal is an occupational hygiene / exposure science / occupational health journal, not a management journal; see below)
- **Publisher:** Oxford University Press, official journal of the British Occupational Hygiene Society (editorially independent)
- **Scope:** Publishes original research on assessment and control of workplace exposures and the associations between specific work exposures and health outcomes, spanning traditional occupational hygiene, exposure science, and occupational epidemiology.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://academic.oup.com/annweh/pages/author-guidelines (and https://academic.oup.com/annweh/pages/online_submission_instructions)
- **Field baseline:** mgmt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 (abstract/front-matter level): "Quantifying and improving performance of a low-cost PM sensor used for occupational hygiene applications" (Ann Work Expo Health 70(2), wxag001); "Letter to the Editor 'On estimating future burdens'" (69(9):970-971); "Psychosocial risks and ethical implications of technology: considerations for decent work" (69(4):360); plus the Vol 68 Issue 3 table of contents surveyed for structural/section-type patterns (Editorial, Commentary, 7 Original Articles, Short Communication, Letter to the Editor).

## Important Caveat on Field Fit
This journal is a natural/occupational-health-science outlet (exposure assessment, industrial hygiene, epidemiology), not a management/ethics/CSR journal. The FIELD_SKILL baseline (management writing conventions: hypotheses/propositions, CARS introductions, theory-driven contribution framing) largely does not apply here. Rather than force a delta-only comparison to an ill-fitting baseline, this skill documents the journal's actual observed conventions directly, flagging where FIELD_SKILL norms are simply inapplicable.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
IMRaD: Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion, and Conclusions, "unless these are clearly inappropriate." No hypothesis/proposition framework of the kind FIELD_SKILL describes; papers are framed around an exposure-assessment or health-association research question, not theory-testing.

### Length
- Original Research / Review Articles: under 5,000 words (excludes abstract, references, tables, figures), max 6 tables/figures.
- Short Communications: under 1,500 words, max 2 tables/figures.
- Commentaries: under 2,000 words.
- Editorials: under 1,500 words.
- Letters to the Editor: under 1,000 words.
These are far shorter than the 10,000-18,000-word management-article lengths in FIELD_SKILL's Section 10 table.

### Abstract
Not required to be structured, but may be organized using the paper's own headings (Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion, Conclusions). No stated word limit. FIELD_SKILL's 150-250 word unstructured-abstract norm does not apply; abstracts observed here are more variable in length and commonly signpost each IMRaD section within a single unstructured block.

### Citations
CSE Scientific Style and Format, 8th edition, **name-year system** — not APA. Reference lists are alphabetical; all author names listed unless more than ten, in which case truncation rules apply. This is a direct override of FIELD_SKILL's APA (Author, Year) convention.

### Headings
Standard IMRaD headings expected; flexibility only where "clearly inappropriate" (e.g., Letters, Commentaries, Editorials use free-form structure).

### Tables and Figures
- Numbered consecutively with informative captions.
- High-resolution requirements: 600 dpi (author guidelines) / 1200 dpi (submission instructions) for line drawings, 300 dpi for color/half-tone.
- Accepted formats: .tif, .jpg, .gif, .bmp, .eps.
- Alt text descriptions required for all images (accessibility requirement not present in FIELD_SKILL).
- Color figures free of charge.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- No footnotes to text.
- No ghost or gift authorship.
- No undisclosed AI use.
- No image manipulation without disclosure.
- Manuscripts may not be shared on social media before publication.
- Revised manuscripts must be uploaded as .doc/.rtf/.tex, not .pdf.
- Funding Statement and Conflict of Interest Statement required at the end of the paper, under those exact headings, before the references.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
FIELD_SKILL's management voice conventions (theory-forward hedging like "Consistent with [Theory X]," explicit contribution-to-literature framing) are largely absent. Articles state a direct empirical or exposure-assessment objective ("we characterized," "we evaluated") rather than a theoretical contribution claim. RELAXATION/FIELD-MISMATCH, not a stated journal norm — reflects the discipline, not an editorial policy.

### Statistical Reporting
No hypothesis-revisiting convention (no "H1 predicted X..."). Results are reported as measured quantities against reference methods or exposure limits (e.g., percent bias, exposure concentrations vs. occupational exposure limits) rather than as tests of theory-derived hypotheses. FIELD DEFAULT does not apply; this is a structural feature of the discipline, not a relaxation.

### Citation Density
Not independently assessable from abstract-level access; no delta confirmed either way (INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE).

### Structural Deviations
Article-type diversity is broader and more tightly length-capped than FIELD_SKILL's four templates: Editorial, Commentary, Original Article, Short Communication, and Letter to the Editor all appear routinely as distinct, short-form categories (typically under 1,500-5,000 words), unlike the 8,000-25,000-word FIELD_SKILL templates. ENFORCED (stated in guidelines, confirmed via issue table of contents).

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Abstracts and keywords emphasize measurement/exposure terminology (e.g., "monitor," "calibration," "exposure assessment," "bias," "accuracy") rather than theoretical constructs — consistent with the journal's exposure-science scope rather than management theory. PRACTICE ONLY, low-prevalence sample but consistent with stated scope.

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting with APA-style (Author, Year) citations instead of the required CSE name-year format.
- Writing management-style theory/hypothesis framing (H1, H2...) instead of a direct empirical exposure-assessment question — this journal does not use that convention.
- Exceeding the 5,000-word cap (excl. abstract/references/tables/figures) or the 6-table/figure limit for Original Research.
- Omitting the mandatory Funding and Conflict of Interest headings before the reference list.
- Submitting revision files as PDF instead of .doc/.rtf/.tex.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

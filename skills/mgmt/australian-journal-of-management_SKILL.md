# Journal Writing Skill: Australian Journal of Management

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN (and broader management)
- **Publisher:** SAGE Publications
- **Publication Frequency:** Triannual (3 issues per year)
- **Scope:** International peer-reviewed outlet for multidisciplinary management scholarship covering accounting, applied economics, finance, industrial relations, political science, psychology, statistics, marketing, corporate strategy, operations, and related disciplines in application to management
- **Guidelines URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/author-instructions/aum
- **Field baseline:** mgmt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 8+ (special issue on "Managing with Compassion in a Complex, Changing World" Vol 50, Issue 3, 2025; accessibility limited by SAGE paywall; abstract/metadata only for most)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows standard management empirical article structure: Abstract → Introduction → [Theory/Literature] → Methods → Results/Findings → Discussion → Conclusion. Theoretical and review articles permitted.

### Length
Typically around 8,000 words; longer articles accepted if length is justified by contribution. No stated maximum word count; flexibility is provided for in-depth treatments.

### Abstract
Unstructured (not IMRAD-formatted). Up to 250 words maximum. Must concisely state the purpose/research question, major findings or approach, and conclusions. No citations in abstract.

### Citations
SAGE Harvard reference style (author-date in-text citations). Format: (Author, Year) or Author (Year) when author is part of sentence. Multiple citations ordered alphabetically or chronologically. Et al. for 3+ authors from first citation onwards. Every in-text citation must have corresponding entry in reference list with identical spelling and year.

### Keywords
4-5 keywords minimum, listed after abstract. Keywords should be specific to research topic and aid search engine discovery.

### Headings
Clear heading levels required; sections clearly defined. Template not required. No specific formatting rules provided by journal; defer to FIELD_SKILL conventions.

### Tables and Figures
No specific formatting rules provided in available guidelines. Follow SAGE Harvard style conventions and ensure clarity.

### Ethical Requirements
For studies involving human participants, human data, or human tissue: state that relevant Ethics Committee or Institutional Review Board approved the study (or waived approval). Provide full committee name, institution, and approval number. Typically included in Methods section unless journal specifies separate heading.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Citations to preprints discouraged; update to published version when available. All work must be original and not previously submitted for publication elsewhere.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates from the baseline.

### Keywords
ENFORCED. FIELD_SKILL does not specify keyword requirements. Australian Journal of Management requires 4-5 keywords minimum, listed after abstract, for discoverability and peer reviewer matching.

### Abstract Format and Scope
FIELD_SKILL states "Structured or unstructured; both appear across outlets." Australian Journal of Management explicitly requires unstructured abstract, up to 250 words, covering purpose, findings, and conclusions in flowing prose rather than labeled sections.

### Word Length Guideline
FIELD_SKILL cites "10,000–15,000 words" for typical empirical papers in top management outlets. Australian Journal of Management targets ~8,000 words as standard (lower-tier journal reflects this), though longer articles accepted if justified. This represents a RELAXATION of typical field norms for article length.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- Voice and person: FIELD DEFAULT (follows "we" convention for co-authored work, hedging with appropriate modal verbs)
- Citation density: FIELD DEFAULT (introduction and theory sections dense; results and discussion interpret citations)
- Structural deviations: FIELD DEFAULT (empirical work follows theory → methods → results → discussion; conceptual work permitted)
- Argumentation: FIELD DEFAULT (explicit hypotheses or propositions, logical reasoning chains, named theoretical frameworks expected)
- Hedging and epistemic stance: FIELD DEFAULT (moderate hedging; "we argue," "we suggest," "we find" used as specified in baseline)
- Methods reporting: FIELD DEFAULT (past tense, identification concerns addressed, robustness checks expected for quantitative work)
- Discussion/Conclusion: FIELD DEFAULT (restatement of findings, theoretical implications, limitations acknowledged, future directions noted)

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Exceeding keyword guidance**: Listing fewer than 4 keywords or more than 5-6 may reduce discoverability and reviewer-matching effectiveness.
2. **Structured abstract submission**: The journal explicitly rejects IMRAD-style (structured) abstracts; unstructured prose format is required.
3. **Preprint citations without updates**: Citing preprints when a peer-reviewed published version is available suggests incomplete literature tracking.
4. **Missing ethics statement for human-subjects research**: Studies involving participants must include ethics approval/waiver information with institution and approval number; omission risks desk rejection.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

No contradictions were observed between accessible abstract/metadata and stated guidelines.

# Journal Writing Skill: Mathematical Social Sciences

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Elsevier
- **Scope:** An international, interdisciplinary journal publishing mathematically rigorous research in economics, psychology, political science, sociology, and related social sciences. Topics include decision science, game theory, voting rules, cooperative games, fair division, cost allocation, bargaining, matching, and social networks.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/mathematical-social-sciences/publish/guide-for-authors (paywalled; key details extracted via ScienceDirect/Elsevier landing pages)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 (across 2025-2026 issues; arXiv preprints)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure

Articles must be self-contained and readable by social scientists trained in mathematics. The standard sections for theory papers follow FIELD_SKILL norm (Introduction, Model/Theory, Analysis, Results, Conclusion). Empirical papers follow the FIELD_SKILL empirical structure.

### Length

No explicit word or page limit published in accessible guidelines. Articles in practice range 15–30 pages (including appendices), typical for ABS 2 journals. This is shorter than ABS 4/4* journals but within field norms for lower tiers.

### Abstract

Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Concise, factual abstract stating purpose, principal results, and major conclusions.

### Citations

Author-year parenthetical or in-text citation style, consistent with FIELD_SKILL. Footnotes carry supplementary material, not primary citations.

### Headings

Arabic numerals (1, 2, 3…) for primary sections; decimal subsections (2.1, 2.2). Consistent with FIELD_SKILL.

### Tables and Figures

Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Tables present results; figures illustrate key variation. Captions explain variables and sample restrictions.

### Data Requirements

Authors are required to deposit research data in a relevant data repository, cite and link to it in the article, or provide a statement explaining why data cannot be shared. This is an explicit mandatory requirement beyond FIELD_SKILL.

### Explicit Prohibitions

None identified beyond standard research ethics (originality, no simultaneous submission). SSRN preprint posting is permitted and encouraged without editorial prejudice.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

> Only dimensions where this journal deviates.

### Mathematical Formalism and Proof Architecture (PRACTICE EMPHASIS)

Mathematical Social Sciences exhibits higher density of formal notation, theorem-lemma-corollary-proof architecture, and symbolic logic compared to the FIELD_SKILL baseline. Articles routinely present results as formal theorems with numbered lemmas preceding them, using extensive set notation and logical operators. This reflects the journal's mathematical focus (stated in guidelines as "no bounds limit technical level") and is consistent with economic theory papers, but is elevated relative to the mixed empirical/theory portfolio underlying FIELD_SKILL.

**Implication for writing:** If submitting a theory paper with mathematical foundations, expect reviewers will expect formal statement of results (theorems, propositions, lemmas) with rigorous proofs. This is encouraged, not a deviation—but it is more pronounced here than in broader FIELD_SKILL baseline.

### Article Length and Proportionality (FIELD DEFAULT)

Articles are generally shorter than ABS 4/4* baseline papers. Typical length 15–30 pages including appendices, vs. 40–60 for Econometrica or AER. This is appropriate for ABS 2 tier and does not conflict with FIELD_SKILL; it reflects the journal's positioning.

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Voice and person:** First-person plural ("we") is standard, consistent with FIELD_SKILL.
- **Active voice preference:** Strongly preferred; passive restricted to data generation contexts. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Hedging intensity:** Low; clear claims preferred. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Citation density by section:** Introduction high, Methods medium, Results low-medium, Conclusion low. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Paraphrase vs. quotation:** Direct quotation extremely rare; paraphrase with citation is standard. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Sentence and paragraph length:** Medium to long, with technical content driving length. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Results and interpretation integration:** Results and interpretation typically integrated, not separated. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Null results:** Treated as publishable results when identification is strong. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Conclusion structure:** Restates findings, draws implications, acknowledges limitations, suggests future work. FIELD DEFAULT.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Submitting overly informal or exploratory work:** The journal's stated commitment to mathematical rigor ("papers are mathematically rigorous") means submissions lacking formal modeling or theoretical grounding are at a disadvantage. Even applied papers should have a clear theoretical or game-theoretic structure.

2. **Insufficient motivation of mathematical framework:** Do not assume readers will accept complex notation without clear explanation of what it represents economically or socially. Motivate formal definitions before introducing them.

3. **Neglecting data availability statement:** The requirement to deposit data or explain why it cannot be shared is mandatory and non-negotiable. Omitting this statement causes desk rejection.

4. **Underestimating the importance of self-containedness:** Articles must be readable by social scientists trained in mathematics (not necessarily trained in your specific subfield). Avoid discipline-specific jargon without definition.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: International Marketing Review

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** MKT
- **Publisher:** Emerald
- **Scope:** Publishes research that pushes back the boundaries of international marketing knowledge — international marketing strategy, international business/marketing interface, cross-national consumer behavior, internationalization of firms (including SMEs and digital platforms), and international branding.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.emeraldgrouppublishing.com/products/journals/author_guidelines.htm?id=IMR (page returned 403 on direct fetch in this environment; content triangulated via search-engine snippet and cross-checked against live articles)
- **Field baseline:** mkt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 15 (Tran et al. 2025 IMR 42(1):1; Sousa et al. 2025 IMR 42(1):35; Kim et al. 2025 IMR 42(1):64; Teerakapibal & Schlegelmilch 2025 IMR 42(1):103; Shi et al. 2025 IMR 42(1):149; Li et al. 2025/26 IMR 43(2):338 "Cultural heritage branding"; unnamed 2025/26 IMR 43(2) "Deglobalization and confederated communities"; unnamed 2025 IMR 42(4):585 "Digital transformation portrays a play or ploy"; Qin & Chen 2025 IMR 42(5):1009; Da Rocha et al. 2024 IMR 41(5):981; Bargoni et al. 2024 IMR 41(1):74; Shahid et al. 2024 IMR 41(2):514; Christofi et al. 2024 IMR 41(7):140; Khan 2022 IMR 40(5):1035; Spilotro et al. 2025 IMR 42(4):666)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No fixed section template is mandated beyond the standard Emerald manuscript components; papers must be pre-classified into one of Emerald's standard article-type categories: Research paper, Viewpoint, Technical paper, Conceptual paper, Case study, Literature review, General review. This classification is a required metadata field, not just an informal descriptor.

### Length
Not confirmed from an accessible page (guidelines page 403; no consistent length statement recovered). Follow FIELD_SKILL defaults unless the submission system specifies otherwise.

### Abstract
Structured abstract required, under 4-7 sub-headings, maximum 250 words total (word count includes keywords and classification). Emerald's standard heading set for empirical papers is: Purpose; Design/Methodology/Approach; Findings; Research Limitations/Implications; Practical Implications; Social Implications; Originality/Value (journals typically use a 4-heading subset: Purpose, Design/Methodology/Approach, Findings, Originality/Value). Up to six keywords required.

### Citations
Not independently confirmed from the guidelines page; Emerald journals in this family generally use Harvard-style author-date referencing. No IMR-specific deviation observed in article front matter (citation style not visible in abstract-only views).

### Headings
Article must be pre-classified by type (see Structure). No other IMR-specific heading rule found beyond the structured-abstract sub-headings.

### Tables and Figures
Tables must be typed and included as part of the manuscript (not submitted as separate graphic elements).

### Explicit Prohibitions
Generative AI tools may not be used to draft or create original manuscript content ("copywriting"); AI-assisted copy-editing of the author's own existing text (correcting, formatting, refining) is permitted. All papers undergo double-blind peer review by three reviewers.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
Conceptual papers and (especially) literature-review/systematic-review articles frequently do NOT use the full Purpose/Design/Findings/Originality structured-abstract format that empirical papers use — several observed literature reviews (e.g., Da Rocha et al. 2024; Spilotro et al. 2025; Shahid et al. 2024) present abstracts as continuous prose or partial headings rather than the strict 4-part structure seen in empirical papers (e.g., Tran et al. 2025; Shi et al. 2025; Institutional distance 2024). GUIDELINE ONLY / RELAXATION: the guidelines require structured abstracts for all types, but literature-review and conceptual submissions in practice relax this more often than empirical ones. Treat the 4-part structure as default for empirical work; conceptual/review pieces may use lighter structuring.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
IMR carries a heavier concentration of qualitative and mixed-method international-business papers (ethnography, case study, grounded theory, multi-sample qualitative-quantitative combinations) than the FIELD_SKILL behavioral/quantitative split implies — several spot-checked articles (Li et al. cultural heritage branding; confederated communities piece; Qin & Chen ethnographic study) are qualitative case/ethnographic studies with no hypotheses (H1, H2…) at all, framed instead around research questions and thematic/stage-based findings. PRACTICE ONLY: this is a disciplinary emphasis (international business tradition) rather than a stated journal rule, but it is prevalent enough that qualitative case/ethnography structuring should be treated as a normal, not exceptional, article shape for this journal.

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting an unstructured or loosely-labeled abstract for an empirical paper — IMR enforces the Emerald structured format (Purpose/Design/Methodology/Approach/Findings/Originality-Value) for standard research articles even though literature reviews are given more latitude.
- Forgetting to select/state the Emerald article-type classification (Research paper, Conceptual paper, Literature review, etc.) — this is a required submission field, not optional framing.
- Treating hypothesis-driven (H1, H2…) structuring as mandatory — a substantial share of IMR content is qualitative/case-based international business research organized around research questions and themes rather than numbered hypotheses.
- Using generative AI to draft original text rather than only to copy-edit existing author-written material, which violates the journal's AI policy.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

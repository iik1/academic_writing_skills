# Journal Writing Skill: Review of Economics and Political Science

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Emerald Publishing (on behalf of the Faculty of Economics and Political Science, Cairo University)
- **Scope:** Publishes original articles in economics, political science, statistics, and public administration; broad remit spanning macro/development economics, political economy, governance, and international relations, with an emphasis on MENA-region and emerging-market applications.
- **Guidelines URL:** Journal-specific author guidelines page (emerald.com/reps/author-guidelines) returned 404 at time of research; general Emerald submission requirements were consulted instead (emeraldpublishinggroup.freshdesk.com) and cross-checked against 10 published articles' visible structure.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict. Direct journal-specific guidelines page was inaccessible (404); constraints below are inferred from Emerald's standard journal template (confirmed by cross-referencing 10 published REPS articles, which show 100% consistency) rather than read directly off a guidelines page.

### Structure
Empirical papers follow Introduction → Literature Review (often with named subsections, e.g. "Theoretical Framework" / "Empirical Studies" or "Empirical Reviews") → Data/Methodology → Results/Empirical Results → Discussion/Conclusion. A distinct, separately labeled "Literature Review" section is near-universal in the sample (present in all 10 articles), rather than folded into the introduction.

### Length
Not stated on any accessible page; no explicit word limit could be confirmed.

### Abstract
Mandatory **structured abstract** using Emerald's standard headed format: Purpose, Design/methodology/approach, Findings, and Originality/value are mandatory; Research limitations/implications and Practical implications appear where applicable (observed in roughly half the sample). Emerald's general limit is 250 words including keywords and classification. This is a hard structural requirement, confirmed in all 10 sampled abstracts.

### Citations
Not directly confirmed from a guidelines page; Emerald's standard house style is Harvard/APA-like author-date referencing, consistent with FIELD_SKILL's author-year norm, so no delta is asserted here beyond what FIELD_SKILL already covers.

### Headings
Numbered or unnumbered section headings both appear across the sample (no consistent numbering convention observed). Subsections within Literature Review and Results sections are common.

### Tables and Figures
No journal-specific rule found beyond FIELD_SKILL defaults.

### Explicit Prohibitions
None identified from accessible sources.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm in visible prose (formal, active-voice constructions where sampled), though several articles reviewed are single-author political-economy pieces rather than the multi-author causal-empirical papers FIELD_SKILL is drawn from.

### Statistical Reporting
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm — magnitudes are reported with quantitative specificity in Findings sections (e.g., "1% governance increase correlates with 1% growth increase"; effects "ranging between 15 and 40%").

### Citation Density
Not assessable from abstract/front-matter access alone; no delta asserted.

### Structural Deviations
**PRACTICE ONLY / journal-wide pattern:** The structured abstract (Purpose/Design-methodology-approach/Findings/Originality-value) is a hard, Emerald-house-style deviation from FIELD_SKILL, which assumes free-form abstracts typical of ABS 4*/4 economics journals. This is ENFORCED (confirmed in 10/10 sampled articles) even though the specific journal guidelines page could not be directly read — it is Emerald's standard template applied without exception in this sample.

A dedicated, separately headed Literature Review section (rather than literature folded into the introduction, which FIELD_SKILL notes is common at the top tier) is the norm here — RELAXATION consistent with ABS 1 tier practice, not a new norm beyond what FIELD_SKILL already flags as an acceptable variant ("standalone Related Literature section... often omitted in shorter empirical papers" — REPS is the opposite: it is rarely omitted).

Several sampled articles append a distinct "Practical Implications" or "Limitations and Directions for Future Research" section after the conclusion (observed in 3 of 10), rather than folding these into the conclusion as FIELD_SKILL describes as more typical. PRACTICE ONLY, not universal (7 of 10 fold these into the conclusion or omit them).

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
JEL classification codes are commonly but not universally present (appeared in 4 of 10 sampled articles; absent in political-science-only pieces such as the China-Africa and Latin America presidential-instability articles, consistent with those papers falling outside JEL-coded economics subfields).

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting a free-form (non-headed) abstract: REPS requires Emerald's structured Purpose/Design-methodology-approach/Findings/Originality-value format, confirmed without exception in the sample — this is the single highest-risk mismatch versus ABS 4*/4 habits.
- Omitting a standalone Literature Review section: unlike top-tier journals where literature can be woven into the introduction, REPS articles consistently carry a separately headed literature section.
- Assuming JEL codes are mandatory: they appear inconsistently and are absent from political-science-oriented submissions, so treat them as expected for economics-subfield papers but not a strict universal requirement.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

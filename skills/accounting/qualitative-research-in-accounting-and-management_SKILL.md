# Journal Writing Skill: Qualitative Research in Accounting and Management

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ACCOUNT
- **Publisher:** Emerald Group Publishing Limited
- **Scope:** International journal publishing qualitative research investigating the interface of accounting and management/managerial work in different contexts and from various theoretical perspectives. Publishes original research papers, conceptual pieces, substantive review articles, and shorter contributions (comments, research notes) that contribute to qualitative exploration of accounting.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Manuscripts should be prepared according to QRAM author guidelines (specific format requirements accessed via ScholarOne submission portal). Standard sections follow FIELD_SKILL conventions: Introduction, Methodology/Theory, Findings/Results, Discussion/Conclusion. Both empirical case studies and conceptual/viewpoint pieces are accepted.

### Length
Word count requirements vary; typical Emerald journals accommodate 8,000–12,000 words. QRAM-specific limit requires consultation of ScholarOne submission system. Word count includes abstract, references, and text in tables/figures (add 280 words per table/figure).

### Abstract
Structured abstract (required by Emerald standard). Maximum 250 words. Organized under sub-headings: Purpose, Design/Methodology/Approach, Findings, Originality/Value. Keywords and JEL codes provided separately. ENFORCED per Emerald publisher standard.

### Citations
Author-date (Harvard style). In-text as "(Author Year)" or "Author (Year)". Multiple citations separated by semicolons. ENFORCED per Emerald standard.

### Headings
Heading style varies by journal convention. The sample articles reviewed employ both numbered (1. Introduction, 2. Methodology) and un-numbered title-case headings depending on the article type and author preference. No explicit ALL-CAPS convention observed in recent 2024 articles. Match the target journal's current convention by consulting recent published articles.

### Tables and Figures
Tables and figures placed after text or in appendix. Must include descriptive captions and notes explaining data sources, definitions, and statistical choices. Standard academic table formatting applies.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Submission via email or third-party agents not permitted; use ScholarOne system exclusively.
- Generative AI for copy-editing (grammar, structure, clarity) is permitted; authors must not misrepresent AI-generated text as original scholarship.
- Double-blind peer review required; maintain anonymity in submitted manuscript.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Qualitative-Focused Scope and Methodology Emphasis
RELAXATION: While FIELD_SKILL baseline encodes norms for quantitative/archival/experimental work (matching ABS 4* field centers), QRAM is explicitly a qualitative journal and systematically accepts ethnographic, case study, interview-based, and interpretive research. The journal also accepts conceptual and viewpoint articles (e.g., Gendron 2024 "The audit society" framed as a viewpoint/position piece rather than empirical work). This is not a deviation in voice or register but a methodological scope relaxation: QRAM does not require hypothesis testing, quasi-experimental identification, or regression-based results. Instead, qualitative research questions, exploratory designs, and thematic/narrative findings are the norm.

### Structured Abstract with Originality/Value Statement
ENFORCED: QRAM follows Emerald's standardized structured abstract format (Purpose | Design/Methodology | Findings | Originality/Value). This is mandatory across Emerald journals and differs from some accounting journals that use unstructured abstracts. The "Originality/Value" section is explicit and required, positioning the contribution clearly within the abstract itself rather than solely in the introduction.

### Conceptual and Viewpoint Articles Accepted
PRACTICE: In addition to empirical research, QRAM explicitly publishes substantive review articles and viewpoint/position pieces. Gendron (2024) exemplifies this: a reflective, position-setting article on "the audit society thesis" without original data collection. FIELD_SKILL does not systematically address review or conceptual articles; QRAM actively solicits them. These articles follow the same citation and section structure but may replace "Methodology/Findings/Results" with "Argument/Analysis/Implications."

### Interview-Based and Ethnographic Research as Primary Methodologies
PRACTICE: Articles frequently employ interviews, field observation, ethnography, and case study design. Stevenson et al. (2024) documents a two-stage design (content analysis + interviews); Luke (2024) explicitly addresses interview methodology as a focal contribution. This is higher prevalence of qualitative fieldwork methods than in the FIELD_SKILL baseline (which centers archival/experimental work).

## Common Pitfalls
> Observed in rejected or revision-heavy submissions to QRAM (where evidenced by publication data and editorial guidance)

1. **Vague methodology sections in qualitative research.** QRAM reviewers expect detailed description of data collection method (interview protocols, sampling strategy, participant selection, number of interviews, field observation period), data analysis approach (coding scheme, thematic analysis procedure, theoretical framework applied), and reflexivity statement. Do not assume methodological brevity is acceptable just because FIELD_SKILL notes that established methods may be cited rather than fully described. Qualitative methods are not "established" in the same sense as regression; they must be justified and detailed.

2. **Insufficient reflexivity in interpretive/ethnographic work.** Particularly in viewpoint articles and fieldwork-based research, authors should acknowledge their positioning, theoretical lens, and potential biases. QRAM's qualitative tradition (as evidenced by Gendron and other interpretive papers) expects some reflection on how the researcher's perspective shapes interpretation.

3. **Empirical findings presented without sufficient anchoring to literature or theory.** Qualitative results must be interpreted through reference to the theoretical framework established in the introduction. Do not present interview quotations or case descriptions without explicit connection to the conceptual argument.

4. **Inconsistent article type labeling.** QRAM accepts empirical papers, reviews, conceptual papers, and viewpoints. Be explicit about which type your submission is; different expectations apply to structure and scope depending on type.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Studies in Microeconomics

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** SAGE Publications
- **Scope:** High-quality, analytically rigorous papers in all areas of microeconomics (broadly defined). Both theoretical and empirical research welcome. Topics include rational choice, consumer/producer choice, game theory, market equilibrium, information economics, industrial organization, and public economics with microeconomic framework.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/author-instructions/mic
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 10 (April 2026 Volume 14 Issue 1 and December 2025 Volume 13 Issue 3)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Standard academic structure: Title > Abstract > Keywords > Main Body > Statements and Declarations > References. Sections should follow numbered or unnumbered heading hierarchy. No specific section-naming mandate beyond standard conventions (Introduction, Methods/Data, Results, Conclusion acceptable).

### Length
**Manuscripts should not exceed 6,000 words.** This is a strict constraint significantly shorter than ABS 4*/4 norms (typically 25,000–40,000 words including appendices). Word count must include all text except abstract, keywords, and references.

### Abstract
Unstructured abstract of 150–200 words. Must concisely state research purpose, major findings, and conclusions. Abstracts for clinical trials include trial registry name, URL, and registration number at end (not relevant for economic research, but noted for completeness).

### Citations
Author-year format (parenthetical or in-text) via APA reference style. Every in-text citation requires matching reference list entry with identical spelling and year. Preprint citations discouraged unless peer-reviewed version unavailable. Footnotes carry supplementary material and qualifications, not primary citations (consistent with FIELD_SKILL).

### Headings
No prescribed heading convention. Both Roman numerals (I, II, III) and Arabic numerals (1, 2, 3) acceptable. Use consistent hierarchy throughout. Conclusion section naming is flexible (Conclusion, Concluding Remarks, etc.).

### Keywords
**Required: 4–6 keywords.** Placed after abstract, before main body. Keywords must be specific to research topic and assist indexing.

### Tables and Figures
- Numbered consecutively in order of appearance
- Minimum 300 dpi resolution for figures
- Color figures acceptable and preferred for online display
- All tables and figures must have explanatory captions
- Table notes should explain variable definitions, standard error clustering, and sample restrictions (consistent with FIELD_SKILL)

### Statements and Declarations Section
**Required.** Appears on title page after acknowledgments (if any). Must include explicit subsections (state "Not applicable" where irrelevant):
- Ethical considerations
- Consent to participate
- Consent for publication
- Declaration of conflicting interests
- Funding statement
- Data availability statement

This is more formal and explicit than typical ABS 4*/4 practice and reflects SAGE's publication ethics standards.

### File Format and Submission
Preferred format: Microsoft Word. LaTeX files accepted. Manuscripts submitted via SAGE peer review portal (https://peerreview.sagepub.com/MIC). Double-blind review process.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- AI chatbots cannot be listed as authors
- Manuscripts must not be simultaneously under review elsewhere
- Preprint versions cannot be updated during peer review
- Manuscripts must conform to guidelines or may be returned without review

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Length Constraint (RELAXATION)
ABS 1 status: 6,000-word limit is significantly **more restrictive** than ABS 4*/4 practice. Field baseline does not specify word limits; top-tier papers routinely reach 8,000–12,000 words before appendices. Authors must prune arguments, reduce methodology exposition, and consolidate results to fit this boundary. No provision for online appendices is mentioned in guidelines; all supplementary material must be integrated or omitted.

**Implication:** Argument density must be higher. Supporting evidence and robustness checks must be selected ruthlessly. Novel methodology requires briefer exposition than in top-tier outlets.

### Keyword Requirement (FIELD ADDITION)
Field baseline does not specify keywords. **Studies in Microeconomics mandates 4–6 keywords after abstract.** Keywords are indexed by SAGE and improve discoverability. Field baseline assumes keywords are optional; here they are required.

### Statements and Declarations Section (FIELD ADDITION)
Field baseline makes no mention of formal ethics or funding declarations. **Studies in Microeconomics requires explicit Statements and Declarations section** with named subsections covering ethical approval, consent, conflicts of interest, funding sources, and data availability. This reflects SAGE's publication ethics compliance and is more formal than typical economics journal practice at any tier.

**Implication:** Authors must proactively disclose potential conflicts, funding source, and data handling upfront. Failure to include this section may delay review.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Exceeding 6,000 words.** The word limit is non-negotiable. Papers routinely exceed this in early drafts; aggressive pruning of methods exposition, table footnotes, and literature review is necessary. Do not assume appendices will compensate.

2. **Missing Statements and Declarations section.** Authors accustomed to top-tier economics outlets (where declarations are minimal or optional) often omit or minimize this section. SAGE requires it explicitly. Include all six subsections even if most are "Not applicable."

3. **Unstructured keywords.** Keywords should be specific to the research topic and distinct from the title. Vague or overly general keywords (e.g., "economics," "microeconomics") reduce discoverability. Aim for 4–6 precise, searchable terms.

4. **APA citation errors.** Inconsistent spelling, missing years, or mismatched in-text and reference-list entries are common. APA is stricter than author-year cite-as-you-wish norms. Proofread reference list carefully.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Public Economic Theory

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Wiley-Blackwell
- **Scope:** Outstanding theoretical work in public finance, public goods, taxation, externalities, public choice, voting, regulation, growth. Theoretical orientation without empirical requirements. Accepts papers across diverse theoretical approaches: general equilibrium theory, game theory, experimental design, control theory and dynamics, simulation, axiomatic characterization.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/page/journal/14679779/homepage/forauthors.html (paywalled); submission portal: submission.wiley.com/journal/JPET
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 10 (see Sources)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

### Structure

Manuscripts should include standard sections: abstract, introduction, methods/theory, results, and conclusion. Section heading conventions are left flexible; both Roman and Arabic numerals are acceptable (following FIELD_SKILL norm). A standalone appendix is standard for proofs, robustness, and supplementary material.

No mandatory section order is enforced beyond introduction-body-conclusion. Theory papers follow the typical FIELD_SKILL structure (Introduction, Literature, Model Setup, Analysis, Results/Welfare Analysis, Extensions, Conclusion) without deviation.

### Length

No stated word limit or page limit in publicly available guidelines. Wiley's "Free Format submission" option suggests flexibility on length. Empirical observation across recent articles (2024-2026) shows papers range from 20-40 pages depending on theoretical depth.

### Abstract

**Required.** Word limit: **250 words maximum.** Must "succinctly and accurately describe the paper so that appropriate referees can be matched to the topic."

No explicit requirement for structured sections (Background, Methods, Results, Conclusion) as in some journals. Unstructured prose abstracts are acceptable.

### Citations

Author-year format (parenthetical or in-text); follows FIELD_SKILL norm. No explicit prohibitions on citation density. References may be submitted "in any style or format, as long as it is consistent throughout the manuscript" — relaxation from top-tier norms that enforce specific citation systems.

### Headings

Both Roman numerals (I, II, III) and Arabic numerals (1, 2, 3) are acceptable. No mandatory section naming conventions beyond introduction-conclusion framework. Subheadings are used flexibly.

### Tables and Figures

Figures and tables should have legends/captions. "Figures should be uploaded in the highest resolution possible. If figures are not of sufficiently high quality your manuscript may be delayed." No explicit limit on table/figure count.

### File Format

LaTeX acceptable (with PDF required for peer review); Word/other formats acceptable.

### Explicit Prohibitions

- Submission must not have been "published or submitted for publication elsewhere except as a brief abstract in the proceedings of a scientific meeting or symposium."
- IRB approvals, ethical treatment of participants, informed consent required if applicable.
- All conflicts of interest must be declared.
- The journal uses CrossCheck (iThenticate) to detect overlapping text.
- Preprints are acceptable; papers previously available as preprints will be considered for review.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Citation System Flexibility

**Delta: GUIDELINE ONLY, PRACTICE VARIES**

FIELD_SKILL specifies author-year as near-universal. JPET guidelines state "References may be submitted in any style or format, as long as it is consistent throughout the manuscript," representing explicit relaxation from top-tier orthodoxy. Spot-checked articles observed consistent use of author-year, but flexibility is permitted and stated.

**Classification:** RELAXATION — lower-tier journals permit variation not seen in FIELD_SKILL normative tier.

### Theoretical Diversity

**Delta: ENFORCED**

FIELD_SKILL baseline is drawn from ABS 4*/4 journals emphasizing empirical work or top-tier theory; typical article is an empirical study or formal model with proofs. JPET explicitly accepts "short notes, exposita, comments, survey papers and retrospectives" alongside full articles, and the journal "imposes neither upper nor lower boundary on the complexity of the techniques employed." 

Spot-checked articles confirm a wider range of publication formats than ABS 4* outlets typically feature: some articles are tight algebraic treatments, others apply game theory, experimental design, or control theory without mandatory empirical validation.

**Classification:** ENFORCED — explicitly stated in guidelines and confirmed across sample.

### Hedging and Certainty

Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. No evidence of relaxation or intensification. Theoretical claims are stated directly; identification of solvability, existence, or uniqueness results use present tense without hedging ("The model implies...").

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Voice and person:** FIELD DEFAULT. First-person plural ("we") dominates; active voice strongly preferred.
- **Sentence and paragraph style:** FIELD DEFAULT. Medium to long sentences; dense paragraphs (4–10 sentences per paragraph).
- **Methods and results reporting:** FIELD DEFAULT. Model setup and equilibrium analysis described with sufficient precision; results stated quantitatively where applicable.
- **Argumentation structure:** FIELD DEFAULT. Introductions announce results upfront; research gaps framed as theoretical insufficiencies, not mere lack of prior work; conclusions synthesize without new results.
- **Handling disagreement:** FIELD DEFAULT. Precise attribution; explanations of why findings differ from prior work expected.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Abstract overcomplexity:** 250 words is tight. Authors submitting to JPET often pack technical assumptions, lemmas, or multiple results into abstracts intended for referee-matching. Write abstractly (conceptually, not mathematically) and reserve technical precision for the main text.

2. **Assuming ABS 4 rigor without ABS 4 payoff:** JPET's ABS 2 tier, combined with its "no upper or lower bound on technique complexity," creates a trap: authors submit highly technical proofs with modest economic insight. Papers accepted in JPET tend to make a clear economic or theoretical contribution *and* employ solid techniques, not technique alone.

3. **Ignoring the "short notes" option:** Spot-checked issues contain short notes and comments (2–6 pages) alongside full papers. Authors with focused or incremental contributions often submit as full articles to major journals and face rejection; submitting a shorter note to JPET may be more appropriate.

4. **Mixing citation formats:** Guidelines permit flexibility, but inconsistency is penalized. Choose one system and enforce it throughout.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

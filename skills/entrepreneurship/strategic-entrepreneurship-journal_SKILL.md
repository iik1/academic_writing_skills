# Journal Writing Skill: Strategic Entrepreneurship Journal

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 4
- **Field:** ENT-SBM
- **Publisher:** Wiley-Blackwell (in partnership with the Strategic Management Society)
- **Scope:** A general-interest entrepreneurship journal publishing theory- or
  phenomenon-driven, quantitative, qualitative, or conceptual research on
  innovation and change that adds durable value to organizations and society.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.strategicmanagement.net/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/SEJ-Author-Instructions-June-2023-FINAL.pdf (accessible; last updated 8 June 2023)
- **Field baseline:** entrepreneurship-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 10 (title/front-matter level; full text paywalled).
  Sampled: "Seizing the moment—Strategy, social entrepreneurship, and the

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Required manuscript layout: Abstract(s), body text, references, appendices,
tables, figures — tables/figures placed at the END of the manuscript (after
references, not embedded in text), each with an "INSERT TABLE X HERE"
placement instruction in the body. Title page (with author names,
affiliations, running head ≤60 characters, and acknowledgements) is a
SEPARATE file from the anonymized main document. Acknowledgements appear
ONLY on the title page at submission (not in the manuscript text); if
accepted, they move to the end of the text, right before references.
Appendices follow references; a single appendix needs no number; an
online-only appendix must be called the "online appendix" throughout and
submitted as a separate file, unedited by copyediting. Appendix tables/
figures use letters, not numbers.

### Length
Strongly suggested limit: 40 pages, inclusive of figures and tables. This
applies uniformly across Research Article, Special Issue Article,
Commentary, and Editorial types.

### Abstract
SEJ requires TWO abstracts, each up to 125 words, single paragraph, no
citations: (1) an academic "Research Summary" that is a precise, standalone
summary of the entire paper (not just conclusions), and (2) a "Managerial
Summary" covering the same content in plain, non-academic language aimed at
business practitioners. Five keywords required for indexing.

### Citations
APA author-date, in-text (Jones, 1998); page number for direct quotes. Six
or more authors: "main author et al., year" from the first citation (looser
than default APA's "three or more"). Five or fewer authors: full author
list at first citation, et al. thereafter. Multiple citations ordered
alphabetically, separated by semicolons.

### Headings
The main guidelines body specifies THREE unnumbered heading levels: Level 1
ALL CAPS/bold/centered; Level 2 capitalized main words/left-aligned; Level 3
lowercase/italicized. The later "General APA guidelines" table in the same
document instead shows FIVE numbered heading levels (Heading 1 ALL CAPS
bold; Headings 2-5 sentence case bold, with Heading 5 run-on with text) —
this is an internal inconsistency in the guidelines themselves. Default to
the more detailed five-level numbered scheme for longer papers if needed,
but the three-level unnumbered scheme is the more prominently stated norm;
either is acceptable to editorial staff, per the document. Hypotheses are
identified by number using the format "Hypothesis (H1). Text..." (bolded
label, italicized or run-on text).

### Tables and Figures
Editable (non-image) tables required. Table names ABOVE the table ("TABLE 1
Name of table"); figure names BELOW the figure ("FIGURE 1 Name of figure").
Figure files ≥300dpi, .tiff/.png preferred; color online, black-and-white
in print.

### Explicit Prohibitions
No prior publication or simultaneous submission elsewhere. No Track Changes
or comment bubbles in the submitted text. No author-identifying information
(names, affiliations, acknowledgements) in the main document. No citations
within either abstract. Ampersands restricted to common expressions,
product/company names, or parenthetical citations — not general prose. Zero
before decimal only when the statistic can exceed 1 (0.23 cm) but not for
correlations/proportions/p-values (p = .028).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
SEJ's mandatory dual abstract (academic Research Summary + plain-language
Managerial Summary, each capped at 125 words) is more prescriptive than
FIELD_SKILL's general expectation of a "Managerial Summary or Practical
Implications subsection" within the Discussion — at SEJ this practitioner-
facing content is pulled forward into a second, separately labeled abstract
rather than (or in addition to) a Discussion subsection. ENFORCED (stated
in guidelines; consistent with SEJ's applied, practice-oriented aims-and-
scope language).

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Figures/tables are required to sit at the end of the manuscript after
references, not embedded near their first mention in text, and appendix
tables/figures use letters rather than numbers — a submission-mechanics
convention not addressed by FIELD_SKILL. ENFORCED (stated in guidelines;
standard Wiley/APA-adjacent submission mechanics, not separately verifiable
in published output since typesetting removes this by publication).

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting only one abstract, or exceeding 125 words on either the
  Research Summary or Managerial Summary — SEJ enforces both as separate,
  length-capped elements, not one combined abstract.
- Embedding tables/figures in the text body instead of placing them at the
  end of the manuscript with "INSERT TABLE X HERE" placement markers.
- Leaving acknowledgements or funding information in the main text rather
  than confining them to the separate title-page file at submission.
- Using the six-or-more-author et al. threshold inconsistently with
  standard APA (which is more restrictive) — SEJ's guidelines explicitly
  relax this to six-plus.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

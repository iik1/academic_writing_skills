# Journal Writing Skill: South African Journal of Economic and Management Sciences

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** AOSIS (African Online Scientific Information Systems Pty Ltd)
- **Scope:** Peer-reviewed journal publishing empirical research, theoretical analysis, and critical reviews in economics, management sciences, accounting, finance, and business in Southern African context
- **Guidelines URL:** https://sajems.org/index.php/sajems/pages/view/submission-guidelines
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 15+ articles from Vol 29, No 1 (2026); includes empirical studies in earnings management, ESG disclosure, working capital, cybersecurity, and HR management

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure

**Original Research Articles** follow a mandatory structure:
- Title
- Abstract (structured with explicit headings)
- Introduction
- Methods
- Results
- Conclusion
- Acknowledgements
- Competing Interests statement
- Author Contributions (via CRediT Contributor Roles Taxonomy)
- Funding statement
- Data Availability statement
- Disclaimer (if applicable)
- References

**Research Notes** use simpler structure: Title, Abstract (with single "Contribution" heading only), Main Text (with subheadings optional), Acknowledgements, and standard declarations.

**Book Reviews:** Title, Book Details, Main Text (with subheadings), Acknowledgements, declarations.

**Corrections:** Mandated title format: "Corrigendum: [Original Title]"

### Length

- **Original Research:** 7,000 words maximum (excluding abstract, tables, figures, references)
- **Research Notes:** 3,000 words maximum
- **Book Reviews:** 1,000 words maximum
- **Abstracts:** 250 words (Original Research); 100 words (Research Notes); none for Book Reviews

### Abstract

**Mandatory structured format for Original Research** with these section headings presented as explicit labeled subsections:
- Background
- Aim
- Setting
- Method
- Results
- Conclusion
- Contribution

This is a HARD CONSTRAINT: abstract sections must be explicitly labeled with these headings and in this order. Abstracts cannot be unstructured narrative.

**Research Notes** abstracts require only a "Contribution" heading within the abstract.

### Citations

**Harvard referencing style is mandatory.** In-text citations use author-year format (parenthetical or narrative style acceptable). Maximum reference count: 60 (Original Research), 30 (Research Notes), 5 (Book Reviews). All references must include DOI where applicable. Footnotes are discouraged; content should be moved to main text or references.

### Headings

Maximum four hierarchical levels allowed. Each level must be visually distinct using varying font sizes and bold/italic combinations. Numbered sections are not required.

### Tables and Figures

- **Original Research:** 7 tables/figures maximum
- **Research Notes:** 2 maximum
- **Book Reviews:** 1 maximum

All tables and figures must include: legend (below figures, above tables), in-text citation with explanatory sentence, and reference list entry if data source is external. Must comply with AOSIS House Style illustration requirements.

### Language

**British English is mandatory** per Oxford English Dictionary standards (e.g., 's' not 'z' in words like "organisation"). Microsoft Word default language should be set to UK English. Tone is formal and academic.

### Explicit Prohibitions

- Manuscripts must be anonymized for double-blind peer review
- No self-plagiarism permitted
- Footnotes are discouraged (move content to text or references)
- Contractions are not permitted
- Copyright permissions required for quotations exceeding 400 words (prose)
- AI tool usage must be disclosed with specific names, purposes, and statement of author oversight
- Keywords: Eight keywords required, separated by semicolons
- File format restrictions: DOC, DOCX, RTF accepted. LaTeX (.tex) must be converted to Word before submission. No locked/protected Word documents

### Ethical Considerations (for empirical research)

**Original Research requiring ethics approval must include dedicated subsection** with:
- Ethical review committee name
- Study approval number/reference
- Consent method (written or oral)
- Confidentiality measures
- Statement if research was non-human subjects or exempt from review

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Abstract Format
ENFORCED DEVIATION. FIELD_SKILL does not specify abstract structure. SAJEMS enforces rigid structured format with six labeled subsection headings (Background, Aim, Setting, Method, Results, Conclusion, Contribution) as a submission requirement. This is more prescriptive than typical ECON field practice (ABS 4*/4 journals allow unstructured abstracts).

### Person and Voice
RELAXATION. Observed abstracts consistently use third-person construction ("The authors investigated," "This study examined") rather than FIELD_SKILL's near-universal first-person plural ("we show," "we find"). This represents a significant departure from top-tier ECON convention. Full article text likely shifts to first-person active voice in introduction and methods as per FIELD_SKILL, but abstracts maintain formal third-person throughout.

### Word Limits
ENFORCED CONSTRAINT. FIELD_SKILL does not specify word limits by section. SAJEMS enforces tight limits: 7,000 words (Original Research), 3,000 (Research Notes). This forces tighter prose than ABS 4*/4 journals, which typically permit greater length for full empirical studies.

### Citation Density and Format
ENFORCED CONSTRAINT. Mandatory Harvard referencing with maximum reference counts (60, 30, 5 by article type) represents explicit boundary-setting not found in FIELD_SKILL. FIELD_SKILL permits high citation density in introductions without specifying ceilings. SAJEMS caps citations, suggesting lower overall citation intensity, particularly in methods and results.

### Table/Figure Limits
ENFORCED CONSTRAINT. Maximum 7 tables/figures for Original Research is a strict requirement not mentioned in FIELD_SKILL. ABS 4*/4 journals place fewer formal restrictions on figure/table count; SAJEMS requires authors to be selective.

### Explicit Hedging Restrictions
No explicit prohibition, but FIELD_SKILL cautions against excessive hedging. Observed articles use moderate hedging ("may," "could," "suggests," "consistent with") in results and conclusions, consistent with FIELD_SKILL norm that clear claims are preferred but appropriate caution appears in specific contexts (mechanism discussion, external validity, data limitations).

### Footnotes
GUIDELINE ONLY (discouraged but not forbidden). FIELD_SKILL is silent on footnotes. SAJEMS author guidelines explicitly discourage footnotes, requesting content be moved to main text or references. This is a minor style preference, not a hard prohibition, and does not override FIELD_SKILL's silent acceptance of footnotes for supplementary material.

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Sentence and paragraph style:** FIELD DEFAULT (medium to long sentences, high paragraph density, high technical vocabulary density—all consistent with FIELD_SKILL norms)
- **Tense conventions:** FIELD DEFAULT (past tense for procedures, present for model properties and theoretical results—identical to FIELD_SKILL)
- **Introduction structure:** FIELD DEFAULT (motivating hook, research question, approach, main results preview, contributions, roadmap—matches FIELD_SKILL except for third-person abstraction in abstract)
- **Results and interpretation:** FIELD DEFAULT (integrated results and interpretation, tables as primary vehicle, quantitative specificity in reporting, handling of null results—consistent with FIELD_SKILL)
- **Methods and identification:** FIELD DEFAULT (sufficient detail for replication, identification strategy stated clearly, standard methods named and cited—matches FIELD_SKILL norms)
- **Discussion and conclusion:** FIELD DEFAULT (genuine synthesis not verbatim summary, policy/theoretical implications, limitations acknowledgment, future directions—consistent with FIELD_SKILL)
- **Handling disagreement:** FIELD DEFAULT (precision without hostility, acknowledgment of prior work and source of difference—identical to FIELD_SKILL)

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Unstructured abstracts:** The most frequent submission error is likely unstructured abstracts. Every subsection heading (Background, Aim, Setting, Method, Results, Conclusion, Contribution) must be explicitly labeled and appear in order. An abstract without these labels will be desk-rejected or returned for revision.

2. **Exceeding section word limits:** Word counting software may differ; authors often underestimate word count. The 7,000-word ceiling for Original Research is strictly enforced. Tables, figures, and references do not count toward this limit, but the introduction, methods, results, and conclusion all do. Exceeding the limit delays submission.

3. **American English spelling:** British English is mandatory (organisation, favour, realise, etc.). US English spellings will be flagged in peer review and require correction. Set your word processor's language setting to "UK English" at project start.

4. **Missing or inadequate ethical disclosure:** If research involved human subjects, the ethical considerations subsection must name the IRB/ethics committee, provide the approval number, and state the consent and confidentiality methods. Missing or vague ethical statements delay publication. If the research is exempt or non-human subjects, state this explicitly.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

Full-text access to some articles was limited by paywalling, but abstracts, metadata, section headings, and article structure were consistently accessible via the journal website and SciELO SA platform. All articles spot-checked confirmed the structured abstract requirement and Harvard referencing format mandated in the guidelines.

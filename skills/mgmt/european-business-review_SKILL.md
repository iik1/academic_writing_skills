# Journal Writing Skill: European Business Review

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN (and general MGMT)
- **Publisher:** Emerald Group Publishing Ltd
- **Scope:** Publishes innovative, straightforward, challenging, thought-provoking quality articles in management, covering leadership, strategy, business ethics, corporate social responsibility, entrepreneurship, and international business topics.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.emeraldgrouppublishing.com/journal/ebr
- **Field baseline:** mgmt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 8 (abstracts and front matter from Vol. 37, 36, 35; 2024-2025)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict. Source: Emerald Publishing official author guidelines for journal submissions.

### Structure
Manuscripts must follow:
- **Abstract (structured, required)**
- **Keywords**
- **Main sections**: Introduction, Literature Review, Methodology (or Design/Approach), Findings (or Results), Discussion, Conclusion
- **End matter**: Acknowledgments, Declarations of Competing Interest, Data Availability statements (where applicable)

### Length
- **Total manuscript**: Typical range 5,000–8,000 words for research articles
- **Abstract**: Maximum 250 words (including keywords)

### Abstract
**ENFORCED — Mandatory structured format with seven labeled sub-headings:**

1. **Purpose** (What are the reason(s) for writing the paper or the aims of the research?)
2. **Design/methodology/approach** (How are the objectives achieved? Include main method(s).)
3. **Findings** (Main results of your research)
4. **Research limitations/implications** (Optional if not applicable)
5. **Practical implications** (Optional if not applicable)
6. **Social implications** (Optional if not applicable)
7. **Originality/value** (Novel contribution and significance of your work)

Only Purpose, Design/methodology/approach, Findings, and Originality/value are mandatory. No citations in abstract.

### Citations
**ENFORCED — Harvard referencing style** (Emerald Harvard variant, not strict APA):
- Author (Year) or (Author, Year) format
- Multiple citations: alphabetical order within parenthetical: (Jones, 2020; Smith & Lee, 2022)
- Et al. for three or more authors from first citation
- DOI included at end of reference where available
- Full reference list at end; footnotes for definitional clarifications only

### Headings
- Organize by hierarchy: H1 (major sections), H2 (subsections), H3 (sub-subsections)
- Section headings labeled for correct font sizing and alignment

### Tables and Figures
- **Tables**: Numbered in roman numerals (Table I, Table II, etc.); captions placed *above* the table
- **Figures**: Numbered consecutively (Figure 1, Figure 2, etc.); captions placed *below* the figure
- Permission and DOI acknowledgment required in captions where applicable
- Tables submitted separately with clear positioning noted in text

### Explicit Prohibitions
- No previously published work or simultaneous submission to other journals
- Plagiarism checked via Crossref Similarity Check
- Manuscripts without suitable ethical statement and informed consent (where applicable) will be rejected and not reconsidered until clarified

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Abstract Format
**ENFORCED — Structured vs. Field Default (unstructured or structured both acceptable):** European Business Review requires a *mandatory seven-field structured abstract*, whereas FIELD_SKILL notes that management journals use both structured and unstructured abstracts. EBR deviates from field flexibility by requiring the specific seven-field Emerald template. This is a journal-specific hard constraint not typical of all top-tier management outlets.

### Citation Style
**ENFORCED — Harvard vs. Field Default (APA):** EBR uses Harvard referencing (Emerald variant), whereas FIELD_SKILL mandates APA in-text citations. Harvard differs notably: Author (Year) as sentence element vs. APA (Author, Year); different reference list formatting (Author Surname, Initial Year, Title, Source vs. APA's different order). This is journal-specific and non-negotiable.

### Table Numbering
**ENFORCED — Roman numerals vs. Field Default (Arabic):** EBR numbers tables using roman numerals (Table I, Table II), whereas standard management journals use arabic numerals (Table 1, Table 2). Caption placement is also inverted (above table in EBR vs. typical placement varies in field).

### Keywords Requirement
**ENFORCED — Explicit requirement:** EBR requires keywords submission as part of the structured abstract section. FIELD_SKILL does not explicitly mandate keywords, though they are common practice. This is a stated requirement unique to Emerald's process.

### Ethical Disclosure Requirement
**ENFORCED — Strict requirement:** Manuscripts without ethical statement and informed consent documentation (where applicable) are explicitly returned and considered non-existent until revised. FIELD_SKILL mentions "Declarations of Competing Interest" but does not stress the ethical pre-screening gate with the same severity. EBR applies this as a desk-reject criterion.

## Common Pitfalls (EBR-Specific)

1. **Abstract structure violation:** Submitting an unstructured or partially structured abstract. The seven-field format is non-negotiable; desk staff check this before peer review. Missing even optional headers (when applicable) signals inattention to instructions.

2. **Citation style inconsistency:** Mixing APA and Harvard formats, or using author-year without (Year) parentheses in the Harvard Emerald style. Consistency is checked early; mixed styles invite desk rejection or major revision requests.

3. **Ethical statement omission:** Omitting or vague ethical disclosure when human subjects/data are involved. This is treated as a gate: the manuscript will be returned unreviewed until corrected.

4. **Table numbering error:** Using arabic (Table 1) instead of roman (Table I) numerals, or placing captions below rather than above tables. These are formatting-specific to Emerald and stand out as non-compliance.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

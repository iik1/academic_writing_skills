# Journal Writing Skill: Eurasian Business Review

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN
- **Publisher:** Springer Nature (on behalf of Eurasia Business and Economics Society)
- **Scope:** Industrial organization, innovation and management science; empirical or theoretical articles in industrial economics, business economics, innovation management, competition policy, corporate governance, organizational change, entrepreneurship, strategic management, accounting, marketing, HR management, and information systems.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://link.springer.com/journal/40821/submission-guidelines (authentication required for full text; public abstracts available)
- **Field baseline:** mgmt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 9 article titles from 2025-2026 (limited by paywall access; abstracts and metadata observed)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
- Standard structure for empirical work: Abstract → Introduction → [Theoretical Framework or Literature Review] → Methods → Results → Discussion → Conclusion
- Position Articles (authored by leading scholars) present state of art, challenges, and future research directions; no empirical data section required
- Article types: Empirical (quantitative and/or qualitative) and theoretical contributions accepted; Position Articles welcome

### Length
- Maximum 20 pages (450 words per page), including all figures, tables, appendices, and references
- This is substantially shorter than MGMT field baseline (10,000–18,000 words); forces compressed writing

### Abstract
- 150–250 words (unstructured format; no structured abstract with labeled sections)
- Must not contain undefined abbreviations or unspecified references
- No citations within abstract

### Keywords
- 4–6 keywords required for indexing

### Citations
- Harvard style: Author (Year) in-text citations with parenthetical format
- Multiple citations in one parenthetical ordered alphabetically or chronologically: (Jones, 2010; Smith & Lee, 2015)
- Et al. for three or more authors from first citation
- Journal abbreviation: Eurasian Bus. Rev. (ISSN 1309-4297 print; 2147-4281 online)

### Headings
- Decimal numbering system with maximum three levels (e.g., 1., 1.1, 1.1.1)
- Avoid hierarchical-only labeling seen in some MGMT outlets

### Tables and Figures
- Follows standard Springer requirements; included in 20-page limit
- Permissions required if previously published elsewhere

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Manuscripts must be original and not under consideration elsewhere
- Double-blind review requires anonymization: remove author names, affiliations, identifying information from all files
- Submit separate title page with author names and contact details
- Ethical statement required for studies involving human subjects or sensitive data

### Review Process
- Double-blind peer review
- First response target: 3 months (85%+ of submissions); 95%+ receive reports within 4 months
- Acceptance rate: 7% (highly selective)
- Hybrid open-access model available post-acceptance

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Manuscript Length Constraint
**RELAXATION** (stricter than FIELD_SKILL): 20-page limit (450 words/page) is more restrictive than MGMT norms (10,000–18,000 words ~15–25 pages). Quantitative papers with tables and regression results will compress more tightly than AMJ/ASQ standards allow. This forces:
- More concise literature review (introductions typically 500–800 words vs. 800–1,500 in MGMT baseline)
- Condensed theory section with shorter hypothesis development
- Results tables without extensive prose interpretation

### Heading Numbering
**ENFORCED** (differs from FIELD_SKILL): Decimal numbering (1., 1.1, 1.1.1) is required. Many MGMT outlets use hierarchical headings (## Introduction, ### Subheading) without numbering. This journal enforces explicit numbering.

### Article Types
**GUIDELINE ONLY** (addition to FIELD_SKILL): Position Articles by leading scholars are explicitly invited. These are not covered by MGMT baseline; they present state of art and research agenda without empirical data or hypothesis testing. More closely parallel "integrative reviews" than standard MGMT empirical work.

### Quantitative Emphasis
**PRACTICE ONLY** (observed but not stated in available guidelines): Search results and article titles (CEO turnover, ESG performance, sanctions impacts, IT systems effects, stock liquidity) indicate strong skew toward quantitative empirical work with regression-based methods. Unlike AMJ/Organization Science, which balance quantitative and qualitative, EABR heavily favors quantitative hypothesis testing. Qualitative work appears under-represented.

### Citation Density in Introduction
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (citation-dense introduction establishing territory and niche), but compressed into fewer words due to page limit.

### Voice and Hedging
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (moderate hedging, "we" for co-authored work, "Our findings suggest," "We argue").

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Exceeding 20 pages**: Writers accustomed to AMJ/ASQ norms (15,000+ words) often submit 25+ pages. The 20-page limit is hard; every section must be condensed 20–30%.
2. **Using hierarchical headings without numbers**: EABR requires decimal numbering; submitting with unmarked levels (##, ###) will be flagged as non-compliant.
3. **Long literature reviews in introduction**: The page constraint forces a brief intro; over-citing foundational work in introduction wastes space; move detailed synthesis to theory/background section.
4. **Qualitative or mixed-methods papers**: Given quantitative emphasis, qualitative ethnography or case studies face higher bar for acceptance; ensure methodological rigor and sample size are clearly justified.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

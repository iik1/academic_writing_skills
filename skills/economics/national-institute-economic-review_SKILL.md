# Journal Writing Skill: National Institute Economic Review

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Cambridge University Press (on behalf of the National Institute of Economic and Social Research, NIESR)
- **Scope:** Quarterly, policy-facing review covering the current economic situation and prospects in Britain, Europe, and the world; heavy emphasis on macroeconomics, productivity, forecasting, financial stability, training/education, and European integration/Brexit. Mixes peer-reviewed research articles with in-house forecast commentary and notes.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/national-institute-economic-review/information/instructions-contributors (full PDF: "Notes for contributors")
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 10 (titles/abstracts): "The Economics of Walking About and Predicting Unemployment in the USA" (Blanchflower & Bryson); "The UK Productivity Shortfall in an Era of Rising Labour Supply" (Benito & Young); "Open Innovation: Toward an Organizational Digital Innovation" (Rengkung et al.); "Effects of Inflation Expectations on Inflation" (Moessner); "The Curious Case of the National Fund" (Ellison); "Business Investment, Productivity and Brexit"; "Understanding the Effects of Brexit on UK Productivity" (Millard, Nicolae, Nower); "An Assessment of NIESR Forecast Accuracy"; "Commentary: Quantitative Tightening: Protecting Monetary Policy from Fiscal Encroachment"; "World Overview" (regular forecast section).

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No mandated section architecture is specified beyond standard research-article norms; the guidelines are silent on required sections. Distinctive to this journal: it publishes multiple article types side by side — Research Article, Lecture, Review Article, Book Review, and short unrefereed "Commentary"/"Note" pieces plus the recurring in-house "World Overview" and "UK Economic Outlook" forecast sections. A submitted research article should follow ordinary empirical/theory structure per FIELD_SKILL, but authors should recognize the journal's house content also includes non-standard, non-IMRaD forecast commentary that does not follow academic norms — this is an editorial/staff genre, not a model for submitted research articles.

### Length
Explicit and specific: articles should be **no more than 8,000–10,000 words**, preferably. This is stricter and more concrete than FIELD_SKILL, which specifies no length norm.

### Abstract
Explicit and short: **around 100 words** (much shorter than typical ABS 4/4* abstracts), to appear "as an introduction to the article in the Review." Unstructured. Must be accompanied by **up to 5 keywords** and **JEL classification codes** (both required, not optional).

### Citations
Author-year in prose is field default (matches FIELD_SKILL), but the journal specifies references appear **at the end of the article as a numbered end-list in a specific format**: `Smith, J. (1995), 'Article Title', Title of Journal, vol., no., pp. 00–00.` for articles and `Smith, J. (1995), Book Title, City/Country, Publisher.` for books. Notes and references are combined at the end; footnote-style acknowledgements go at the foot of the first page marked with an asterisk, excluded from note numbering.

### Headings
Not specified beyond general structure; no explicit heading-numbering convention given.

### Tables and Figures
Explicit and specific — a delta from FIELD_SKILL's silence on this: every table and chart must include a heading, definitions, units, and source of data. Charts should be supplied in Excel (not embedded as pictures in Word) with accompanying data; the journal prints only in black and red, so authors are asked to avoid multi-color charts (patterns should be used instead of colors for differentiation). Stata graphs, if used, should be sized to fit an 85mm column width and supplied without the default blue-grey shadow.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Submission implies the paper is unpublished and not under consideration elsewhere (standard).
- Authors must have secured permission for any previously published chart/table before submission; copyright problems are noted as a common cause of production delay.
- Abbreviations must be spelled out in full on first use.
- **English (British) spelling required, not American spelling** — an explicit, journal-specific mechanical requirement not present in FIELD_SKILL.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
No delta observed in the peer-reviewed research articles sampled (Blanchflower & Bryson, Benito & Young, Moessner) — first-person plural, direct claims, and field-standard register are consistent with FIELD_SKILL. PRACTICE ONLY / low-prevalence note: the journal's in-house "World Overview"/"Commentary" content (not authored via the normal peer-review submission route) is written in a more institutional, forecasting-bulletin register, but this does not apply to submitted research articles and is not a stated norm for contributors.

### Citation Density
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm in the sampled research articles.

### Structural Deviations
GUIDELINE ONLY / ENFORCED distinction: the 100-word abstract cap and 8-10k word article ceiling are both stated (ENFORCED — HARD_CONSTRAINTS) and plausible given the journal's short, policy-note-length articles compared to ABS 4/4* norms; this is a RELAXATION appropriate to ABS 1 tier, not a new norm to generalize elsewhere.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
The journal's mixed content model (peer-reviewed research alongside institute-authored forecasts and short commentaries) means the "journal" as a whole is broader in genre than a typical ABS journal; authors submitting a research article should ignore the forecast/commentary sections as a stylistic model.

## Common Pitfalls
- Writing an ABS-4*-length abstract (150-250 words): this journal wants roughly 100 words.
- Embedding charts as pictures inside the Word file instead of supplying Excel source charts with data, or using multi-color charts that cannot survive black-and-red print reproduction.
- Omitting JEL codes or keywords, both of which are explicitly requested in addition to the abstract.
- Using American spelling instead of British spelling.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

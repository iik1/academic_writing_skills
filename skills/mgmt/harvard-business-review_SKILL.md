# Journal Writing Skill: Harvard Business Review

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN
- **Publisher:** Harvard Business School Publishing
- **Scope:** General-management practitioner magazine for senior business leaders; covers strategy, leadership, culture, operations, innovation, communication, talent, and career topics. Not a peer-reviewed academic research journal.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://hbr.org/guidelines-for-authors
- **Field baseline:** mgmt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 5 (guidelines page + "Bring Your Extended Leadership Team into Strategy Decisions" Dec 2025; "A Counterintuitive Approach to Leadership" Nov 2025; "Your AI Strategy Needs More Than a Single Leader" Aug 2025; "When You Have to Execute a Strategy You Disagree With" Dec 2025; "Great Leaders Empower Strategic Decision-Making Across the Organization" Nov 2025 — titles/summaries only, full text paywalled)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No prescribed section template (no Abstract/Theory/Methods/Results/Discussion). Articles are narrative essays built around one central "aha" idea, typically opening with a concrete example or scenario rather than a literature review. A short "Summary" or "Idea in Brief" style overview may appear near the top on the published page (site-generated), not authored as a formal abstract.

### Length
Full-length magazine articles run roughly 2,000–4,000 words (submission pitches are 500–750 words / 2–3 pages); shorter web-only pieces run 200–1,000 words. No stated page limit in the academic sense.

### Abstract
No structured or unstructured academic abstract. Pitches instead answer: what is the central message/"aha"; what is new, useful, or counterintuitive; why senior leaders need to know it; how it applies in practice ("so what"); source of the author's expertise; supporting research/examples.

### Citations
No formal in-text citation apparatus (no APA-style parentheticals, no reference list). Sources, data, and prior work are named narratively in the prose (e.g., "A 2024 study by X found…") or via linked text on the web. HBR explicitly expects "rigorous" sourcing and disclosure of financial ties, but not academic-style citation formatting.

### Headings
Headings are short, punchy, and often imperative or benefit-framed (e.g., "Start with the extended team," "Name the tension"), not generic labels like "Discussion" or "Results."

### Tables and Figures
Not typical; when used, figures are simple, magazine-style infographics rather than regression tables or data displays.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- No previously published work.
- No promotional content for the author's own products/services/consulting.
- Must disclose financial relationships with any company mentioned (consulting, board seats, etc.).
- Must disclose any use of generative AI tools to the editor.
- Ideas "should not be easily replicable by simply asking a large language model" — originality bar is explicit and stated in these terms.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates.

### Voice and Register
RELAXATION / FIELD-INAPPROPRIATE: FIELD_SKILL's academic register (hedged claims, "we argue," theory-building prose) does not apply. HBR articles use direct second-person address to the reader ("you," "your team"), open with anecdotes or rhetorical questions rather than territory-establishing citations, and adopt a confident, advisory, sometimes contrarian tone signaled in titles ("counterintuitive," "your X needs more than…"). First-person "we"/"I" is used sparingly and mainly to reference the author's own experience or research, not to narrate a theory-building argument.

### Statistical Reporting
RELAXATION: No coefficient reporting, hypothesis-testing language, or effect-size conventions. Evidence is presented as illustrative examples, case vignettes, or loosely cited studies/data points, not as regression tables.

### Citation Density
RELAXATION: Citation density is very low to none, in contrast to FIELD_SKILL's citation-dense Introduction/Theory expectation. Prior work, if mentioned, is named in prose without a reference list.

### Structural Deviations
FIELD DEFAULT DOES NOT APPLY: None of the four FIELD_SKILL structural templates (quantitative deductive, qualitative inductive, theoretical/conceptual, integrative review) apply. There are no hypotheses, no propositions, no Methods/Results sections, and no formal Discussion/Conclusion split. Structure is a single narrative argument organized around 3–5 short, prescriptive subheadings building to practical takeaways.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
- Titles are typically prescriptive or provocative statements/questions aimed at a managerial action or tension (e.g., "When You Have to Execute a Strategy You Disagree With"), not descriptive noun-phrase titles common in academic MGMT journals.
- Content must be actionable: articles close with practical guidance a leader can apply, not a research agenda or limitations section.
- Author bio with credentials/links appears embedded in the piece, functioning as the "expertise" signal in place of an institutional affiliation footnote.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- Underlying subject matter (leadership, strategy, ethics/CSR-adjacent management topics): FIELD DEFAULT
- Expectation of author expertise/credibility: FIELD DEFAULT (though signaled via bio/track record here rather than institutional affiliation)

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting an academic-style manuscript (literature review, hypotheses, regression tables) instead of a practitioner narrative essay — HBR will reject on fit, not on rigor.
- Opening with a citation-dense "territory" paragraph instead of a concrete scenario or the central "aha."
- Writing for an academic peer audience instead of "senior leaders driving change," per HBR's own stated criterion.
- Omitting disclosure of financial ties to companies mentioned, or of generative-AI use in drafting — both explicitly required.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Industrial Economics

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Wiley-Blackwell
- **Scope:** Industrial organization — theoretical and empirical analysis of firm behavior, market structure, competition, antitrust, innovation, and regulation.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/page/journal/14676451/homepage/forauthors.html (also mirrored at https://jindec.ulb.ac.be/?q=page/instructions-authors)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 10 (abstracts/front-matter only, full text paywalled) — Moschini & Perry "Innovation, Licensing, and Competition: Evidence From Genetically Engineered Crops" (2026); Harrington "Hub-and-Spoke Collusion With a Third-Party Pricing Algorithm" (2026); Bourreau, Raizonville & Thébaudin "Interoperability Between Ad-Financed Platforms With Endogenous Multi-Homing" (2026); Siebert, Linde & Norton "The Determinants of Negotiated Pharmaceutical Prices" (2026); Mamrak "Patents, Antitrust, and Innovation: Evidence From the Xerox Case" (2026); Jansen "Share to Scare: Technology Sharing in the Absence of Intellectual Property Rights" (2026); Jin, Lu, Zhou & Fang "Flagship Entry in Online Marketplaces" (2026); Scott & Qin "The High Cost of Low Range: Estimating the Hidden Cost of Range Anxiety in Electric Vehicles" (2026); Shiller "Inconspicuous Personalized Pricing" (2025)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Title page separate from main text, containing title, author names/affiliations/emails, the abstract, and acknowledgments. No other mandated section structure beyond FIELD_SKILL norms. Editors also accept short Notes (reporting significant new data/results or brief comments on prior Journal content) and substantial review articles; book reviews are not published. Empirical papers are explicitly required to have "a sound theoretical base," and case studies must be motivated by and inform economic theory rather than being purely descriptive — a stronger theory-grounding requirement than the ECON baseline states.

### Length
No explicit word/page cap stated for the main manuscript; double-spaced, single-sided, wide margins, consecutively numbered pages, Times New Roman 12pt preferred.

### Abstract
Strictly capped at 100 words (JEL standard) — far shorter than typical ABS 4/4* practice. No equations, diagrams, or footnotes permitted (plain numbers are fine). Central message must appear in the first sentence; must identify geographic scope. Single authors are instructed to write "I" and use direct phrasing ("I find...") rather than "This paper finds..."; editorial "we" is explicitly discouraged for single-authored papers (this is a single-author-only rule — see Voice and Register below for multi-author practice).

### Citations
In-text citations show the year in square brackets (not the parenthetical author-year format that dominates top ECON journals, though author name placement is otherwise similar). Journal titles are spelled out in full, never abbreviated. Working papers are referenced as book references with institutional location.

### Headings
Major section headings use Roman numerals and should be kept short. Subsections are to be minimized; when unavoidable, use numerical notation like II(i), II(ii) rather than descriptive subheadings.

### Tables and Figures
Figures numbered consecutively in Arabic numerals, submitted as TIF or EPS. Tables numbered in Roman numerals, centered, with titles in small caps. Table notes are prefaced with the literal word "Notes." Only essential tables belong in the main text; ancillary/supplementary tables are directed to an online supplement rather than the print manuscript.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Manuscript must be unpublished and not under consideration elsewhere. Avoid potentially defamatory material. Mathematical notation must avoid ambiguous symbols (e.g., distinguish 1 vs. l, × vs. x vs. χ); sentences should not begin with a mathematical symbol. Formulae central to the argument must be displayed and numbered (1), (2), etc. on the left margin.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
Guidelines push single authors toward "I" and away from "we," a RELAXATION/refinement of the FIELD_SKILL first-person-plural default that applies only to sole-authored papers — the sampled multi-author abstracts (Bourreau et al., Jin et al., Siebert et al.) use "we" consistently, matching FIELD_SKILL. ENFORCED for single-author papers (guideline stated, and consistent with the journal's stronger overall push toward direct, compact phrasing in the abstract).

### Abstract Format
GUIDELINE-driven delta not present in FIELD_SKILL at all (FIELD_SKILL is silent on abstract mechanics): the 100-word ceiling, ban on equations/footnotes in the abstract, and requirement to state geographic scope are ENFORCED norms specific to this journal, confirmed by the consistently short, single-paragraph abstracts observed across sampled articles.

### Structural Deviations
Heavier theory-grounding requirement for empirical/case-study papers than FIELD_SKILL implies — ENFORCED per guidelines; sampled empirical papers (Moschini & Perry, Siebert et al., Scott & Qin) all pair estimation with an explicit structural or theoretical model rather than pure reduced-form description, consistent with this requirement.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Roman-numeral section headings with minimized subsectioning is a distinctive structural convention (FIELD_SKILL allows either Roman or Arabic numerals with no subsection guidance) — GUIDELINE ONLY, not verifiable from abstract-level spot-checks.

## Common Pitfalls
- Writing a 150–250 word abstract in the style typical of ABS 4/4* journals — this journal enforces a hard 100-word JEL-style cap with no equations or footnotes.
- Using "we" in a single-authored paper's abstract instead of "I" and indirect phrasing ("this paper shows") instead of direct phrasing ("I find").
- Treating an empirical/case-study contribution as purely descriptive without an explicit theoretical or structural frame — editors have signaled this is a required, not optional, element.
- Using parenthetical author-year citations instead of square-bracket year citations, or abbreviating journal titles in the reference list.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

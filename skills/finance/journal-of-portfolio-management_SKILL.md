# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Portfolio Management

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** FINANCE
- **Publisher:** Portfolio Management Research (PMR), part of the "with. Intelligence" group
- **Scope:** Practitioner-oriented research for institutional investors and asset managers on portfolio theory, asset allocation, security selection, risk management, performance measurement, and factor/quantitative investing.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.pm-research.com/sites/default/files/2023-03/PMR_Article_Submission_Guidelines_2023.pdf (PMR family-wide "Article Submission Guidelines" memo, applies to JPM); author landing page at https://jpm.pm-research.com/authors
- **Field baseline:** finance-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 8 (full text: Jacobs & Levy, "Building on Finance Theory to Forge the Future of Investment Practice," 50th Anniversary Issue 2024. Abstract/front-matter only: Chambers, Dimson & Ilmanen, "The Norway Model" (2012); Bonaparte & Fabozzi, "Catching the FoMO Fever: A Look at Fear in Finance" (2025); Philips, "Estimating Expected Returns: Then and Now" (2024); Easley, López de Prado & O'Hara, "The Volume Clock" (2012); plus 3 additional titles surfaced via search (Swade et al. "Factor Zoo," Brown/Fei/Robinson "Portfolio Management in Private Equity," and the 2025 Special Issue Editor's Introduction on Factor-Based Investing) for title/topic pattern only, not structural detail)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
First page contains ONLY title, date, author names/contact info, one-sentence author role/affiliation bios (e.g., "John Doe is the chief investment officer of Acme Investment Management, Inc."), and corresponding-author designation. Second page contains ONLY title, date, abstract, three-item "Key Findings"/"highlights" list, keywords, and JEL codes. Main text begins on the third page. No heading appears before the introductory material — the article opens directly into unheaded introductory prose, followed by a "roadmap" paragraph, then the first heading. Up to three heading levels: top-level bold uppercase, second-level indented bold, third-level bold in-line paragraph headings.

### Length
Target 4,000 words (excluding abstract, exhibits, references); rarely accepted below 2,500 or above 7,500 words. Detailed empirical results and proofs are pushed to an online supplement (not counted in length) rather than the main text or a print appendix.

### Abstract
Target 160 words (much shorter than the FIELD_SKILL 150–250 word norm skews toward the low end here). Must be non-technical, must contain no references, must define acronyms on first use. A mandatory three-item bulleted "Key Findings"/"key takeaways" list accompanies the abstract, each item one or two sentences, max 40 words.

### Citations
Author–date format per Chicago Manual of Style (CMS) 17th edition, Ch. 15 (not a generic author-year style — CMS-specific conventions for hyphenation, abbreviation, and capitalization apply throughout).

### Headings
Three-level max, with specific bold/uppercase/indentation formatting per level (see Structure above). Distinct from the field default of plain Arabic-numbered headings — no numbering is used; formatting (case/indent/inline) signals level instead.

### Tables and Figures
All tabular AND graphical material is called an "Exhibit" (never "Table" or "Figure"), numbered consecutively in one Arabic-numeral sequence regardless of type. Detailed mechanical specs govern exhibit typography (9-pt+ Times New Roman for table cells, 12-pt bold for exhibit titles, decimal-point alignment, specific chart line/gridline/axis conventions) — exceptional level of prescriptive formatting detail for a finance journal. Equations must be displayed on separate lines, numbered at the right margin, with notation explained in a text box; non-conforming equation formatting can trigger a return for resubmission even after acceptance.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Manuscripts "clearly written primarily for an academic audience will be rejected at the preliminary review" — an explicit, enforced prohibition on academic register. Simultaneous submission to more than one PMR-family journal is prohibited. Upon acceptance, authors must remove all prior versions from SSRN/ResearchGate/other public sites, and their institution must not require public posting — a data/preprint-availability posture opposite to the FIELD_SKILL norm of routine replication/preprint availability.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
ENFORCED / RELAXATION. The guidelines explicitly instruct authors to "write in plain English, minimizing the use of technical jargon" for an audience of investment professionals rather than academics — a real relaxation of FIELD_SKILL's tolerance for high technical density and jargon-without-apology. In practice this is inconsistently realized: shorter, practitioner-authored pieces (e.g., "The Norway Model") are genuinely plain-language and citation-light in the abstract, but longer pieces by academically active authors (e.g., Jacobs & Levy) retain dense citation, formal econometric/optimization vocabulary, and footnoted technical asides much closer to FIELD_SKILL's ABS 4 register. Treat the plain-English instruction as a real but unevenly enforced norm — lean toward accessible framing in the abstract, introduction, and conclusion even if technical density in the middle sections resembles the field baseline.

### Structural Deviations
ENFORCED. The three-page front-matter template (title/author page, then abstract/highlights/keywords page, then main text) and the mandatory "Key Findings" three-item bullet list are journal-specific structural requirements with no FIELD_SKILL counterpart — FIELD_SKILL abstracts are single-paragraph with no companion bullet list. Also ENFORCED: no heading precedes the introduction (FIELD_SKILL doesn't specify this one way or the other, but JPM is explicit that introductory material is unheaded).

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
ENFORCED. Universal "Exhibit" terminology replacing Table/Figure, confirmed in the sampled article (Jacobs & Levy uses "Exhibit 1," "Exhibit 2," "Exhibit 3" for line/frontier charts). Footnotes (not endnotes) are used for tangential commentary rather than for references — consistent with the sampled article's extensive footnoting of asides, definitions, and secondary citations. Author bio lines follow the exact "[Name] is [role] of [firm]" template in the sampled article.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- Argumentation structure (opening significance, gap, contribution list, roadmap): FIELD DEFAULT — the sampled article's introduction and conclusion follow the FIELD_SKILL pattern (contributions framed as "First... Second... Third... Fourth...", roadmap-style closing).
- Hedging and epistemic calibration: FIELD DEFAULT.
- Conclusion structure and length: FIELD DEFAULT (short, restates findings, closes with a forward-looking/practitioner-relevance note).
- Robustness/identification treatment: FOLLOWS FIELD_SKILL norm where applicable, though JPM's more conceptual/applied pieces often lack a formal empirical identification section entirely — this is a function of article type (many JPM pieces are applied/conceptual rather than reduced-form empirical), not a stated journal norm.

---

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting an academic-register manuscript: guidelines state this triggers rejection at preliminary review before it reaches peer review.
- Labeling exhibits as "Table 1" or "Figure 1" instead of a single continuous "Exhibit" sequence — explicitly flagged as incorrect in the guidelines.
- Pasting tables/charts into Word as static images instead of native Word tables or linked Excel data — explicitly prohibited because underlying data must be reproducible.
- Omitting the three-item Key Findings/highlights list, which is a mandatory companion to the abstract, not optional supplementary material.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Field Writing Style: INNOV

## Scope and Intended Use
This baseline describes the normative writing conventions expected at the
top of the innovation studies / innovation management field, as evidenced
by its ABS 4* and ABS 4 journals. It is intended as a prior for any
journal-specific writing skill in this field: journal-specific skills
should assume everything below unless they explicitly state a deviation.
The baseline covers empirical, conceptual, and review-style contributions,
since leading INNOV journals routinely publish all three.

## Typical Article Structure
Leading journals in this field expect a recognizable IMRaD-like macro
structure, adapted to accommodate both quantitative and qualitative work:
abstract, introduction, theoretical background/literature review,
hypotheses or research questions (for quantitative work) or conceptual
framework (for qualitative/conceptual work), data and methods, results,
discussion, and conclusion (often including limitations, theoretical and
managerial/policy implications, and future research directions). A
structured abstract or a highlights-style summary of purpose, approach,
and findings is treated as near-universal front matter. Keywords
following the abstract are expected. Distinct discussion and conclusion
sections are the norm, though shorter conceptual pieces may fold these
together. Standalone "theoretical contribution" subsections within the
discussion are common but not universal. Practice varies on whether
managerial/practitioner implications receive their own subsection or are
woven into the discussion: no consensus norm exists.

## Voice, Person, and Register
Register is formal and impersonal in framing sections (introduction,
literature review) but shifts to active, first-person plural voice ("we
find," "we argue," "our analysis shows") once authors report their own
analytical choices and results. Passive constructions remain common in
methods sections when describing procedures applied to data ("data were
collected," "responses were coded"), coexisting with active voice for
authorial decisions ("we collected," "we coded"). First-person singular
is avoided even in single-authored pieces; "the author" or passive
constructions substitute. Overall tone is measured and non-polemical:
disagreement with prior work is expressed as an unresolved tension or
gap rather than as direct criticism of named authors.

## Sentence and Paragraph Style
Sentences are typically medium to long, often compound-complex, reflecting
the need to qualify claims and situate them relative to prior literature.
Simple declarative sentences are used to state key claims or findings,
usually followed by elaboration or qualification. Paragraphs in the
introduction and literature review build cumulatively toward a stated
gap or contribution rather than front-loading conclusions; paragraphs in
results and discussion sections are more likely to open with the claim
and follow with supporting detail. Technical and field-specific
terminology (e.g., absorptive capacity, ambidexterity, open innovation,
disruptive innovation) is used precisely and assumed familiar to the
reader without extensive definition, except when a paper is explicitly
introducing or reconceptualizing a construct.

## Argumentation and Rhetorical Moves
Introductions follow a CARS-like (Create a Research Space) pattern:
establish the importance and broad relevance of the phenomenon, review
what is established in prior literature, identify a specific gap,
tension, contradiction, or unanswered question, and state how the paper
addresses it. Gaps are framed constructively — as underexplored
dimensions, unresolved tensions between literatures, or boundary
conditions not yet tested — rather than as flaws or errors in prior
work. Contributions are typically stated explicitly and enumerated
(e.g., "this paper contributes in three ways") near the end of the
introduction, and echoed again in the discussion. Positioning relative
to multiple theoretical lenses or literatures (e.g., resource-based
view, dynamic capabilities, institutional theory) is expected, with the
paper's approach justified as integrating or extending these lenses.

## Methods Conventions
Methods sections are written primarily in past tense, describing what
was done in a specific study. The level of procedural detail is
substantial: sample construction and justification, data sources,
variable operationalization or coding procedures (for qualitative work),
and analytical technique are all expected to be reported with enough
detail to support replication or evaluation of rigor. Quantitative
papers report model specification, estimation technique, and robustness
checks (alternative specifications, endogeneity treatment, subsample
analysis) as a standard expectation rather than an optional addition.
Qualitative and case-based papers report sampling logic, data sources
(interviews, archival material, observation), and coding/analysis
procedures (e.g., grounded theory, thematic analysis) with comparable
procedural detail, along with explicit discussion of trustworthiness or
validity safeguards. Practice varies on where robustness checks are
placed (main text vs. appendix/online supplement): no consensus norm
exists.

## Results and Reporting Norms
Quantitative results are reported with precision, combining tables
(descriptive statistics, correlation matrices, regression or model
output) with prose that interprets rather than merely restates the
numbers. Statistical significance is reported using conventional
thresholds and precise estimates rather than vague characterizations.
Interpretation of practical or economic significance, not just
statistical significance, is expected. Qualitative results are typically
organized thematically or around the emergent framework, supported by
illustrative evidence (quotations, vignettes, or representative
examples) integrated into the narrative rather than presented in raw
form. Figures depicting conceptual frameworks or process models are
common in both quantitative and qualitative papers.

## Discussion and Conclusion Norms
Discussion sections restate key findings and explicitly connect them
back to the gap identified in the introduction, emphasizing theoretical
contribution before turning to practical or managerial implications.
Limitations are disclosed explicitly and specifically (not merely as a
formulaic disclaimer), typically followed by concrete directions for
future research that extend from those limitations or from unresolved
tensions in the findings. Managerial, practitioner, or policy
implications are expected in most empirical papers, though their
prominence and placement vary: no consensus norm exists on whether they
warrant a dedicated subsection.

## Citation and Literature Engagement
Citation density is highest in the introduction and literature
review/theoretical background sections, moderate in the discussion, and
lowest in results. Citations are used overwhelmingly to paraphrase and
synthesize prior findings rather than to quote directly; direct
quotation is reserved almost exclusively for qualitative evidence (e.g.,
interview excerpts) rather than for citing other scholars' claims.
Disagreement or contradiction across prior studies is presented as a
pattern spanning multiple works ("studies disagree on...", "findings are
mixed regarding...") rather than as a critique of a specific named
author or paper. Synthesis across streams of literature — rather than
sequential summary of individual studies — is expected in the
literature review.

## Hedging and Epistemic Caution
Moderate hedging is standard: claims are qualified with terms such as
"suggests," "may," "appears to," or "is associated with," particularly
when generalizing beyond the study's specific sample or context.
Hedging intensifies when discussing causal interpretation of
non-experimental data and when extrapolating findings to contexts,
industries, or firm types not directly studied. Confidence increases
when a claim is directly supported by the paper's own results, and
authors are willing to state findings plainly in that case; hedging is
reserved for extrapolation, boundary conditions, and engagement with
mixed prior evidence.

## What This Baseline Does NOT Specify
This baseline does not specify word or page limits, reference list
formatting or citation style (e.g., APA vs. author-date variants),
number of permitted tables/figures, abstract word counts, submission or
formatting requirements, turnaround expectations, or preferences for
specific theoretical lenses, methods, or epistemological traditions
(e.g., positivist vs. interpretivist). These are left to
journal-specific skills, which should record only where they deviate
from or add detail to this baseline.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Management, Spirituality and Religion

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN
- **Publisher:** International Association of Management, Spirituality & Religion (IAMSR); distributed by Taylor & Francis/Routledge (published by Routledge until Jan 2021)
- **Scope:** Interdisciplinary forum on relationships between management, spirituality, religion, and society across all spiritual/religious traditions; publishes conceptual, theoretical, and empirical work as well as critical, theological, and philosophical reflections. Serves management/organizational scholars, religious studies/practical theology academics, business ethics/CSR researchers, and practicing managers.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.iamsr.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/2024-03-03_JMSR_Author_Submission_Guidelines_for_web.pdf (dated March 2024)
- **Field baseline:** mgmt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 12 titles/abstracts via abstract-page snippets, ResearchGate/Ingenta mirrors, and search-engine extracts (tandfonline.com blocks direct fetch/scrape with 403; full text was not accessible). Sample spans 2020–2025 and includes quantitative (SEM/survey), qualitative (interview/case study), and conceptual/theoretical pieces: "Spirituality as reinforcement of people-focused work: a philosophical foundation" (2020); "Reconceptualizing organizational spirituality: Theological roots for scientific and practical fruits" (2020); "Aligning Spiritual Values for Meaningful Work: An Empirical Study of Person-Organization Fit in Vietnam" (2025); "Fostering Sustainability Through Workplace Spirituality: A Qualitative Study of Three Case Study Organizations in a Muslim Country's Context" (2024); plus 8 additional abstracts on workplace spirituality, spiritual leadership, Sufism/decoloniality critique, and spiritual capital identified via search snippets.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No fixed section template is mandated beyond standard scholarly convention. Manuscript is submitted with title page (author contact details) separate from the anonymized manuscript body. Track Changes and self-identifying references must be removed for double-blind review.

### Length
Submissions generally should not exceed 10,000 words including references (longer accepted in special cases). Regular papers typically run 5,000–9,000 words — notably shorter than the FIELD_SKILL norm of 10,000–18,000+ words for top management outlets. This is a RELAXATION consistent with ABS 1 tier.

### Abstract
150 words maximum (unstructured), stricter than the FIELD_SKILL 150–250 word range. Requires three mandatory keywords selected from the JMSR ScholarOne controlled keyword list, plus three free-choice keywords — a six-keyword requirement not present in FIELD_SKILL.

### Citations
Latest APA style, including DOIs for all references where available. This matches FIELD_SKILL (APA), with the added DOI requirement as journal-specific practice.

### Headings
Primary, secondary, and tertiary headings only — explicitly NOT numbered headings. This is a specific formatting instruction not covered in FIELD_SKILL (which does not address numbered vs. unnumbered headings).

### Tables and Figures
Maximum of six tables/figures per manuscript. All tables/figures placed on separate pages at the end of the manuscript after the references (not embedded in text), with an in-text placement marker (e.g., "Insert Table 1 about here"). Footnotes to be used sparingly. Tables may be single-spaced/smaller font; body text is double-spaced, 12-point Times New Roman.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Manuscript must be de-identified for double-blind review (no self-citation giveaways, anonymized Word metadata).
- Must not be under review elsewhere or previously published; no plagiarism.
- Generative AI may only be used to improve readability/language, not for substantive content generation; cannot be listed as an author. Disclosure required in a dedicated section titled "Declaration of Generative AI and AI-assisted technologies in the writing process," placed after the body text and before the References list, using a specified statement template. No disclosure needed if no such tool was used (routine grammar/spellcheck tools and AI used for data analysis are exempt from disclosure).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Structural Deviations
Shorter typical length (5,000–9,000 words) and a hard 6-table/figure cap are the main structural deltas; otherwise sampled articles follow standard IMRaD/theory-building templates consistent with FIELD_SKILL, with "Findings" used for qualitative work and "Results" for quantitative work as expected.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Scope draws explicitly on theological, philosophical, and critical/decolonial framing (e.g., Sufism, Islamic spirituality, theological roots of organizational constructs) alongside conventional management theory — broader interdisciplinary citation base than a typical mgmt-field article, spanning religious studies and philosophy sources in addition to management literature. This is PRACTICE ONLY (a function of scope, not a stated guideline requirement), consistent with the journal's five-community readership stated in its aims.

## Common Pitfalls
- Exceeding the 150-word abstract cap (tighter than most management journals) or omitting the required three controlled keywords from JMSR's ScholarOne list.
- Embedding tables/figures in the body text instead of placing them on separate pages after the references with an in-text callout.
- Using numbered section headings instead of the required primary/secondary/tertiary heading style.
- Omitting the mandatory AI-use declaration section (or its exact heading) when generative AI tools were used in drafting.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

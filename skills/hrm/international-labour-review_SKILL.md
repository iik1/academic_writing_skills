# Journal Writing Skill: International Labour Review

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** HRM&EMP (multidisciplinary)
- **Publisher:** Open Library of Humanities (transitioned April 2025; formerly Wiley-Blackwell)
- **Scope:** Multidisciplinary peer-reviewed journal covering labour and employment across economics, law, industrial relations, social policy, sociology, and history. Established 1921.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.ilo.org/resource/manuscript-submission-guidelines
- **Field baseline:** hrm-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 5 (Wright 2024, Grimshaw 2024, and references to special features on collective bargaining and paid domestic work, Vol. 163 Issues 3-4, 2024)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

### Structure
Manuscripts must be submitted in three separate files:
1. Title page (with author names, affiliations, and contact information)
2. Main text file
3. Tables, figures, appendices, and supporting information (separate files)

All submissions should be double-blind (author-identifying information removed from main text).

### Length
**7,000–10,000 words total**, including tables, boxes, footnotes, and references. This is a hard upper limit and represents a tighter constraint than the field baseline allows. Even for complex empirical work, the journal enforces word economy.

### Abstract
**Maximum 100 words** (unstructured). Keywords are required but word limit not specified in public guidelines.

### Citations
**Chicago Manual of Style, author-date system.** References arranged alphabetically by first author's surname (inverted). For works with >10 authors, list first 7 followed by "et al." When citing electronic sources, include DOI if available; URL may substitute. Access dates required only if publication/revision date unavailable. In-text citations use parenthetical form (Author Year: page).

### Headings
No explicit guideline published; observe journal practice from Vol. 163 (2024) articles. Typical structure appears to follow IMRaD-style sections (introduction, literature/theory, methods if empirical, findings/results, discussion, conclusion) with decimal numbering or section names (not specified in guidelines).

### Formatting
- **Font:** Times New Roman, 12pt
- **Line spacing:** Double-spaced
- **File format:** Word document (.doc or .docx)
- **Submission language:** English, French, or Spanish (if translated, provide original also)

### Tables and Figures
Final versions must be provided in Excel or vector graphics format. Must be numbered and captioned. Integrate references to tables/figures into text.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Plagiarism screening applied at submission
- Double-blind peer review enforced; identifying information must be removed from main text
- Conflict of interest statement required
- Funding sources must be disclosed in acknowledgments section
- All cited sources must appear in reference list and vice versa

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Tightness
**ENFORCED.** The 7,000–10,000-word constraint is more binding than ABS 4*/4 field norms. Field baseline expects detailed literature reviews, methods sections, and discussion of implications; this journal requires the same content compressed into a tighter envelope. This privileges editorial economy and policy relevance over methodological or theoretical elaboration.

### Citation Density
**ENFORCED / PRACTICE.** Chicago author-date is standard, but the word limit has a disciplinary effect: high citation density in literature/theory sections must yield to brevity. Observe that Grimshaw et al. (2024) and Wright (2024) articles in Vol. 163 Issue 4 follow dense citation in early sections but economize heavily in results/findings.

### Multidisciplinary Framing
**PRACTICE.** Unlike the ABS 4*/4 baseline (which assumes psychology/organizational behaviour framing), ILR explicitly welcomes law, labour process, and political economy framings. Articles may foreground institutional, legal, or historical perspectives rather than individual-level mechanisms. The field baseline notes this variance exists but does not specify prevalence; ILR institutionalizes it. No change to voice or hedging required, but theoretical repertoire extends beyond AMO/social exchange/psychological contract framing.

### Policy and Practice Implications
**ENFORCED.** The journal is explicitly oriented to "foster informed policy debates." Discussion sections consistently include explicit paragraphs addressing implications for labour law, ILO standards, trade union strategy, or employment regulation. This is more strongly stated in scope than the field baseline implies, which treats practical implications as "required" but does not emphasise policy influence as a core mission.

### Quantitative-Qualitative Balance
**FIELD DEFAULT.** The baseline notes that no single quantitative approach is obligatory in HRM journals; ILR extends this further. The journal publishes empirical quantitative work (regression-based studies, panel designs), qualitative studies (interviews, ethnography), and conceptual/review articles with equal standing. No presumption that quantitative designs are "gold standard."

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Voice and register:** FIELD DEFAULT. Formal, impersonal with first-person plural permitted (we, our) in introduction and methods. Same hedging intensity (moderate–high). Qualitative papers expected to include participant quotations as evidence.
- **Sentence and paragraph style:** FIELD DEFAULT. Medium-to-long sentences with subordination for qualification and citation. Paragraphs structured around single claims with topic sentences.
- **Argumentation (CARS structure):** FIELD DEFAULT. Introductions establish practical/theoretical importance, identify gap, and state contribution explicitly.
- **Methods reporting:** FIELD DEFAULT. Quantitative papers report sample, measures (with reliability), estimation strategy; robustness and endogeneity discussed where relevant. Qualitative papers report sampling, data collection, analytic approach, reflexivity, and ethical approval.
- **Results and reporting norms:** FIELD DEFAULT. Quantitative results narrated in prose with reference to tables; qualitative findings organised thematically with illustrative quotations and interpretation. Null findings reported straightforwardly.
- **Discussion and conclusion:** FIELD DEFAULT. Explicit revisit of contributions, distinction of theoretical vs. practical implications, limitations, and future research directions expected. Conclusions concise, avoid new evidence/citations.
- **Hedging and epistemic caution:** FIELD DEFAULT. Moderate-to-high hedging in discussion/conclusion. Causal language qualified in observational designs. Qualitative papers hedge generalisability explicitly.

---

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Exceeding word limit.** Reviewers enforce the 7,000–10,000 ceiling strictly. Manuscripts over 10,000 words risk desk rejection or forced revision before review. Prioritise clarity and policy-relevant findings over comprehensive literature surveys.

2. **Abstract too long or structured.** The journal requests unstructured abstracts ≤100 words. Avoid background/methods/results/conclusion structure common in STEM; instead write a narrative summary of the contribution, gap, and key finding.

3. **Identifying author information in main text.** Double-blind review is enforced. Remove author names, institutional affiliations, and identifying details (e.g., "our previous research at X University") from the main text file. Cite your own work in third person if necessary.

4. **Missing conflict of interest or funding disclosures.** The journal requires explicit statements of competing interests (if none, state "The authors declare no competing interests") and all funding sources listed in acknowledgments. Omission causes revision requests.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

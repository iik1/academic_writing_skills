# Journal Writing Skill: African Journal of Employee Relations

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** HRM&EMP
- **Publisher:** UNISA Press (University of South Africa)
- **Scope:** Research in employment relations, HRM, labour relations, and organisational behaviour in African contexts; historically focused on South African and broader African workplace issues
- **Guidelines URL:** https://unisapressjournals.co.za/index.php/AJER/about/submissions
- **Field baseline:** hrm-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 8 (Mabaso 2026; Fitong Ketchiwou 2025; Bartlett et al. 2025; Le et al. 2025; Matjie et al. 2025; Adegbite et al. 2025; Rossouw & Strydom 2025; Mhangwani & Malindini 2025)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure

**Required Sections (in order):**
- Title
- Author(s) with ORCID and affiliation
- Abstract
- Keywords
- Introduction
- Literature review (standalone section in quantitative papers; integrated with introduction in some qualitative papers)
- Research objectives (explicitly labelled)
- Research design (explicitly labelled; combines methods and design)
- Ethical considerations (required subsection)
- Results/findings
- Discussion
- Limitations and strengths (explicit subsection)
- Recommendations (explicit subsection)
- Implications (explicit subsection, typically addressing practitioner/organizational/policy implications)
- Contribution (explicit subsection; may be subsumed in discussion)
- Conclusion
- Acknowledgements (brief, optional)
- References

*Delta from FIELD_SKILL:* More prescriptive section labeling than most ABS 4*/4 HRM journals. The field baseline permits section flexibility; this journal enforces explicit "Research Objectives," "Research Design," "Limitations," "Recommendations," "Implications," and "Contribution" sections or subsections. Qualitative papers may collapse literature review into introduction.

### Length

**Word count:** 5,000–10,000 words (including tables and figures in the count; not abstract or references).

*Delta from FIELD_SKILL:* Explicit low word limit at ABS 1 tier (field baseline does not specify; ABS 4*/4 journals typically allow 8,000–12,000 words). This constraint may require more concise literature review and streamlined results presentation.

### Abstract

**Format:** Unstructured paragraph (not subsection-labelled).

**Word limit:** 250 words maximum (strictly enforced).

**Required content elements:** background, purpose, design/methodology, findings, limitations, implications, contribution, and recommendations (all to be woven into prose).

**Prohibitions:** No footnotes, no citations, no references within abstract.

*Delta from FIELD_SKILL:* Field baseline does not specify abstract structure; this journal's requirement to include findings, limitations, implications, contribution, AND recommendations within 250 words is stricter than many ABS 4*/4 journals and requires dense, efficient prose in the abstract.

### Keywords

**Number:** 4–6 terms (some articles use up to 6; guidelines state 5–8 elsewhere, but 4–6 observed in practice).

**Capitalization:** Only proper nouns capitalized.

*Alignment with FIELD_SKILL:* Standard HRM practice; no significant delta.

### Tables and Figures

**Maximum:** 7 combined (tables and figures counted together).

**Placement:** Integrated within text at appropriate points; not appended at end.

*Delta from FIELD_SKILL:* Explicit maximum is a constraint. Field baseline does not specify a limit. This low ceiling (7 combined) is typical of lower-tier journals and may require consolidation of results presentation.

### References

**Maximum:** 60 sources.

**Citation style:** APA 7th edition.

**In-text format:** Author-date system (e.g., "Smith, 2012, p. 45" or "Smith et al., 2012").

**Reference list:** All sources listed in full, including DOIs where available. Use "et al." in text while listing all authors in reference list.

*Alignment with FIELD_SKILL:* APA citation style is standard in HRM field; no delta.

### Headings

Follows FIELD_SKILL norms (no explicit journal guideline provided; standard section headings observed in articles).

### Explicit Prohibitions and Requirements

- **Anonymization:** Double-blind peer review requires author identities masked. Do not include author names, affiliations, or self-identifying information in the manuscript body (except author and affiliation block on title page, which is stripped for review).
- **Formatting:** Use 1.5 spacing, 12pt Times New Roman font, A4 paper, normal margins.
- **File format:** Microsoft Word document only.
- **Italics vs. underlining:** Italics for standalone work titles and foreign language terms (provide English equivalents in parentheses); underlining reserved for URLs only.
- **Article titles:** Presented in "double inverted commas" in reference lists.
- **Ampersand use:** Use "and" in running text; "&" only in parenthetical citations and reference lists.
- **Footnotes:** Limited to essential content notes (not bibliographic). Numbered 1, 2, 3 in 10pt font. No endnotes.
- **Block quotations:** Quotations exceeding 40 words must be indented, without italics or quotation marks.
- **UK English spelling:** Explicitly required.
- **Single spaces:** Between words and sentences (not double-spacing after periods).

*Delta from FIELD_SKILL:* Field baseline does not specify these technical requirements. The joint emphasis on anonymization, strict formatting (1.5 spacing, Times New Roman, A4), and UK English is a journal-specific constraint.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Contribution Statement Prominence

**Finding:** ENFORCED. Journal guidelines require explicit "Contribution" subsection or statement (either embedded in discussion or as standalone subsection). Articles examined all include explicit statements of contribution, often phrased as "This study contributes to X literature by..." or "The contribution of this research is...".

**Delta from FIELD_SKILL:** Field baseline notes that explicit contribution statements are "the norm" at ABS 4*/4 level. At this ABS 1 journal, the requirement is similarly mandatory, but the emphasis is stronger and more formulaic (contribution is labelled separately, not optional).

### Practical Implications Emphasis

**Finding:** ENFORCED. All spot-checked articles include explicit "Implications" subsection addressing practitioner, organizational, or policy implications. Framing is consistently applied to practitioners (HR managers, education leaders, manufacturing supervisors) and policymakers.

**Delta from FIELD_SKILL:** Field baseline treats practical implications as "required" in HRM (reflecting applied orientation). This journal enforces this via explicit subsection labeling and consistent emphasis. Typical phrasing: "Implications for practice," "Recommendations for practitioners," "Policy implications."

### Limitations Subsection

**Finding:** ENFORCED. Journal requires explicit "Limitations and strengths" or "Limitations" subsection. All articles examined include this.

**Delta from FIELD_SKILL:** Field baseline notes that discussions "typically close with limitations and directions for future research as a distinct subsection." This journal formalizes this requirement in guidelines. The emphasis on "strengths" alongside limitations is less typical of ABS 4*/4 practice (which more often emphasize limitations alone).

### Recommendations Subsection

**Finding:** ENFORCED. Journal guidelines require explicit "Recommendations" subsection. All articles examined include action-oriented recommendations addressed to practitioners, organizations, or policymakers.

**Delta from FIELD_SKILL:** Field baseline does not mandate recommendations as a distinct subsection. This journal requires them, reflecting a lower-tier, applied-practice orientation. Recommendations are typically 1–2 paragraphs and are action-focused (e.g., "Organizations should implement...").

### Ethical Considerations Subsection

**Finding:** ENFORCED. Journal guidelines require explicit "Ethical considerations" subsection in methods section or as standalone section. All qualitative and mixed-methods papers examined include ethics approval statements (IRB/ethics committee approval codes and dates).

**Delta from FIELD_SKILL:** Field baseline notes ethical approval statements are "near-universal" in HRM but does not mandate a separate subsection. This journal requires explicit labeling of "Ethical considerations" as a distinct section heading, reflecting compliance with research integrity standards.

### Abstract Content Density

**Finding:** PRACTICE ONLY. Abstracts routinely combine 8 required elements (background, purpose, design, findings, limitations, implications, contribution, recommendations) into 250 words, resulting in dense, highly structured prose. Typical pattern: 1–2 sentences background, 1 sentence purpose, 1 sentence design, 2–3 sentences findings, 1 sentence limitation, 1 sentence implication/contribution/recommendation.

**Delta from FIELD_SKILL:** Field baseline does not specify abstract content requirements. The density and structural requirements here are higher than typical ABS 4* HRM abstracts, which often omit limitations, implications, and recommendations from the abstract. This journal's requirement creates tighter, more formula-driven abstracts.

### Methods Section Organization

**Finding:** ENFORCED (guidelines say "Research Design" section). Journal requires "Research Design" as explicit section heading (not "Methods"). Observed structure includes: research approach/paradigm (quantitative/qualitative/mixed), sample/participants, data collection method, instruments/measures (with reliability stats if quantitative), and analysis approach.

**Delta from FIELD_SKILL:** Field baseline describes "Methods sections" as standard in HRM. This journal renames it "Research Design" and may integrate some design rationale (paradigm, approach) with operational details, slightly collapsing conceptual and procedural content.

### Discussion-Conclusion Integration

**Finding:** PRACTICE ONLY. Some articles keep discussion and conclusion separate (as field baseline suggests); others combine them into a single "Discussion and conclusion" section. No clear enforcement, but integration is observed in approximately half of examined articles.

**Alignment with FIELD_SKILL:** Field baseline notes "practice varies" on this point; no delta.

### Results Section Prose Style

**Finding:** PRACTICE ONLY. Quantitative papers narrate results with reference to tables; qualitative papers organize findings thematically with illustrative quotations. Both follow field baseline norms. No journal-specific deviation detected.

**Alignment with FIELD_SKILL:** FIELD DEFAULT.

### Citation Density and Paraphrase

**Finding:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Citation density is highest in literature review and introduction, lowest in results, rising again in discussion. Paraphrase is strongly preferred over direct quotation. No deviation observed.

**Alignment with FIELD_SKILL:** FIELD DEFAULT.

### Voice and Register

**Finding:** PRACTICE ONLY. Articles employ formal, impersonal register with some use of first-person plural ("we examined," "our findings show") in introduction and methods, consistent with field baseline. No journal-specific deviation in tone or hedging observed.

**Alignment with FIELD_SKILL:** FIELD DEFAULT.

### Hedging and Caution

**Finding:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Hedging is moderate to high in discussion and conclusion sections; causal claims in observational designs are qualified appropriately. No deviation observed.

**Alignment with FIELD_SKILL:** FIELD DEFAULT.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Exceeding word count:** Submissions often exceed the 5,000–10,000-word ceiling. Tighten literature review and consider combining or omitting some results tables to stay within limit. The low ceiling relative to ABS 4* journals is a major constraint.

2. **Underdeveloped or missing subsections:** Journal requires explicit subsections for Limitations, Recommendations, Implications, and Contribution. Do not collapse these into discussion. Label them clearly.

3. **Abstract overloading:** Trying to fit background, purpose, design, findings, limitations, implications, contribution, AND recommendations into 250 words is difficult. Prioritize findings and implications; use telegraphic prose.

4. **Non-anonymized manuscripts:** Double-blind review requires anonymization. Remove author names, identifying affiliations, and self-citations that reveal identity. Check for author names in acknowledgements or running text.

5. **Exceeding 7 tables/figures:** Journal's combined maximum is low. Consolidate results into fewer, more information-dense tables rather than splitting across multiple tables.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

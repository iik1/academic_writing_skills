# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Managerial Psychology

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** PSYCH (WOP-OB)
- **Publisher:** Emerald
- **Scope:** Micro-level (employee characteristics, perceptions, attitudes, behaviors) and meso-level (team) phenomena in organizations; limited macro-level (HRM) work only when tied to individual/team-level psychological mechanisms. Explicitly excludes practitioner-only pieces and papers whose primary disciplinary home is strategy, sociology, or finance.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.emerald.com/jmp/pages/author-guidelines (Emerald standard guidelines template; also see JMP-specific editorial at https://www.emerald.com/jmp/article/35/6/453/235912/JMP-editorial-on-increasing-your-chances-of)
- **Field baseline:** psychology-wop-ob-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 5 (Mendy, Jain & Thomas 2025, "Artificial intelligence in the workplace..." Vol 40(5); issue 40(5) special-issue front matter, 8 further titles/abstracts reviewed; abstract snippets for a 2026 psychological-contract-breach study, a transformational leadership/work engagement study, a qualitative crisis-management study, and an emotional intelligence/CFA-SEM study, located via search-engine abstract extraction — full text paywalled)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Standard sections: Introduction, Literature Review, Theoretical Framework(s)/Hypotheses, Methods, Results/Findings, Discussion, Conclusion — same canonical order as FIELD_SKILL. JMP's own editorial stresses that quantitative theory sections must explain *why* a relationship is expected (mechanism), not just assert that hypotheses follow from a named theory ("theory name dropping" is explicitly flagged as a rejection driver); qualitative papers are expected to develop theory inductively with explicit attention to plausibility, credibility, and dependability.

### Length
Research/feature articles: 7,500 words. A newer "Brief Reports" manuscript type is capped at 4,000 words inclusive of the structured abstract, references, and all table/figure/appendix text (FIELD_SKILL does not specify a length; this is a JMP-specific hard cap).

### Abstract
Structured abstract required, placed at the start of the manuscript and not counted in the word limit, using Emerald's standard labeled sub-headings (Purpose; Design/methodology/approach; Findings; Research limitations/implications; Practical implications; Social implications; Originality/value — the latter three used only where applicable), maximum 250 words. JMP's editorial additionally asks authors to include a compact summary of practical implications on the abstract page itself.

### Citations
In-text citations and the reference list use Harvard style (author-date), not APA in-text format — this differs from the APA-style in-text citation FIELD_SKILL implies is standard for this field. Tables and figures, however, follow APA style conventions for internal formatting.

### Headings
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.

### Tables and Figures
Table/figure formatting follows APA style specifically (noted separately from the Harvard body-text citation style above).

### Explicit Prohibitions
Guidelines and the JMP editorial explicitly flag as unacceptable or strongly disfavored: single-source, single-time-point data collection; cross-sectional designs used to test mediation (allowed only with strong theoretical justification plus robustness checks); single-item measures; outdated Baron-and-Kenny mediation testing (Hayes or Cheung/Lau approaches expected instead); OLS regression on nested/multilevel data; simple bivariate statistics (correlations, ANOVA) without an experimental design; student-only samples as the sole sample; practitioner-oriented pieces with no theoretical contribution (redirected to outlets like Harvard Business Review); literature reviews that only identify gaps without theoretical development.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.

### Statistical Reporting
ENFORCED. Beyond FIELD_SKILL's general APA-reporting expectation, JMP's editorial names specific acceptable/unacceptable analytic choices: contemporary mediation testing (Hayes PROCESS or Cheung/Lau bootstrapping) is required over Baron-and-Kenny; hierarchical/multilevel modeling is required for nested data instead of OLS; confirmatory factor analysis is expected before structural models and must be conducted correctly (a named rejection reason when omitted or mishandled).

### Citation Density
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.

### Structural Deviations
PRACTICE ONLY / relaxation. As an ABS 3 outlet, single-study designs appear more readily accepted in practice than FIELD_SKILL's noted trend toward multi-study packages at the 4/4* level — several spot-checked abstracts describe single-study or single-review designs. Label this a RELAXATION rather than a new norm.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
ENFORCED. The journal explicitly asks for a "substantial (versus pro-forma)" practical implications section, written in plain language avoiding jargon, and suggests organizing implications by stakeholder group (e.g., managers, employees, organizations) — a more prescriptive practical-implications expectation than FIELD_SKILL's general statement that practical implications are near-universal in the field.

GUIDELINE ONLY. The editorial states replication studies and context-specific replications are acceptable if the contribution is clearly articulated (e.g., explaining why theory might operate differently in the new context) — this is stated guidance not independently confirmed across the spot-checked abstracts.

## Common Pitfalls
- Using APA in-text citation style instead of the required Harvard style (tables/figures are the APA exception, not the body text).
- Naming a theory to justify a hypothesis without explaining the causal mechanism ("theory name dropping"), which JMP's editorial calls out directly as a rejection driver.
- Testing mediation on cross-sectional, single-source data without strong theoretical justification and robustness checks, or using Baron-and-Kenny instead of Hayes/Cheung-Lau approaches.
- Writing a pro-forma practical implications paragraph rather than a substantial, jargon-free, stakeholder-organized section.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

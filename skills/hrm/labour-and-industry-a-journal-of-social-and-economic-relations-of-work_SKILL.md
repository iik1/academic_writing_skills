# Journal Writing Skill: Labour and Industry: A Journal of Social and Economic Relations of Work

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** HRM&EMP
- **Publisher:** Taylor & Francis (Informa UK Limited)
- **Scope:** Multi-disciplinary research on social, organizational, and institutional aspects of work and industrial relations; encourages contributions drawing on diverse theoretical perspectives and addressing contemporary challenges (pandemic recovery, climate, intersectionality, technological change)
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.tandfonline.com/journals/rlab20 (author guidelines behind paywall; full submission instructions not publicly accessible)
- **Field baseline:** hrm-field-writing-style

### Structure
IMRaD with standard back matter: Title, Abstract, Keywords, Introduction, Literature/Conceptual Grounding, Methods (if empirical), Results/Findings, Discussion, Conclusion, Acknowledgements, Ethics statements (for primary data collection). Symposia and special issue proposals are also accepted.

### Length
Abstract: approximately 100–200 words (Taylor & Francis standard; journal-specific limit not confirmed). Full manuscript length not specified in accessible sources; Taylor & Francis journals typically allow 8,000–12,000 words but this range should be verified with submission guidelines before finalizing.

### Abstract
Structured or unstructured (not confirmed in accessible materials); approximately 100–200 words; should cover research problem, aims, methods (if empirical), and key findings or arguments. Keywords required (number not specified; assume 4–6 per Taylor & Francis practice).

### Citations
Citation style: not confirmed in accessible guidelines. Likely APA or Harvard variant (common for Taylor & Francis social science journals) but should be verified. Citation density follows field norms: highest in introduction and literature sections, lower in results, rising again in discussion.

### Headings
Standard hierarchical heading structure (H1 = Introduction, Methods, etc.; H2 = subsections within sections). Bold or underlined formatting typical for main headings. Exact heading format and capitalization style should be verified in full guidelines.

### Tables and Figures
Tables and figures permitted; should be numbered and captioned. High-resolution artwork required. Exact placement conventions (end of manuscript vs. embedded) and formatting standards should be verified in full guidelines.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Double anonymization required (author identifying information removed during review). Ethical approval statements are near-universal requirement for primary data collection. No other explicit prohibitions noted in accessible sources.

### Scope and Problem Framing
**PRACTICE ONLY:** Labour and Industry emphasizes contemporary, real-world labor problems and emerging social science perspectives (climate change, intersectionality, technological disruption, pandemic recovery, good work) more explicitly than the generic HRM&EMP baseline. The journal's aims specifically encourage "challenging the conceptual boundaries of work and industrial relations" and contributing to "theory, practice and public policy dimensions." This reflects a stronger applied and critical-scholarship orientation than some higher-tier HRM outlets. Articles are expected to locate contributions both within academic debates and in concrete real-world contexts.

### Voice and Critical Register
**PRACTICE ONLY:** Labour and Industry tolerates—and appears to encourage—more explicitly critical and problem-focused scholarship than ABS 4* outlets. Evidence: the journal's strategic use of parenthetical emphasis ("matter[s]"), invocation of "sociological imagination" and "speaking truth to power," and problem-oriented framing of contemporary labor challenges. Register remains formally academic, but permits stronger normative positioning relative to labor issues. This aligns with the field baseline's tolerance for narrative and interpretive prose but skews toward advocacy and critique.

### Contributions Statement
**PRACTICE ONLY:** Contributions are expected to be explicit and typically frame benefits for multiple audiences: researchers (theoretical contribution), practitioners/managers/unions/policymakers (practical implications), and the field's critical project (advancing understanding of labor under contemporary conditions). This multivalent contribution framing is more pronounced in sampled articles than in some ABS 4 journals.

### Methods Flexibility
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm: both quantitative, qualitative, and conceptual/theoretical articles accepted. No delta.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Underestimating the journal's critical framing:** Treating Labour and Industry as a neutral empirical outlet. The journal explicitly values "challenging conceptual boundaries" and contributing to understanding of contemporary labor problems. Framing that is purely descriptive or avoids taking a position on labor governance, worker welfare, or equity issues may be seen as insufficiently engaged with the journal's mission.

2. **Insufficient grounding in real-world context:** The journal strongly encourages authors to "locate their contribution in the local context of a study, within the context of international debates." Purely theoretical or academically bounded arguments that do not connect to concrete workplaces, sectors, worker groups, or policy environments may be underdeveloped.

3. **Omitting practical/policy implications:** While the field baseline expects practical implications, Labour and Industry treats them as a non-negotiable component. Discussion sections must explicitly address implications for HR practitioners, managers, unions, workers, or policymakers, not merely theoretical extensions.

4. **Vague treatment of contemporary issues:** The journal's scope emphasizes pandemic recovery, climate change, technological disruption, intersectionality, and good work as emerging themes. Articles that do not engage with at least one such contemporary dimension, or that treat it tangentially, may be seen as missing a key opportunity for relevance.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

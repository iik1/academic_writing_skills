# Journal Writing Skill: Banks and Bank Systems

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** FINANCE
- **Publisher:** Business Perspectives (Sumy, Ukraine)
- **Scope:** Monetary policy, bank regulation, international financial organizations, international payment systems, investment banking, and risk management in banks; strong emphasis on applied/country-specific and emerging-market banking studies. Open access since January 2017; quarterly.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://businessperspectives.org/journals/banks-and-bank-systems (guidelines page itself returned HTTP 403 to direct fetch; details below assembled from indexed/cached guideline text and the publisher's general "for authors and editors" page)
- **Field baseline:** finance-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 15 (titles/abstracts and metadata only — full text behind 403 on publisher site). Sampled across issues 157, 172, 344, 389, 395, 414, 438, 449, 464, 472, 476, 503: "Determinants of banking sector development in developing and emerging economies," "The impact of digital banking channels and organizational culture on operational excellence in Jordanian banking," "Bank capital management in emerging and frontier markets: Bibliometric analysis," "Fraud prevention in the Indonesian banking sector using anti-fraud strategy," "Effect of electronic banking on financial performance of deposit money banks in Nigeria," "From innovation to stability: Evaluating the ripple influence of digital payment systems and capital adequacy ratio on a bank's Z-score," "An empirical analysis of the determinants of the U.S. banks' profitability," "The influence of financial inclusion on banks' performance and risk: new evidence from MENAP," and others on sustainable finance and bank performance (Bangladesh), oil price/inflation and bank deposits (Azerbaijan), operational risk in e-money, ESG disclosure in banking, digital banking/fintech and capital structure, Target2 imbalances and ECB activity, and social CRM/customer lifetime value in banking.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Required structure follows **AIMRaD** explicitly (Abstract, Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion) — the journal names this standard directly rather than leaving structure implicit as FIELD_SKILL does. Manuscript types accepted: original empirical papers, theoretical papers, and review papers.

### Length
4,000–6,000 words for full papers; 1,000–2,000 words for short communications. Word count excludes author information, title, abstract, keywords, references, and appendices. Supplementary materials capped at 5 pages. Title capped at 15 words.

### Abstract
Unstructured, single-paragraph, 150–250 words. Must not cite sources or include tables/figures within the abstract itself. Keywords: 5–10, single words or short word-groups (not sentences); if the study's object (including country/region) is not in the title, it must appear in the keyword list. JEL classification codes required (per AEA JEL system).

### Citations
Business Perspectives' own reference-list/citation-style guide, or APA style, is accepted. Reference list must be **alphabetical** and capped at **50 sources** — a hard numeric ceiling not present in FIELD_SKILL.

### Headings
Not otherwise specified beyond the AIMRaD requirement; numbered Arabic sections consistent with FIELD_SKILL norm appear to be followed in practice (titles reviewed show conventional Introduction → Literature/Methods → Results → Discussion/Conclusion flow).

### Tables and Figures
Permission required for reuse of any table, figure, scheme, or photograph from other authors' work — stated explicitly in guidelines (standard copyright practice, but journal calls it out directly).

### Explicit Prohibitions
None beyond standard originality/plagiarism screening (all submissions screened for originality; double-blind review). No explicit language/tone prohibitions found in accessible guideline text.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
FIELD_SKILL describes the canonical finance structure as Introduction → Data → Empirical Analysis/Results → Conclusion, with "Discussion" folded into "Conclusion" in most cases. This journal ENFORCES a named **AIMRaD** label (Abstract, Introduction, Methods, Results, **Discussion**) — closer to a general-science/applied-social-science template than the finance-specific norm. In practice this likely means a standalone "Discussion" section is expected rather than merged into "Conclusion," and "Methods" as a section label (vs. "Data" or "Empirical Strategy") is more explicit. GUIDELINE stated; article titles/abstracts sampled are consistent with an applied-empirical, methods-forward framing but full-text section headers could not be directly verified (403 on full text) — treat as GUIDELINE ONLY pending verification.

### Citation Density — RELAXATION
Hard 50-source cap on the reference list. FIELD_SKILL's ABS 4*/4 baseline assumes dense, extensive literature engagement with no stated ceiling; this ABS 1 journal caps citations, consistent with the shorter (4,000–6,000 word) paper length. Classify as RELAXATION, not a new norm — authors should trim literature review breadth relative to top-tier baseline expectations, not add sophistication.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Sampled titles/abstracts skew heavily toward applied, single-country or regional empirical studies (Nigeria, Jordan, Indonesia, Azerbaijan, Bangladesh, MENAP, U.S.) and bibliometric/literature-review-style papers, rather than the identification-heavy causal-inference papers typical of ABS 4*/4 finance outlets. This is PRACTICE ONLY (not stated in guidelines) but consistent enough across the sample to note: contributions framing can lean more descriptive/applied ("this study examines the effect of X on Y in [country]") than the aggressive novelty-claim framing FIELD_SKILL describes for top-tier journals. Treat FIELD_SKILL's contribution-framing guidance as an aspirational ceiling rather than the observed norm at this tier.

## Common Pitfalls
- Exceeding the 50-source reference cap by importing a top-tier-journal-style literature review — trim citations to fit the ABS 1 length and cap.
- Labeling sections "Data" and "Conclusion" only, omitting an explicit "Methods" and "Discussion" section — the journal's stated AIMRaD structure expects both labels present.
- Writing an abstract that references figures/tables or cites sources — explicitly disallowed here even though not all finance journals prohibit this.
- Omitting country/region from keywords when it isn't in the title — required per guidelines, easy to miss given how country-specific most published studies are.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

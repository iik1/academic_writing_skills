# Journal Writing Skill: British Journal of Industrial Relations

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 4
- **Field:** HRM&EMP
- **Publisher:** Wiley-Blackwell (for the London School of Economics)
- **Scope:** Multidisciplinary, international journal of work, labour, and
  employment relations, covering institutions, policies, and practices of
  industrial relations and their economic and social implications. Heavily
  weighted toward labour economics and institutional/quantitative analysis
  of unions, collective bargaining, wages, and workplace regulation,
  relative to the more psychological/managerial HRM work found elsewhere
  in the field.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/page/journal/14678543/homepage/forauthors.html
- **Field baseline:** hrm-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 10 abstracts read in full, plus 30+ additional
  titles/authors surveyed across issues from 2023-2026 (Vol. 61-64).
  Examples: "Unions, Wages and Hours" (Blanchflower & Bryson, 2025);
  "Building Power From the Bottom-Up..." (Riordan et al., 2026); "Wages
  and Working Conditions in Global Value Chains" (Fransen et al., 2026);
  "Works Councils and Apprenticeship Training" (Weis, Muehlemann & Pfeifer,
  2026); "Who Benefits From Right-to-Disconnect Legislation in Europe?"
  (Regier, 2026); "Digital Transformation of Retail Work" (Tilly & Carré,
  2026); "Minimum Wages and Human Capital Investment: A Meta-Regression
  Analysis" (Doucouliagos & Zigova, 2025); "The Labour Share and Corporate
  Financialization" (Guschanski & Onaran, 2025); "Power and bias in
  industrial relations research" (Doucouliagos et al., 2024); "Zero hours
  contracts and self-reported (mental) health in the UK" (Farina, Green &
  McVicar, 2024).

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No distinctive structural requirement beyond standard IMRaD-with-theory;
guidelines page does not specify a mandatory section list. Follows
FIELD_SKILL norm.

### Length
No explicit word/page limit stated on the public guidelines page (a
1,000-word "long abstract" cap applied only to a 2024 special-issue call
for papers, not to regular submissions — do not generalise this).

### Abstract
No structured-abstract requirement found; guidelines page is silent on
abstract word count. Spot-checked abstracts are consistently unstructured,
single-paragraph, and empirically framed (state the question/data, then
headline findings) — consistent with FIELD_SKILL norm for quantitative
papers.

### Citations
Harvard (author-date) reference style is explicitly required. This is a
FIELD DEFAULT for the field generally, but note it is an ENFORCED
house-style requirement here (some HRM&EMP journals use numbered or APA
variants).

### Headings
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm; no journal-specific heading convention found.

### Tables and Figures
Figures, supporting information, and appendices must be supplied as
separate files (not embedded in the main manuscript file). This is an
ENFORCED submission-mechanics rule, not a content/style delta.

### Explicit Prohibitions
None beyond standard Wiley/COPE research-integrity requirements: mandatory
disclosure of IRB/ethics approval, informed consent, and conflicts of
interest for primary data collection; data-sharing is expected.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm, but in practice the journal's content mix skews
much further toward econometric/labour-economics register than the
HRM&EMP field baseline implies. The great majority of sampled titles and
abstracts are quantitative studies using large administrative or survey
datasets (CPS, ESS, national administrative registers) with an economics-
style empirical framing (state research question, describe data/method,
report headline coefficients/effects in the abstract itself). Qualitative
and psychological/managerial framing (e.g., AMO, social exchange theory)
was rare to absent in the sampled abstracts, unlike the more even
qual/quant mix implied by FIELD_SKILL. PRACTICE ONLY — treat as a strong
disciplinary lean, not a stated rule: qualitative and mixed-methods papers
are still published (e.g., the algorithmic-management/union-leadership
study), so do not exclude qualitative framing, but default toward an
economics-style empirical register when uncertain.

### Statistical Reporting
PRACTICE ONLY, low-prevalence relative to a stated norm but consistent
across the quantitative sample: abstracts in this journal routinely report
actual effect sizes in the abstract itself (e.g., "15 log points," "5 log
points," specific percentage gaps), rather than only direction/
significance as FIELD_SKILL's general reporting norm describes for prose
narration. Consider including headline quantitative magnitudes in the
abstract, not just in tables, when writing for this journal.

### Citation Density
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.

### Structural Deviations
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
None beyond the disciplinary/topical lean noted above.

## Common Pitfalls
- Treating this as a generic HRM/psychology-style outlet: the realistic
  peer pool and editorial taste run closer to applied labour economics.
  Papers framed purely in organisational-behaviour terms with no
  economic/institutional angle are a weaker fit.
- Omitting quantitative headline results from the abstract: sampled
  abstracts consistently front-load specific effect sizes rather than
  deferring all numbers to tables.
- Submitting figures/appendices embedded in the main manuscript file
  instead of as separate files, per the explicit submission-mechanics
  requirement.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

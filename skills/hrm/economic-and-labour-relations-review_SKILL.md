# Journal Writing Skill: Economic and Labour Relations Review

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** HRM&EMP
- **Publisher:** Cambridge University Press
- **Scope:** Multidisciplinary journal bridging economics and labour relations, publishing empirical research (quantitative and qualitative), policy analysis, theoretical articles, and review essays on employment, labour markets, inequality, and workplace phenomena across international contexts.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-economic-and-labour-relations-review/information/author-instructions
- **Field baseline:** hrm-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 12 (from Volume 37, Issue 1, 2026; see Sources table)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Empirical articles, review essays, and policy analyses all follow recognisable sections (introduction/literature review, methods, results/findings, discussion, conclusion) consistent with FIELD_SKILL baseline. No structural deviations noted in guidelines or observed practice.

### Length
- **Maximum 8,000 words** (including abstract, tables, endnotes, author biographies combined)
- Research articles typically occupy full allocation
- Policy focus articles may extend toward upper limit due to detailed reform analysis

### Title
- **Maximum 12 words**
- Titles observed in sample articles follow this constraint strictly; some use numbered lists of policy options or research questions

### Abstract
- **Maximum 250 words**
- No structured format specified, but observed abstracts establish research gap, identify sample/data source, name methodology, and state main findings in 150–200 words
- Unstructured prose format (not tabular; no METHODS/RESULTS/CONCLUSIONS subheadings)

### Citation Style
- **Cambridge A format** (author-date parenthetical style)
- Consistent with standard academic convention

### Headings
No explicit guidance provided; observed practice follows standard hierarchy (1st level: major sections; 2nd: subsections within results/findings). No journal-specific house style observed.

### Tables and Figures
- Must follow Cambridge artwork guidelines (not detailed in accessible portions of guidelines)
- Practice observed: tables integrate regression coefficients with marginal effects; figures present trend data or illustrative relationships
- AI-generated images require declaration in caption or methods

### Other Formatting
- **Submission format:** Microsoft Word or LaTeX
- **Proofreading:** Mandatory before submission; papers not following guidelines rejected
- **Double-blind peer review:** Requires anonymous main document + separate title page with author details, competing interests declaration
- **AI declaration:** If AI tools used for text generation, data analysis, or image creation, declare in acknowledgments, methods, or figure captions with tool name, version, and usage description
- **Accessibility:** All materials must meet WCAG 2.1 Level AA; provide alt-text for figures, tables, multimedia

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Theoretical Framing and Field Scope
**PRACTICE ONLY / Significant field expansion:** While FIELD_SKILL describes HRM&EMP as anchored in psychological and managerial theories (AMO, social exchange, job demands-resources), ELRR welcomes heterodox and critical economic frameworks. Observed articles employ post-Keynesian economics (Financial Instability Hypothesis), institutional economics, political economy of work, and Bourdieusian sociology alongside conventional HR theory. The journal explicitly encourages "post-Keynesian (heterodox)" and "rights-, equality- or justice-based approaches" to economic policy. This represents a more pluralistic approach than the baseline suggests. When framing a paper for ELRR, heterodox or critical frameworks are not barriers; they may be viewed as intellectual assets if grounded in rigorous evidence.

### Policy Analysis Orientation
**PRACTICE ONLY / Emphasis variance:** FIELD_SKILL notes that practical implications are "required component." ELRR takes policy analysis further: some accepted article types (Policy Focus, 5,000–10,000 words) are structured as problem diagnosis and reform design. Papers on minimum wages, pension systems, and labour regulations present technical findings as stepping stones to actionable policy recommendations. When submitting policy-oriented work to ELRR, treat the policy design phase with methodological rigor equal to the empirical or analytical foundation; reviewers expect both technical soundness and practical feasibility assessment.

### Statistical Reporting Rigor
**PRACTICE ONLY / Trend toward econometric thoroughness:** FIELD_SKILL states that "degree of econometric sophistication demanded is lower and less uniform than in economics-field journals." ELRR practice deviates: observed quantitative articles employ instrumental variables, propensity score matching, fixed effects, non-linear models, and robustness testing across 8–15+ model specifications as standard. Authors justify methodological choices explicitly in the empirical strategy section. This suggests reviewers expect econometric rigour approaching (though not identical to) top-tier economics journals. When submitting quantitative work, include robustness checks and endogeneity/selection bias discussion; under-specify at your peril.

### Credibility Signalling and Transparency
**PRACTICE ONLY / Journal-specific emphasis:** FIELD_SKILL does not specify norms for methodological transparency and limitation acknowledgment beyond general hedging guidance. ELRR practice shows consistent, expansive treatment of limitations. Authors acknowledge missing data, unmeasured confounds, regional variation, and sample constraints as distinct paragraphs or subsections. The editorial commentary ("Considering Citation") emphasizes ethical scholarship, proper citation, and intellectual honesty as foundational to credibility. Transparency about limitations is not weakness; it strengthens trust.

### Literature Review Integration
**PRACTICE ONLY / High consistency:** FIELD_SKILL notes "practice varies on whether literature review is standalone or interwoven." ELRR articles consistently separate the literature review as a distinct section, culminating in explicit hypothesis statements, research questions, or theoretical framework presentation—even in qualitative papers. When writing for ELRR, structure a dedicated literature review section rather than integrating literature into the introduction.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Insufficient methodological justification in quantitative work:** Reviewers expect explicit rationale for choice of Probit vs. OLS, IV strategy, matching approach, or fixed effects specification. Omitting this signals incomplete methodological thinking.

2. **Underestimating policy analysis rigor:** If your paper is policy-oriented, treat the reform design or cost-benefit analysis with equal technical care as the empirical foundation. Hand-waving reform proposals weaken otherwise solid research.

3. **Conflating "heterodox" with "loose."** The journal welcomes post-Keynesian or institutional frames, but they must be operationalised with as much rigour as conventional economics. Theoretical novelty does not excuse sloppy methods.

4. **Failing to declare AI tool use:** Especially for text generation, data analysis visualisation, or image creation. Omission of such declarations may trigger desk rejection or retraction risk.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

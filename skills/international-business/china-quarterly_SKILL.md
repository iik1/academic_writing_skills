# Journal Writing Skill: China Quarterly

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** IB&AREA (International Business and Area Studies)
- **Publisher:** Cambridge University Press
- **Scope:** Peer-reviewed academic journal established 1960, covering contemporary China (including Taiwan, Hong Kong, Macau, Tibet) across interdisciplinary perspectives: economics, geography, history, international affairs, law, politics, anthropology, sociology, literature, and the arts.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/china-quarterly/information/instructions-contributors
- **Field baseline:** international-business-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 5 (2025-2026 publications; "The Resilience of Self-reliance in China," "Shouldering the Burden: The Communist Party's Deepening Penetration into Village China," and abstracts of 3 others)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No single mandatory structure is prescribed. Research articles should tell "something interesting and important about China" and engage substantively with China studies literature. Common sections include Introduction, Theory/Conceptual Background, Methodology (for empirical work), Findings/Results, Discussion, and Conclusion. Dual-language abstract (English and Chinese) and keywords required. Competing Interests declaration and Acknowledgements section required.

### Length
- **Research Articles:** Up to 9,000 words, all-inclusive (including abstract, footnotes, bibliography, tables, figures)
- **Research Reports:** Up to 4,500 words, all-inclusive
- **Abstracts:** 100–150 words (unstructured); must be "a straightforward overview of your paper that any China scholar can understand, regardless of their disciplinary background"
- **Keywords:** Five or six keywords

### Abstract
Must prioritize clarity and accessibility for an interdisciplinary audience. Avoid jargon, technical terminology, and discipline-specific shorthand. Non-specialists should be able to understand the research question, gap addressed, approach, and core contribution. Abstract must be provided in both English and Chinese versions.

### Citations
Footnote-based author-date format (not parenthetical). Format: surname and year in footnotes (Duckett 2010, 54) with full details in References list at end. Multiple works: Rozelle 1991, 1996. Consecutive identical references: use "Ibid." with page number if different. Within sentences: As Duckett (2010, 67) notes. Reference list: alphabetize by author surname; single-author entries precede multi-author entries; oldest publications first when same author/year. Use full author names; avoid replacing names with dashes.

### Headings
No specific rules stated in guidelines. Articles examined use hierarchical title-case headings (main sections, subsections) with numbered or unnumbered structure. Avoid overly narrow disciplinary framing in subheadings.

### Tables and Figures
Number with Arabic numerals; include headings and sources. Statistics must conform to Western norms (thousands, millions) not ten-thousand format. Supply camera-ready copy legible in grayscale. Provide alt-text descriptions for accessibility. All images must cite sources and obtain reproduction permissions.

### Language and Spelling
- Use Oxford English spelling with "z" throughout (organize, not organise), except "analyse" (not "analyze")
- Spell out numbers one through ten; use numerals for 11 and above
- Write "per cent" in running text, "%" in footnotes
- Dates: Day Month Year format (e.g., 2 March 2012; use ordinals: 11th, 20th)
- Page ranges: 324–26 but 324–336

### Romanization and Chinese Language
- Pinyin romanization without diacritical marks, except established names: Chiang Kai-shek, Sun Yat-sen, Taipei, Kuomintang, Peking University, Tsinghua University, and names of people living outside mainland China
- Provide Chinese characters for all Chinese phrases and proper nouns on first appearance in main text in parentheses: (pinyin characters)
- Keywords and abstract must be provided in both English and Chinese

### AI Tool Usage
Papers using generative AI must declare it: tool name, date, access method, and what content was generated.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Papers that are primarily policy briefs, purely descriptive (lacking substantive engagement with China studies literature), narrowly technical/methodological (inaccessible to broad interdisciplinary audiences), or use China merely as a single case study for disciplinary debates (rather than engaging substantive China-studies questions) are unlikely to succeed.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Citation Format: ENFORCED DELTA
FIELD_SKILL specifies "author-date parenthetical" citation style (e.g., Smith 2015, 45). China Quarterly uses **footnote-based author-date format** instead. Citations appear in footnotes with the same author-date information, with full bibliographic details in a References list. This is the primary enforced deviation from the field baseline.

### Accessibility Emphasis: PRACTICE ENFORCED
FIELD_SKILL describes the field expectation: "formal, scholarly register that remains accessible to a broad management audience." China Quarterly makes this **more explicit and stringent**: abstracts must explicitly avoid jargon and technical terminology; guidance states papers should explain technical methods so "non-specialists can follow the logic." The journal values "substantive findings rather than technical procedures." This is more demanding than the field baseline on clarity for non-specialists outside the disciplinary field.

### Bilingual Abstract and Keywords: ENFORCED
FIELD_SKILL does not mention bilingual outputs. China Quarterly **requires abstracts and keywords in both English and Chinese**. This is an enforced constraint specific to the journal's international audience and regional focus.

### Word Limits: ENFORCED (ABS tier relaxation)
FIELD_SKILL does not specify word limits (noting it belongs in journal-level skills). China Quarterly enforces strict word limits: 9,000 words for research articles (all-inclusive), 4,500 for research reports. This is tighter than typical ABS 4*/4 journals (which often allow 12,000-15,000 words) but acceptable for ABS 2 tier; label as **RELAXATION** relative to higher-tier field norms.

### Restricted Submission Types: ENFORCED PROHIBITION
FIELD_SKILL assumes empirical, conceptual, and review/agenda-setting papers are all acceptable. China Quarterly explicitly discourages policy briefs, purely descriptive work, and narrowly technical papers inaccessible to interdisciplinary readers. This is not a delta from FIELD_SKILL tone (which already expects engagement with broad literature) but a more explicit statement of scope limitations.

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Voice and person:** FIELD DEFAULT (third-person academic voice standard; "we" used for authors' own analytical claims; impersonal/passive acceptable for literature synthesis)
- **Paragraph and sentence style:** FIELD DEFAULT (medium to long sentences with qualified claims; topic-sentence-led paragraphs)
- **Argumentation and rhetorical moves:** FIELD DEFAULT (CARS logic in introduction; explicit contribution statements; respectful repositioning vs. refutation)
- **Methods reporting:** FIELD DEFAULT (procedural detail required; robustness checks expected; past tense for completed action)
- **Results and reporting norms:** FIELD DEFAULT (tables and prose both required; present tense for what results "show")
- **Discussion and conclusion structure:** FIELD DEFAULT (explicit loop back to gap and contributions; theoretical implications before practical ones)
- **Hedging and epistemic caution:** FIELD DEFAULT (moderate hedging standard; unhedged language reserved for describing literature gaps)

## Common Pitfalls Specific to China Quarterly

1. **Ignoring the accessibility requirement in abstracts:** Authors trained in disciplinary specialization often write abstracts that assume reader familiarity with field terminology. China Quarterly reviewers explicitly expect abstact language comprehensible to China scholars from any discipline. Rewrite abstracts to remove all field-specific jargon.

2. **Parenthetical citations instead of footnote format:** The most frequent formatting error for authors trained in standard ABS 4*/4 field conventions. Convert all parenthetical author-date cites to footnote format before submission.

3. **Omitting bilingual abstract or keywords:** Even if the journal system accepts English-only, peer reviewers expect both English and Chinese versions. Budget time for professional translation of abstract and keywords if not a native Chinese speaker.

4. **Exceeding word limits (9,000 or 4,500):** The all-inclusive count includes abstract, footnotes, and references. Authors often overlook footnotes in word counting. Use word-count tools that capture all text including notes and references.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: California Management Review

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN
- **Publisher:** Walter A. Haas School of Business, UC Berkeley (distributed by SAGE)
- **Scope:** Publishes original, research-based articles on management broadly defined, explicitly requiring practitioner relevance; interprets management to include any business-school subject matter or outside field applicable to management practice.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://cmr.berkeley.edu/resources/submit/ (primary); https://journals.sagepub.com/author-instructions/cmr (SAGE mirror, thin on detail)
- **Field baseline:** mgmt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 19 (title/abstract/summary level across Vol. 64 Issue 1, Vol. 64 Issue 2 special issue on Global Value Chains, Vol. 65 Issue 4 special issue on Strategic Agility, plus 1 full-text CMR Insights piece: "Want a Healthier Workforce? Take a Look at Your Pay Strategy")

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No fixed section template is mandated (unlike a rigid IMRaD). Manuscript types differ by length/purpose: In-Depth Article (research-based, full peer review), Spotlight Article (concise, practitioner-focused), Insight (short, online-only, open access, often opinion or applied-research), Quick Submit (author-uncertain length, editorial triage within 7 business days).

### Length
- In-Depth Article: 6,000–8,000 words
- Spotlight Article: 3,000–4,000 words
- Insight: 500–3,000 words
- Quick Submit: 500–8,000 words
- Word counts exclude charts, tables, figures, and endnotes. Double-spaced, 12-point font.

### Abstract
100-word summary of the manuscript's basic argument (much shorter than FIELD_SKILL's 150–250 word norm), plus a minimum of 3 keywords.

### Citations
Chicago Manual of Style, 18th Edition, **endnotes** (not footnotes, not author-date/APA in-text as in FIELD_SKILL). Journal citations with 3+ authors list only the first author + "et al." No ibid. or op. cit.; use shortened notes for repeat citations. DOIs/URLs required where applicable. Improper reference formatting is grounds for the submission not being processed.

### Headings
Maximum three descending heading levels; headings should be "descriptive but brief."

### Tables and Figures
Uploaded as separate high-resolution native files (not embedded in the manuscript document). Authors are responsible for obtaining copyright permissions for any reused figures.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- No author names anywhere in the manuscript, charts, figures, graphs, or filenames (blind review).
- No simultaneous submission to other outlets.
- No previously published material.
- No submission fee.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
Writing is explicitly practitioner-facing rather than scholar-facing: guidelines require content to be "as jargon-free as possible," with uncommon terminology and acronyms defined on first use and technical material pushed to notes/appendices. Every manuscript must include prescriptive, actionable advice for managers — a stronger and more explicit practitioner mandate than FIELD_SKILL's general "some managerial relevance" note. (ENFORCED — stated in guidelines and consistently visible across sampled articles and the Insights piece, which used numbered "Key Takeaways" framed as direct recommendations.)

### Citation Density
Because citations run as Chicago-style endnotes rather than dense in-text APA parentheticals, prose reads less citation-cluttered on the page even when evidentiary grounding is similar; argument flows narratively with citation markers deferred to endnotes rather than interrupting sentences. (ENFORCED / RELAXATION — a lighter in-text citation footprint than the FIELD_SKILL norm, consistent with the ABS-3 tier.)

### Structural Deviations
No hypotheses/propositions apparatus is expected as a default — sampled articles (case studies, frameworks, multi-firm qualitative syntheses) are organized around practical frameworks, numbered takeaways, or thematic sections rather than a Theory-and-Hypotheses or Theoretical-Background template. Shorter formats (Spotlight, Insight) compress or omit a formal literature/theory section entirely. (PRACTICE ONLY, reinforced by format-length HARD_CONSTRAINTS — this is a relaxation of FIELD_SKILL's structural templates appropriate to the ABS-3, practitioner-first positioning, not a new field norm.)

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
CMR Insights (500–3,000 words, open access, online-only) is a format with no FIELD_SKILL analogue: short, often single-author-voice pieces built around a small number of bolded "Key Takeaways" and a brief conclusion, closer to an opinion/applied-research hybrid than a standard journal article. (ENFORCED for that submission track only.)

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting in APA in-text citation style instead of the required Chicago endnote format — guidelines state improper references block processing.
- Writing a 150–250 word FIELD_SKILL-style abstract instead of the required ~100-word summary.
- Embedding tables/figures in the manuscript file rather than uploading them as separate native-format files.
- Treating the piece as a standard academic paper without an explicit practitioner takeaway — CMR reviewers expect prescriptive, manager-facing guidance, not just a theoretical contribution statement.
- Leaving author-identifying information in the manuscript text or filename, violating blind review.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

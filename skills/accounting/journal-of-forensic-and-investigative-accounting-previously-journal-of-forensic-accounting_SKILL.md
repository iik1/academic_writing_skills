# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Forensic and Investigative Accounting (previously Journal of Forensic Accounting)

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ACCOUNT
- **Publisher:** NACVA (National Association of Certified Valuators and Analysts)
- **Scope:** Publishes creative and innovative studies in forensic accounting, fraud detection and investigation, valuation, expert witness testimony, litigation services, and related topics. Covers both basic research and practice-oriented work.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.nacva.com/jfia (see links for "Guidelines for Manuscript Preparation" and "Editorial Policy and Style Information" PDFs)
- **Field baseline:** account-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 1 full article (Volume 16, Issue 1, 2024: "Understanding the Role of Memory in Interviews Undertaken by Auditors") — access to additional full-text articles limited due to paywalls; supplemented with article metadata and NACVA website information

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm with one notable exception: articles frequently include pedagogical and applied implementation sections not typically required in ABS 4*/4 empirical papers. Standard sections include: Abstract, Introduction (Roman numeral I), hypothesis development or theoretical background, Methods/Research Design, Results/Findings, Discussion and Conclusion. For practice-oriented or teaching-focused papers (common in this journal), authors may include dedicated sections on "Learning Objectives," "Implementation Guidance," and "Exercises" or "Case Studies." These sections reflect the journal's emphasis on translating research to forensic accounting practice and education.

### Length
Guidelines explicitly limit manuscripts to a reasonable length to be published electronically. No specific word count available from accessible sources, but observed article (34 pp. with extensive appendices and student handouts) suggests tolerance for applied materials and pedagogical content alongside core research.

### Abstract
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Structured abstract expected, conveying research question, method/approach, and key findings/contributions in concise form. Separate keywords field typical.

### Citations
Author-date format (Chicago Manual of Style or similar). In-text: "(Author Year)" or "Author (Year)". Extensive footnotes (superscript numbers) for elaboration, methodological notes, and citations to supporting standards (e.g., PCAOB standards, AICPA guidance). Observed article uses both parenthetical citations and numbered footnotes extensively, particularly when citing practitioner standards and regulatory guidance.

### Headings
Roman numeral system for primary sections (I, II, III, IV, V, VI, etc.). Level 1 headings: bold, capitalized (e.g., "I. INTRODUCTION", "II. MOTIVATION"). Level 2 headings: italicized, not capitalized, integrated as subsection labels within text (e.g., "Short-Term and Long-Term Memory" as a subsection within Section III). This matches FIELD_SKILL norm for AAA-style journals but is explicitly practiced in observed articles.

### Tables and Figures
- Arabic numbering (Table 1, Figure 1, etc.)
- Descriptive titles
- Tables appear to accommodate detailed content (validation results, exercise timing estimates)
- Student handout figures and exercises integrated into main text
- Follows FIELD_SKILL norm for table formatting and placement

### Formatting
Times New Roman font observed in published article. Double-spaced manuscript format likely expected (standard for accounting journals). Standard margins and sequential page numbering. Matches FIELD_SKILL defaults.

### Explicit Prohibitions & Requirements
- **Originality:** Unpublished manuscripts not under consideration elsewhere, consistent with FIELD_SKILL norm
- **No explicit ORCID or AI disclosure requirements found** in accessible sources, unlike newer AAA journals (Journal of Forensic Accounting Research). JFIA submission guidelines not fully accessible; authors should check NACVA website directly for current requirements.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Scope and Methodological Openness
JFIA explicitly welcomes applied, practice-oriented, and teaching-focused research in addition to traditional empirical and theoretical work. The journal publishes pedagogical articles (classroom exercises, case studies, teaching modules) alongside traditional research papers. This is a **PRACTICE EMPHASIS** not typical of the ABS 4*/4 baseline. Where FIELD_SKILL emphasizes quantitative/archival/experimental archival research as the dominant tradition, JFIA signals equal interest in translating forensic accounting knowledge to practice and education contexts. Articles that integrate practitioner context, regulatory guidance (PCAOB, AICPA standards), and professional application are particularly valued.

### Integration of Pedagogical and Implementation Sections
Articles with a teaching or professional development focus may include dedicated sections on "Learning Objectives," "Implementation Guidance," and detailed "Exercises" or "Case Studies" with instructor notes and validation data. This is a **JOURNAL-SPECIFIC PRACTICE** absent from FIELD_SKILL. The observed article (Fico & Walsh 2024) includes three structured memory exercises with student handouts, instructor notes, time estimates, and classroom validation results (pre/post survey data). This integration of applied materials is not a violation of standards but rather a encouraged feature when the research contribution is educational or practice-focused.

### Extensive Use of Regulatory and Professional Standards Citation
Articles frequently cite and interpret PCAOB audit standards, AICPA guidelines, forensic accounting professional standards, and valuation methodologies. The observed article cites PCAOB AS 1105, AS 2401, AS 1301 extensively and integrates these into the motivation and example sections. This **PRACTICE EMPHASIS** is consistent with the journal's practice-oriented scope but more prominent than in FIELD_SKILL baseline, which expects standards citations but does not foreground them as heavily.

### Footnote Density
The observed article uses extensive superscript numbered footnotes (in addition to parenthetical in-text citations) for methodological elaboration, additional citations, definitions, and cross-references. Footnote count exceeds typical FIELD_SKILL practice (which reserves footnotes for exceptional notes). This is a **GUIDELINE PRACTICE** that appears journal-specific, likely enabling dense technical explanation without overloading the main text.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Underestimating the journal's emphasis on practical and pedagogical value** — Unlike many ABS 4*/4 journals, which prioritize novel empirical findings or theory, JFIA values research that translates to forensic accounting practice, education, or professional development. Purely academic contributions without clear application to fraud detection, valuation, expert testimony, or auditor training may be misaligned. Explicitly frame contributions for practitioners and educators, not just academics.

2. **Insufficient integration of regulatory and professional standards** — The journal's scope is deeply grounded in PCAOB, AICPA, and forensic accounting standards. Authors should demonstrate how their research relates to or informs professional practice under these standards. Simply citing standards in passing is insufficient; integrate them into the research question, hypothesis development, or practice implications.

3. **Neglecting to address diverse forensic accounting domains** — While the observed article focuses on interviewing and auditing, the journal encompasses valuation, damages estimation, fraud investigation, expert witness work, and litigation services. Research should be positioned relative to at least one of these forensic accounting domains and should explain its relevance to practitioners in that domain.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

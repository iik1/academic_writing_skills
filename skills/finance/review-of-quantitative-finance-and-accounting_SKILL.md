# Journal Writing Skill: Review of Quantitative Finance and Accounting

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** FINANCE
- **Publisher:** Springer Nature (Springer Science+Business Media), hybrid open-access model
- **Scope:** Publishes theoretical and methodological work in finance and accounting with empirical applications, as well as purely theoretical/methodological papers with potential future applications. Covers corporate finance, financial economics, econometrics, monetary economics, and accounting/earnings topics. Editor: Cheng-Few Lee.

### Structure
No structure guidance found beyond standard Springer conventions (Springer's generic manuscript template applies: title page, abstract, keywords, main text, references, tables/figures). No journal-specific mandated section list was found on accessible pages.

### Length
No explicit word or page limit found in accessible guideline text or search snippets. Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (no strict cap; determined by content).

### Abstract
**ENFORCED (stated in guidelines):** Abstract must be 150–250 words, single paragraph, with no undefined abbreviations or unspecified references. This is a specific, narrower band than FIELD_SKILL's general "150–250 words" description — here it is a hard, stated requirement rather than an observed typical range.

**ENFORCED:** 4–6 keywords required for indexing (FIELD_SKILL does not specify a keyword count).

### Citations
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (author-year format observed consistently in spot-checked articles).

### Headings
No journal-specific heading convention found; follows FIELD_SKILL norm (Arabic numeral sectioning observed, e.g., standard numbered sections in spot-checked papers).

### Tables and Figures
No journal-specific rule found beyond standard Springer formatting requirements (submitted as editable files, not embedded images, per generic Springer template — could not confirm this is stated specifically on this journal's page, so treat as GUIDELINE ONLY / typical Springer default).

### Explicit Prohibitions
**ENFORCED:** Double-blind review — author-identifying information must NOT appear on the manuscript or a separate title page; it is entered instead into the submission system's interface fields. This is a deviation from journals using single-blind or non-blind review, and authors must actively scrub self-identifying citations/acknowledgments from the manuscript body.

**ENFORCED:** No simultaneous submission to more than one journal; work must be original and not previously published (partial or full), unless it is an expansion of prior work.

**ENFORCED:** A "Statements and Declarations" section (financial/non-financial competing interests, covering the 3 years prior to beginning the work, plus any reasonably-perceived-as-relevant interests outside that window) is required; submissions missing it are returned as incomplete.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
Double-blind peer review is the clearest journal-specific delta: authors must prepare a fully anonymized manuscript (no author names, self-citations phrased identifiably, acknowledgments, or funding statements that reveal identity in the body text) and supply identifying/declaration information only via the submission portal. FIELD_SKILL does not address blinding since it is a submission-mechanics issue outside its scope — flagged here because it materially affects how a manuscript must be prepared before submission (e.g., "we thank our colleagues at [University]" must be removed or generalized).

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
PRACTICE ONLY / low-prevalence: A notable share of spot-checked papers are single- or narrow-topic empirical pieces with compact 1–2 JEL codes and short (2–6 word) keyword lists rather than the longer keyword sets sometimes seen at 4*/4 journals — consistent with the journal's ABS-3 tier but not a stated rule (RELAXATION in degree of granularity, not a distinct norm). Sample also includes occasional review/bibliometric-style papers (e.g., "Machine learning in accounting and finance research: a literature review") and a purely theoretical/policy paper ("Fiscal and macroprudential policies in a monetary union") alongside standard empirical designs — consistent with the stated scope's explicit inclusion of literature reviews and purely theoretical/methodological contributions, which is broader than the typical ABS 4*/4 empirical-paper emphasis in FIELD_SKILL.

## Common Pitfalls
- Leaving identifying information (author names, institution-revealing acknowledgments, self-citations phrased as "as we showed in [Author, Year]") in the manuscript body, which violates the double-blind requirement.
- Omitting the "Statements and Declarations" section — guidelines state this causes the submission to be returned as incomplete.
- Writing an abstract outside the 150–250 word band or leaving abbreviations undefined within it.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

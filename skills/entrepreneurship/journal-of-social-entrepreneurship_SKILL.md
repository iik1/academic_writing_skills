# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Social Entrepreneurship

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ENT-SBM
- **Publisher:** Taylor & Francis
- **ISSN:** 1942-0676
- **Scope:** Peer-reviewed research on social entrepreneurship, social innovation, social enterprises, and their intersection with economic development, sustainability, and system change. Publishes both empirical and conceptual work.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

**Note:** Journal-specific author guidelines were not directly accessible via public sources. The following reflect Taylor & Francis standard journal practices and inferences from published articles and journal metadata.

### Structure
Follows standard academic empirical structure: Abstract, Keywords, Introduction, Literature Review/Theoretical Framework, Methods (for empirical work), Results/Findings, Discussion, Limitations and Future Research, Conclusion, References. Conceptual and review articles substitute thematic organization for the empirical spine but retain Introduction–Contributions–Discussion skeleton (consistent with FIELD_SKILL).

### Length
**Submission word limit:** 6,000–8,000 words total (including text, references, tables, figures, and appendices). This is a stated constraint from journal metadata and differs from the FIELD_SKILL baseline, which does not specify limits.

### Abstract
**Word limit:** Appears to follow Taylor & Francis standard of 150–300 words. Abstract should summarize research questions, methods (for empirical work), and key findings. Format appears unstructured (not explicitly divided into objectives/methods/results/conclusions sections).

### Keywords
**Number:** 5–10 keywords required; used for indexing and discoverability.

### Citations
**Style:** Harvard or APA referencing (inferred from published articles; no explicit journal mandate found). DOIs required for all journal articles.

### Headings
Follows Taylor & Francis style: first-level headings (Introduction, Literature Review, etc.) in bold; second-level in bold italics; third-level in italics. Initial capitals for proper nouns.

### Tables and Figures
Figures submitted separately at minimum 300 dpi (JPG or PNG). Tables numbered sequentially with title above and borders. Both should be referenced in text.

### Explicit Prohibitions
None identified beyond standard academic integrity expectations (plagiarism, fabrication, duplicate submission).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Word Limit (RELAXATION)
The journal enforces a **6,000–8,000-word hard cap** including all components. This is significantly tighter than many ABS 4/4* entrepreneurship journals, which often accommodate 10,000–12,000 words. Authors must be more concise in presenting theory, methods, and discussion.

### Theory Section Brevity
Empirical articles appear to compress the theory and hypothesis development section relative to FIELD_SKILL norms. Introduction and theory sections are denser (more citations per page) but shorter in absolute page count to accommodate the word limit. This means **higher citation density in a smaller space**, requiring more economical argument construction.

### Conceptual/Review Articles
Conceptual articles in this journal lean toward **applied frameworks and actionable insights** rather than purely theoretical development. Examples include frameworks for measuring social impact, integrating AI into social enterprises, and multi-level system-change models. This reflects the field's strong application orientation but is more pronounced at ABS 2 than at ABS 4/4* where abstract theorizing is equally valued.

### Discussion and Implications
The Discussion section explicitly addresses **practical and policy implications** in addition to theoretical contributions. Social entrepreneurship's applied focus (supporting founders, policymakers, NGO leaders) is more foregrounded than in the FIELD_SKILL baseline, which treats practical implications as a subsection. Here, practical implications are given equal weight to theoretical contributions, sometimes dominating the discussion narrative.

### Citation Density in Methods
Citation density is **lower in the methods section** than FIELD_SKILL suggests. Methodological justifications are brief, with references to standard techniques (e.g., regression, survey design) often omitted unless the method is novel or contested. This may reflect space constraints rather than a stylistic choice.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- **Voice and person:** First-person plural ("we test," "we find") throughout. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Sentence and paragraph style:** Medium to long sentences with embedded citations; dense paragraphs in introduction/theory, more discursive in discussion. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Argumentation (CARS pattern):** Establishment of phenomenon importance, narrowing to research gap, explicit research questions, enumerated contributions. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Methods conventions:** Procedural transparency, detailed sampling/screening, construct measurement with scale items and Cronbach's alpha, common-method bias discussion, robustness checks. FIELD DEFAULT (though compressed due to word limit).
- **Results reporting:** Hypothesis-by-hypothesis reporting; correlation and regression tables with coefficients, standard errors, p-values; effect sizes increasingly expected. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Hedging and epistemic caution:** Moderate hedging concentrated in limitations; results reported in direct declarative language; forward-looking hedges in limitations ("future research should..."). FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Engagement with literature:** Paraphrase dominates; direct quotation rare except for foundational definitions. Self-citation and canonical theorists recur heavily. FIELD DEFAULT.

## Common Pitfalls for This Journal

1. **Exceeding word limit:** The 6,000–8,000-word cap is strict and is a hard constraint. Manuscripts that exceed this limit may be desk-rejected or require substantial revision. Plan your theory, methods, and discussion carefully; abbreviate where possible without sacrificing clarity.

2. **Insufficient practical framing:** Theoretical contributions alone are not enough. The Discussion must explicitly address "so what?" for practitioners, policymakers, or social enterprise leaders. Implications for practice carry equal weight to theoretical implications.

3. **Weak or absent systems-level framing:** Recent journal trends (2024–2025) show strong emphasis on multi-level, system-change, and sustainability perspectives. Purely individual-level or organization-level studies without connection to broader social or environmental systems may be viewed as incremental. Position your work within a macro context.

4. **Buried conceptual frameworks in empirical work:** If your empirical study is informed by or develops a conceptual framework, make that framework explicit and visual (via figure). The journal favors transparent, reusable frameworks over implicitly embedded theory.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

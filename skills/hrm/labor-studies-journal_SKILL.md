# Journal Writing Skill: Labor Studies Journal

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** HRM&EMP
- **Publisher:** SAGE Publications (on behalf of United Association for Labor Education)
- **Scope:** Multidisciplinary journal covering work, workers, labor organizations, and labor studies and worker education in the US and internationally. The official journal of the United Association for Labor Education (UALE), with readership including union educators, labor activists, scholars, and practitioners.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/author-instructions/lsj
- **Field baseline:** hrm-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 8-10 article titles from Volume 50 (2024-2025) via abstract/metadata; full-text paywalled, so spot-check limited to guidelines compliance via stated editorial criteria

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows IMRaD macro-structure (introduction, literature/theory, methods, results/findings, discussion, conclusion) expected in empirical HRM work, but emphasis is placed on clarity and accessibility over technical sophistication. Acknowledgements, ethics statements, and author contribution statements are standard. Double-blind peer review requires anonymous submission (authors' names appear only on cover page).

### Length
**Maximum 30 pages total**, including abstract, tables, and figures. Double-spaced throughout. This is a tighter constraint than the FIELD_SKILL baseline, which does not specify a universal page limit.

### Abstract
Not explicitly specified in publicly available guidelines. Standard practice inferred to be present but word-count/structure requirements not detailed; assume brief structured abstract following SAGE norms.

### Citations
**Chicago Manual of Style, 16th edition, author-date system**. In-text citations use author, year of publication, and page number where appropriate. References cited list at end with year following author name, titles in headline style. This is a fixed departure from many HRM journals which use APA; Chicago author-date is non-negotiable.

### Headings
Not explicitly specified; FIELD_SKILL norms apply (section headings organized around claims/arguments).

### Tables and Figures
**Numbered separately** (tables and figures have independent numbering). **Placed separately at the end of the manuscript** on separate sheets, in the order they appear in the text. This is stricter than some HRM journals' practice of inline placement or mixed numbering.

### Language and Tone — Accessibility Requirement
**Explicit and enforced editorial criterion:** Manuscripts must be "free from disciplinary jargon and methodological detail only of interest to people with advanced specialized training." This is a significant departure from FIELD_SKILL's tolerance for formal register and theoretical density. LSJ reviewers explicitly assess "practitioner accessibility" as a key evaluation dimension. Methodological sophistication (quantitative or qualitative) is explicitly de-emphasized relative to clarity and novelty. The journal prioritizes "novelty of topic, clarity of presentation, and accessibility" over theoretical complexity and methodological rigor—an inversion of the FIELD_SKILL baseline.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Authors' names or identifying marks cannot appear anywhere except on the cover page (double-blind requirement).
- New evidence or citations should not be introduced in the conclusion.
- No disciplinary jargon; avoid methodological detail accessible only to specialists.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice, Register, and Accessibility
**RELAXATION:** LSJ explicitly prioritizes accessibility to a non-specialist audience (union educators, labor activists, practitioners, scholars from multiple disciplines) over the formal-but-dense register acceptable in ABS 4*/4 HRM journals. Simplification of language and removal of disciplinary jargon is not a stylistic preference but an editorial requirement. Quantitative methods in particular should be explained for a general reader, not assumed to be familiar. This is a significant departure from FIELD_SKILL, which tolerates greater theoretical density and jargon.

### Statistical Reporting and Methodological Presentation
**RELAXATION:** Econometric sophistication and detailed methodological exposition are not treated as requirements or quality signals. The journal welcomes diverse methods (quantitative, qualitative, mixed) but demands that all methods be explained accessibly. Robustness checks and endogeneity discussion, while present in some articles, are not universal expectations as they are in FIELD_SKILL (which derives from more technically rigorous ABS 4* journals). Methodological detail that would appear in ABS 4 journals should be simplified or moved to appendices for LSJ.

### Citation Density and Theoretical Framing
**PARTIAL DEPARTURE:** While FIELD_SKILL baseline specifies high citation density and dense theoretical engagement, LSJ welcomes but does not require heavy theoretical apparatus. The journal is receptive to papers that prioritize novelty of data or context (under-researched sectors, occupations, countries, worker groups) over original theoretical contribution. Citation of seminal frameworks (e.g., AMO theory, social exchange, job demands-resources) is still expected, but papers grounded primarily in empirical novelty rather than theoretical innovation are explicitly valued.

### Structural Requirement: Emphasis on Practical/Policy Implications
**ENFORCED:** LSJ explicitly evaluates "contribution to the literature" based on addressing "an important topic of interest to LSJ's readers" and bringing "new data, methods, or perspective." Practical implications for unions, worker organizations, labor educators, or policymakers are not optional but central to the journal's scope. Discussion sections should explicitly connect findings to these practitioner audiences, not only to scholarly theory.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- **Argumentation and Rhetorical Moves:** Follows FIELD_SKILL CARS-like intro structure and explicit contribution statements. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Methods Transparency and Ethical Approval:** FIELD DEFAULT for quantitative (detailed sample, measures, strategy) and qualitative (sampling, access, analytic approach, reflexivity). Ethics statements required as in FIELD_SKILL.
- **Results and Reporting Norms:** FIELD DEFAULT. Quantitative results narrated in prose with tables; qualitative findings thematically organized with illustrative quotations.
- **Discussion and Conclusion Structure:** FIELD DEFAULT. Revisits contributions, distinguishes theory from practice, includes limitations and future research.
- **Hedging and Epistemic Caution:** FIELD DEFAULT. Moderate to high hedging; causal language qualified in observational designs.

---

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Over-theorization:** Submitting a paper heavy on theoretical apparatus and light on empirical novelty or practitioner relevance. LSJ reviewers will downweight theoretical elegance if the paper does not address a topic of concern to labor educators, activists, or worker organizations.

2. **Unexplained methodology:** Using quantitative or qualitative methods without explaining them for a general audience. Methods sections and results must be accessible to readers without advanced training in econometrics, statistical modeling, or specialized qualitative traditions. This is non-negotiable.

3. **Exceeding 30 pages:** Hard limit. Papers must be tightly written; lengthy literature reviews or methodological exposition should be trimmed or moved to appendices.

4. **Non-Chicago citation style:** Chicago author-date is required. APA, Harvard, or other styles will lead to desk rejection or revision requests.

5. **Violating anonymity:** Any identifying information (author names, institution, funding acknowledgments that reveal identity) outside the cover page compromises double-blind review and may trigger desk rejection.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

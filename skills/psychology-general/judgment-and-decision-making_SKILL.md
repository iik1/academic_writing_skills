# Journal Writing Skill: Judgment and Decision Making

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** PSYCH (GENERAL)
- **Publisher:** Society for Judgment and Decision Making (published via Cambridge University Press since 2022; formerly self-hosted at journal.sjdm.org / jbaron.org)
- **Scope:** Normative, descriptive, and prescriptive analysis of human judgment and decision making, spanning cognitive psychology, experimental economics, and experimental philosophy; primarily experimental work but also accepts theory, review, and non-experimental contributions.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/judgment-and-decision-making/information/author-instructions/preparing-your-materials ; legacy style guide https://jbaron.org/journal/style.htm ; legacy submission page https://jbaron.org/journal/submit.htm
- **Field baseline:** psychology-general-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 2 full front-matter/structure reviews (jdm211119 — SVO Slider Measure test-retest reliability; jdm210706 — Kruger 1999 above/below-average replication), plus review of journal-wide style-guide documentation covering many more articles' worth of house-style rules

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Single manuscript document, in order: title page (authors, affiliations, ORCID, corresponding-author contact) → Abstract → Keywords (up to 10) → main text with tables/figures embedded in-line → Acknowledgements → Competing Interests Statement → Data Availability Statement → Financial Support Statement → Supplementary Materials → References. Main text follows Introduction / Method / Results / Discussion / (Conclusion), consistent with FIELD_SKILL IMRaD. Appendices are used for descriptive/robustness detail that would clutter Method or Results (e.g., "Appendix A: Descriptive analysis of X," "Appendix B: Attrition in our sample"). Article types: Empirical Article, Review Article, Theory Article, Registered Report, Commentary (invited only).

### Length
No article length restrictions ("JDM does not have article length restrictions"); contact the Editorial Office if length is a concern. Abstract capped at 250 words.

### Abstract
Single paragraph, max 250 words, covering problem, method, results, and conclusions.

### Citations
APA author-date style. In text: "and" for two authors, "et al." for three or more; "&" only inside parenthetical citations and the reference list. No mid-sentence citations — group citations at the end of the sentence/paragraph rather than interrupting a clause. References alphabetical by author, full initials, italicized volume numbers, page numbers required for all sources, journal titles given in full.

### Headings
Use the word processor's built-in heading/Styles menu for section headings rather than manual bold/font formatting; do not use manual centering or position controls anywhere in the manuscript.

### Tables and Figures
Placed within the text near first reference, not collected at the end or in appendices. Labeled consecutively (Figure 1, Table 1, ...) with captions that explain content. Figures/tables must not duplicate results already narrated in prose. Sideways tables must not exceed 6.5 inches in width. Graphics submitted as separate vector files (EPS preferred; PDF/EMF acceptable) or PNG/GIF for photos/screenshots; no images of mathematical expressions — use LaTeX notation instead.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Terminology substitutions required by house style: "almost significant" (not "marginally significant"); "denominator neglect" or "proportion dominance" (not "ratio bias"); "external validity" (not "ecological validity"); "adjust for" covariates in regression (not "control for").
- Statistics reported in parentheses, not set off by commas.
- British punctuation convention for quotations (punctuation mark falls outside closing quotation marks).
- Do not mention IRB/ethics approval unless specifically required.
- Avoid excessive procedural/methods detail in the main Method section — move it to an appendix.
- No manual page-position formatting, no Word equation-editor output (post-2007), no hidden/complex formatting codes.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
Journal house style explicitly prefers active voice over passive construction and short sentences with plain vocabulary, going further than FIELD_SKILL's general "plain prose" preference by naming specific banned/preferred terms (see Explicit Prohibitions) and by preferring "subjects" over "participants" (participants acceptable only if used consistently). ENFORCED (stated in style guide; consistent with plain, direct register observed in spot-checked abstracts).

### Structural Deviations
Explicit appendix convention for supplementary descriptive/robustness material (e.g., attrition analyses, categorical-construct breakdowns) that FIELD_SKILL does not call out — this journal treats appendices as a routine offload valve for procedural or robustness detail rather than an occasional device. PRACTICE ONLY, but reinforced by the explicit guideline to keep Method sections lean and move excess detail to appendices — ENFORCED overall.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Extensive footnote use for methodological transparency and disclosure of analytic decisions was observed in the Kruger replication spot-check, going beyond FIELD_SKILL's general transparency expectation into a house convention of footnoting rather than prose-narrating secondary analytic choices. PRACTICE ONLY (single-article observation; not stated in guidelines) — treat as a RELAXATION-compatible option, not a requirement, consistent with this journal's ABS 3 tier.

## Common Pitfalls
- Using "marginally significant," "ratio bias," "ecological validity," or "control for" (in regression) — this journal's style guide requires specific alternate terminology.
- Citing sources mid-sentence instead of grouping citations at the end of the sentence or paragraph.
- Collecting tables/figures at the end of the manuscript instead of embedding them near first reference in text.
- Manually formatting headings or centering text instead of using the word processor's Styles/heading menu — flagged explicitly in submission requirements as unacceptable.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

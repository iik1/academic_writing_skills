# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Property Investment and Finance

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** FINANCE
- **Publisher:** Emerald Group Publishing Limited (also distributed via ScienceDirect/Elsevier)
- **Scope:** International forum for property valuation and investment, property management, decision-making in all property markets (commercial, residential, REITs, real estate indices, alternatives)
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.emeraldgrouppublishing.com/journal/jpif (full guidelines behind firewall; Emerald standard practices apply)
- **Field baseline:** finance-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 7 (2024–2025: Coën & Guardiola on Brexit monetary policy; Ahiadu et al. on Australian investor behavior; Gamage & Perera on DCF valuation; Hoesli & Shmygel on discount rates; Maioli & Livingstone on blockchain; climate risk article; tokenization article)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines & Observed Practice)

### Structure
Follows standard FIELD_SKILL structure (Introduction, Methods/Data, Results, Conclusion, References). All observed articles use numbered sections (1. Introduction, 2. Data and Methods, etc.). Some articles include Practice Briefings or Real Estate Insights as distinct editorial categories (shorter, practitioner-focused pieces without formal Methods sections).

### Length
No explicit word limit found in publicly accessible guidelines. Observed articles range from ~6,000–10,000 words (pp. 370–453 in 6–8 page journal format), notably shorter than ABS 4* benchmarks (which often run 10,000–15,000+ words). This is a RELAXATION of field norms.

### Abstract
Structured abstract (Emerald standard):
- **Maximum 250 words** (observed in guidelines; adhered to in all articles)
- **Five mandatory fields:** Purpose, Design/methodology/approach, Findings, Originality/value, plus one optional (Research limitations, Practical implications, or Social implications)
- Single paragraph; structured subheadings within the paragraph (not separate lines)
- No keywords or JEL codes within abstract body; these appear as separate metadata fields on publication page

### Citations
Author-year parenthetical format (e.g., "Coën & Guardiola (2025)" or "(Coën & Guardiola, 2025)"). Observed in all articles. Matches FIELD_SKILL norm; no delta.

### Headings
Numbered Arabic numerals (1, 2, 3; 2.1, 2.2 for subsections). Observed consistently. Matches FIELD_SKILL norm.

### Tables and Figures
Tables include detailed footnotes defining variables, sample construction, and statistical methods (e.g., clustering of standard errors). Figures are integrated in-text or placed after references. Matches FIELD_SKILL norm.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- AI-generated images are not permitted in submissions (stated in recent guidelines)
- Copy-editing with generative AI is permissible
- Papers on mathematical/computing development for Automated Valuation Models (AVMs) are not accepted unless Real Estate-focused

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Methodological Diversity
This journal accepts broader methodological range than ABS 4* norms: Delphi studies (two-round expert panels), secondary literature reviews with case analysis, structural VAR models, and partial least squares structural equation modeling all appear prominently. FIELD_SKILL emphasizes reduced-form causal identification; JPIF is more hospitable to mixed-method and qualitative-validation approaches. CLASSIFICATION: PRACTICE ONLY (not enforced in guidelines; reflects lower tier flexibility).

### Practical Implications Emphasis
A recurring finding: all observed articles close with explicit **Practical implications** sections (either in abstract or as dedicated subsections in text), addressing specific implications for valuers, investors, and practitioners. This is more prominent and systematic than ABS 4* journals, where applications are mentioned but not foregrounded as a named section. CLASSIFICATION: ENFORCED (appears in abstract structure template and in all sampled articles).

### Article Length and Density
Observed articles are shorter (pp. 370–453 = 6–8 page format) than ABS 4* baseline (10+ pages typical). This implies lower technical density and potentially less extensive robustness treatment. CLASSIFICATION: RELAXATION (acceptable deviation at lower tier).

### Editorial Categories
The journal publishes two distinct article types:
1. **Research articles** — standard structure (Introduction, Data, Methods, Results, Conclusion)
2. **Practice Briefings** and **Real Estate Insights** — shorter, less formal; may omit Methods section entirely, focusing on practitioner guidance. CLASSIFICATION: PRACTICE ONLY (editorial convention, not applicable to standard research submissions).

### Hedging and Confidence Calibration
Observed language in results sections: "the study demonstrates," "findings reveal," "analysis shows" (active, strong declaratives matching FIELD_SKILL). However, practical implications sections use more tentative framing ("practitioners may consider," "could improve," "suggests potential for"). This split between empirical claims (strong) and application claims (measured) is more pronounced than in ABS 4* writing. CLASSIFICATION: PRACTICE ONLY (reflects nature of practitioner-facing content).

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Voice and person:** First-person plural ("we find," "we examine") in multi-authored work; FIELD DEFAULT
- **Active voice dominance:** Strong active constructions; no deviation; FIELD DEFAULT
- **Sentence and paragraph density:** Medium to long sentences, dense paragraphs; FIELD DEFAULT
- **Introduction rhetorical structure:** Significance, gap, research question, method preview, findings preview, contributions, literature positioning, roadmap — all observed; FIELD DEFAULT
- **Contributions framing:** Papers state contributions assertively ("we are first to," "novel contribution"); FIELD DEFAULT
- **Results table design and reporting:** Multiple specifications in adjacent columns, statistical significance notation (* p<0.10, ** p<0.05, *** p<0.01); FIELD DEFAULT
- **Economic significance reporting:** Coefficients translated into economically meaningful terms (basis points, percentage of mean); FIELD DEFAULT
- **Conclusion structure and length:** Restates finding, states implications, acknowledges limitations, suggests future directions; 1–4 paragraphs; FIELD DEFAULT

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Underdeveloped practical implications.** Reviewers in this field expect explicit guidance for practitioners (valuers, investors, fund managers). Ending with only academic implications (e.g., "future research should examine…") without addressing "what this means for practice" reads as incomplete.

2. **Insufficient introduction exposition.** The journal emphasizes author guidance requiring "full exposition in the introduction and conclusions on why the paper is important." Sparse motivation or delayed significance framing is flagged in desk reviews.

3. **Weak or vague findings in the abstract.** Editors expect quantitative specificity even in structured abstracts. Vague findings ("a positive effect") without reported magnitudes (e.g., "discount rates are 89 basis points lower") reduce desk-review likelihood.

4. **Inappropriate AVM methodology submissions.** Papers focusing on mathematical/algorithmic development of Automated Valuation Models are desk-rejected unless the application is explicitly real estate investment-focused (not general computer science or valuation technology).

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

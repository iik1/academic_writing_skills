# Journal Writing Skill: African Journal of Accounting, Auditing and Finance

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ACCOUNT
- **Publisher:** Inderscience Enterprises Ltd
- **Scope:** The journal publishes quantitative, empirical, and case-based research on accounting, auditing, and finance topics in African contexts and with relevance to developing economies. Focus areas include IFRS adoption, capital structure, dividend policy, internal audit effectiveness, financial accessibility, tax compliance, and corporate governance in Sub-Saharan Africa.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.inderscience.com/jhome.php?jcode=ajaaf (author guidelines accessible via submission system)
- **Field baseline:** account-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 articles across volumes 8-9 (2024-2025): "Capital structure and solvency of manufacturing firms: evidence from Ghana"; "Ethical values and employee fraud in Ghanaian banking sector during COVID-19"; "Determinants of the West African economic and monetary union's banks going-public decision"; "Impact of financial accessibility on small and medium-size enterprises' success"; "IFRS adoption and performance in Sub-Saharan Africa"; "Managers' perception of dividend policy in Nigerian firms"

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Standard manuscript: Title, Abstract, Keywords, Body (numbered sections), References, and optional Appendix. Typical empirical papers follow Introduction → Hypothesis Development / Theory → Methods → Results → Discussion/Conclusion structure. Standalone Robustness sections are common but may be embedded within Results. Tables and figures placed after references or at end of manuscript.

### Length
No explicit overall word limit stated in public guidelines. Abstracts: approximately 100 words maximum 150. Keywords: approximately 10-15 words or phrases. Biographical notes per author: approximately 100 words maximum 150.

### Abstract
Structured summary. Must state research question or motivation, method/data source, key findings, and contribution. Single paragraph format typical. Separate keywords field required.

### Citations
Harvard (name and date) short reference system throughout text. In-text citations as "(Author Year)" or "Author (Year)" depending on grammatical role. Full alphabetical reference list at end of article. Footnotes avoided but succinct numbered notes may follow references.

### Headings
Section numbering varies by article (some use Roman numerals, others Arabic numerals; not specified in journal guidelines). Author may follow either style. Title case or sentence case headings both observed.

### Tables and Figures
Placed after references or at end. Tables include panel labels and variable definitions in notes. Statistical choices explicitly reported (e.g., standard-error clustering).

### Explicit Prohibitions
Articles must not have been previously published or be under consideration elsewhere. Conference papers acceptable only if completely rewritten with copyright clearance; similarity index with original must not exceed 50%. Briefs and research notes not published; only original papers, review papers, technical reports, and case studies accepted.

### Language
All papers must be written in UK English.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Citation Density and Practice
RELAXATION: Citation density in introduction and hypothesis development sections is lower than ABS 4* baseline (15-40 citations per section) and more targeted. The field-wide norm assumes elite-tier archival data and extensive prior work; AJAAF papers often address emerging topics in African accounting where prior literature is thinner or less developed. A 10-15 work introduction is acceptable and not viewed negatively.

### Scope and Generality
RELAXATION: Generalizability bounds and limitations are stated explicitly (following FIELD_SKILL norm) but with greater acknowledgment that many papers are single-country or few-country studies (Ghana, Nigeria, Mauritius). Cross-national comparisons and pan-African samples are less common at ABS 1 level than at ABS 4*. Limitation paragraphs emphasize geographic and sample specificity without apology.

### Methods Presentation
ENFORCED: Multivariate regression analysis predominates; reported with regression equations, coefficient estimates, t-statistics or p-values, and sample size. Structural equation modeling (SEM) is also standard for latent variable studies (e.g., fraud diamond/pentagon theories). Robustness checks are expected and reported (alternative variable definitions, subsamples, alternative estimation approaches). No deviation from field norm in procedural detail or identification strategy discussion.

### Voice and Register
FIELD DEFAULT: High formality and active voice throughout, matching ABS 4* baseline. Use of "we" (even in single-author papers) is standard. Clinical, precise register in empirical work. No colloquialisms or rhetorical questions.

### Hedging and Confidence Calibration
FIELD DEFAULT: Medium hedging level, calibrated to identification strength. Well-identified quasi-experimental results warrant direct claims ("we find," "we conclude"). Mechanistic interpretations use softened language ("suggest," "consistent with," "appear to"). Null results and limitations explicitly acknowledged.

## Common Pitfalls
1. Overstating causal claims without identification: AJAAF reviewers apply the same standard as ABS 4* journals—avoid causal language unless exogenous variation is credibly established. Many ABS 1 papers are archival/observational; correlation language is appropriate unless design (natural experiment, instrumental variable) justifies stronger claims.

2. Under-reporting robustness checks: Expected norm is three to five robustness tests (alternative variable definitions, alternative samples, placebo tests). Omitting these or relegating them entirely to appendix raises reviewer concern about cherry-picking.

3. Insufficient acknowledgment of geographic/sample limitations: Unlike broader FIELD_SKILL papers (which may study multiple countries or time periods), many AJAAF submissions are single-country. Do not leave bounds vague ("results may apply to developing economies"); name the specific geographic, institutional, or temporal constraints explicitly.

4. Inappropriate formality in case studies / qualitative subsections: While empirical papers match ABS 4* tone, case studies and qualitative components (e.g., fieldwork-based sections) may adopt slightly more narrative prose. However, high formality and precision are still expected; colloquialisms and first-person anecdote are not appropriate.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

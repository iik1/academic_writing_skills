# Journal Writing Skill: Annual Review of Psychology

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 4
- **Field:** PSYCH (GENERAL)
- **Publisher:** Annual Reviews Inc. (nonprofit)
- **Scope:** Invitation-only synthetic review journal covering the full breadth
  of psychology (biological, cognitive, developmental, social, personality,
  clinical/counseling, community, environmental psychology). Not an outlet
  for empirical reports.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.annualreviews.org/pb-assets/authors%20assets/authorhandbook-harvard.pdf
  (generic Annual Reviews "Instructions for the Preparation of Manuscripts,"
  Harvard/name-year citation family, covers Psychology among ~24 journals);
  https://www.annualreviews.org/page/authors/general-information (portal,
  403-blocked in this environment but referenced by the handbook)
- **Field baseline:** psychology-general-field-writing-style

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict. Sourced from the generic
> Annual Reviews author handbook (Harvard-style family, which explicitly
> lists Psychology). Journal-specific supplements (exact page/word length,
> submission portal) are gated behind an invitation-only Production Editor
> relationship and were not accessible in this environment.

### Structure
Not IMRaD. Author-defined, numbered first-level headings (e.g., "1.
INTRODUCTION," "2. ...," closing with a "CONCLUSIONS" or equivalent
section) organized around the review's own logical structure, not a fixed
template. No Method/Results/Discussion sections — this is a synthesis
review, not a report of new data. The journal also publishes one
"prefatory," explicitly autobiographical chapter per volume by a senior
scholar, structurally and tonally distinct from the rest of the volume
(see Voice and Register below). This is a fixed AR journal-wide format,
not something to emulate for a standard submission unless invited to write
one.

### Length
No fixed word count found in guidelines; length is individually assigned
per author in the invitation letter and enforced via an online "Article
Length Estimator" tool. Authors must adhere to the length specified in
their own invitation rather than a published journal-wide cap.

### Abstract
150 words maximum (Harvard-style AR family default; Psychology is not
listed among the exceptions that get more).

### Citations
Harvard (author–year) in-text citations, not numbered. Ampersand for two
authors ("White & Gray 2004"), "et al." for three or more in text. No
comma between name and year. Literature Cited list alphabetical by author
surname, then year; last name first with initials, no periods between
initials, double-spaced, first line not indented. Include article/chapter
titles (Psychology is not among the exceptions that omit them). Same-year
same-author disambiguated with a/b/c suffixes.

### Headings
Clearly formatted, numbered or unnumbered at AR's discretion but observed
articles consistently use numbered first-level sections (1., 2., 3., ...).
No fixed subsection depth is mandated by the handbook.

### Tables and Figures
Up to AR page-width (6.33 in), each figure submitted as a separate file
with its own caption (not embedded in text), figures numbered
consecutively as referenced. Color is free and encouraged. Tables use ND
(no data), NA (not applicable), NT (not tested) rather than blank cells or
dashes.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- No footnotes used merely to house references.
- Direct quotation should be minimized; quoting/paraphrasing more than
  ~250 words from one source requires copyright permission.
- Do not pad the bibliography with marginally relevant citations "for
  completeness," but also do not under-cite competitors/newcomers.
- Postprint/published-version text may not be posted anywhere on the open
  internet (preprints are permitted with required acknowledgment language).
- "Anonymous" is never used for unattributable sources; cite by sponsoring
  body or title instead.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Voice and Register
Sharper delta than FIELD_SKILL's generic "review journals use thematic
headings" note: standard AR-Psychology reviews open with a real-world or
disciplinary vignette (Stokoe et al. open with a newspaper headline
controversy) before narrowing to the review's organizing question — a more
narrative, essayistic on-ramp than the terse funnel opening typical of
empirical papers in this field. The annual prefatory/autobiographical
chapter is a distinct, non-generalizable format: first-person (singular or
plural), reminiscent, personal-history-driven, permits informal asides and
humor, and is explicitly framed as a career retrospective rather than a
literature synthesis. This format has no equivalent for ordinary invited
contributors and should not be imitated outside a prefatory-chapter
assignment.

### Citation Density
FIELD_SKILL already notes higher density for review journals generally;
AR-Psychology articles observed here also embed short in-margin glossary
definitions of key technical terms (e.g., "Discursive psychology: the
study of how people deploy and orient to psychological matters...") tied
to first use in text — a lightweight, recurring definitional apparatus
beyond ordinary jargon-defined-at-first-use practice. This corresponds to
the handbook's optional "Terms and Definitions" list element (up to 20
terms, 20 words each), which this journal's authors use more visibly than
a simple end-of-article glossary would suggest.

### Structural Deviations
Numbered section scheme with an explicit forecasting paragraph at the end
of the Introduction (e.g., "We start our discussion of this work in
Section 2 by... In Section 3... In Section 6... we conclude") — a
signposting convention stronger than FIELD_SKILL's general note about
transitions; it is a full roadmap paragraph, not just topic sentences.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Optional end-of-article elements (Summary Points, Future Issues,
Terms and Definitions, Reference Annotations, Related Resources, Sidebar)
are genuinely optional and inconsistently used — of the full-text articles
inspected, neither included a Summary Points or Future Issues list, so
these should not be treated as a default requirement despite being
prominent in the handbook's Article Components section.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- Statistical reporting norms: FIELD DEFAULT (not applicable; this is a
  synthesis-review journal, no primary statistical reporting).
- Hedging and epistemic caution: FIELD DEFAULT.
- Argumentation/CARS funnel at the paragraph level (once past the opening
  vignette): FIELD DEFAULT.
- Discussion/conclusion norms (synthesis-closing rather than
  General-Discussion-per-study, consistent with FIELD_SKILL's review-journal
  variant): FIELD DEFAULT.

## Common Pitfalls
- Treating this as an empirical-report venue: submissions must be
  synthesis reviews with author-defined structure, not IMRaD reports —
  there is no Method/Results section to fill in.
- Assuming numbered in-text citations: this journal uses Harvard
  (author-year) style, not the numbered-citation family used by some other
  Annual Reviews titles (e.g., Genetics, Immunology).
- Attempting to imitate the prefatory chapter's autobiographical, informal
  voice in a standard invited review; that format is reserved for a single
  specially commissioned chapter per volume.
- Assuming a fixed word limit; length is individually negotiated per the
  invitation letter, not a published journal-wide cap (only the 150-word
  abstract cap is fixed).

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Natural Resources Journal

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ECON (nominal classification; journal is a student-edited law review, not an economics research journal)
- **Publisher:** University of New Mexico School of Law
- **Scope:** "An international, interdisciplinary forum devoted to the study of natural and environmental resources," emphasizing law and policy. Explicitly excludes purely scientific articles and press releases.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://lawschool.unm.edu/nrj/article-submission.html (core policy); detailed formatting in a separate "Manuscript Preparation for Accepted Authors" document referenced but not publicly retrievable in this pass; submission portal at https://nrjsubmissions.scholasticahq.com/for-authors

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No fixed section template is published. The journal sorts content into distinct genres per issue: **Articles**, **Notes** (shorter, often student-authored), **Symposium** pieces, **Lecture Transcripts**, and **Book Reviews**. A prospective author choosing to write "for NRJ" is choosing among these genres, not just writing a standard empirical/theory paper. Purely scientific articles (i.e., papers without a legal/policy dimension) are explicitly excluded from consideration.

### Length
Not publicly specified in the accessible guidelines; a separate "Manuscript Preparation for Accepted Authors" document (referenced but not retrieved) likely governs final formatting for accepted pieces. No page or word limit could be confirmed.

### Abstract
No article in the sampled issues (12 articles/notes across 3 issues) carries a published abstract. This is a structural absence, not a length restriction — the journal simply does not display abstracts in its table of contents or repository metadata.

### Citations
**Bluebook citation format is mandatory** ("All submissions must employ Bluebook citation standards"). This is a hard, confirmed override of FIELD_SKILL's author-year parenthetical norm: NRJ uses footnote-based legal citation (Bluebook), not economics-style in-text author-year citation.

### Headings
Not specified beyond genre labels (Article/Note/Symposium/Lecture Transcript/Book Review) used at the table-of-contents level.

### Tables and Figures
Not specified in accessible guidelines.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Purely scientific articles are not accepted.
- Press releases are not accepted.
- Material must be original and previously unpublished, in whole or in part.
- Authors must secure permissions for copyrighted material and trademarks used in the piece.

### Voice and Register
Titles and genre mix (legal-brief-style titles such as "Backdoor NEPA Proceduralization: Less Environmental Substance Begets More Environmental Procedure," "A Horse Named 'Stream Depletion Theory'") indicate a law-review register: argumentative, policy-oriented, often rhetorical or wordplay-driven titling, rather than the plain declarative "X affects Y: evidence from Z" titling convention typical of ABS 4*/4 economics. This is a STRUCTURAL/GENRE delta rather than a sentence-level voice delta that could be confirmed from full text (PRACTICE ONLY, based on title conventions across all 12 sampled pieces).

### Citation Density
Cannot assess prose-level density without full text, but the mandated citation FORMAT (Bluebook, footnote-based) itself signals a fundamentally different citation architecture than FIELD_SKILL's author-year-in-text norm. ENFORCED (stated in guidelines; consistent with law-review genre).

### Structural Deviations
The journal is organized by genre (Article/Note/Symposium/Lecture Transcript/Book Review), not by the empirical/theory paper types FIELD_SKILL describes. Notes are typically shorter, single-authored, and often written by law students on narrower legal questions (e.g., "Climate Change Will Make the Final Call in New Mexico's Groundwater Appropriation"). Authors targeting NRJ should decide which genre fits their contribution; FIELD_SKILL's Introduction/Data/Empirical Strategy/Results/Conclusion template does not apply to Notes, Symposium pieces, Lecture Transcripts, or Book Reviews. ENFORCED (genre structure is consistent across all 3 sampled issues).

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Multi-authored pieces (2-3 authors) coexist with sole-authored pieces at roughly even frequency in the sample, consistent with a law-review norm rather than the near-universal multi-author, "we"-voiced convention of top economics journals. Author-count mix is PRACTICE ONLY / FIELD DEFAULT-compatible (cannot confirm voice/pronoun usage without full text).

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting a standard quantitative economics paper (Data/Empirical Strategy/Results structure) risks a poor fit: NRJ explicitly excludes "purely scientific articles" and expects a legal/policy dimension.
- Using author-year citations instead of Bluebook-style footnotes will not conform to the journal's stated citation requirement.
- Omitting an abstract is not a defect for this journal — abstracts are not part of its published format.
- Titles that read as plain empirical-results statements (the FIELD_SKILL norm) are atypical here; NRJ favors more argumentative, sometimes wordplay-driven titles reflecting law-review conventions.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

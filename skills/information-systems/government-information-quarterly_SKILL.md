# Journal Writing Skill: Government Information Quarterly

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** INFO MAN
- **Publisher:** Elsevier
- **Scope:** Examines the intersection of policy, information technology,
  government, and the public — government information flows, digital/AI-enabled
  government services, IT's impact on the governed/governing relationship, and
  information policy in relation to democratic practice. Welcomes three
  submission types: rigor-and-relevance original research, significant
  theoretical contributions, and significant practical contributions.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/government-information-quarterly/publish/guide-for-authors
  (403 on direct fetch; content recovered via search-engine snippets and a
  full open manuscript file with cover letter/highlights/response-to-reviewers)
- **Field baseline:** information-management-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 1 full manuscript with review history (Chatterjee,
  Khorana & Kizgin, "Harnessing the Potential of Artificial Intelligence to
  Foster Citizens' Satisfaction: An Empirical Study on India," GIQ-D-20-00353,
  2021) plus abstracts/scope summaries for roughly a dozen further GIQ articles
  spanning 2020-2025 (AI governance, digital government inclusion, generative
  AI adoption, AI in administrative communication, 50th-anniversary reflection
  piece) retrieved via search snippets.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Full Length Article is the dominant type; journal also accepts Review
Article, Short Communication, Case Report, Short Survey, and others. No
single mandated section-heading scheme is stated, but the observed norm
(confirmed in the spot-checked manuscript) is: Introduction (ending with
explicit numbered research questions and a one-paragraph roadmap of the
paper's section-by-section structure) → Literature review → Theoretical
background / conceptual model / hypotheses development → Research
methodology → Results → Discussion (with labeled subsections: Theoretical
contributions; Practical implications; Limitations and future research
directions) → Conclusion → References → Appendix.

### Length
Typical manuscripts run 25-30 pages, double-spaced.

### Abstract
Unstructured, maximum 250 words. Must stand alone (article abstracts are
often displayed separately from the full text) — avoid citations unless
essential (and if used, cite author/year), and define any non-standard
abbreviation at first use.

### Keywords
1 to 7 keywords required for indexing.

### Highlights
Required at submission: 3 to 5 bullet points, each a maximum of 85
characters including spaces, capturing the novel results/methods of the
study.

### Citations
No distinct citation-style mandate surfaced beyond standard Elsevier
author-year referencing (visible throughout the spot-checked manuscript,
e.g., "Zhang et al., 2016").

### Headings
No fixed heading-numbering scheme stated; numbered decimal subheadings
(e.g., "3.2.1") are common practice, as seen in the spot-checked manuscript.

### Tables and Figures
No journal-specific rule beyond standard Elsevier requirements surfaced.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Standard Elsevier publishing-ethics policy applies. Submission requires an
Author Statement/declarations page (competing interests, funding source,
inclusive-language attestation) as a separate manuscript component —
confirmed present in the spot-checked submission package.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Structural Deviations
GIQ manuscripts place a **literature review section** (labeled distinctly
from "theoretical background") ahead of, and separate from, the theory/
hypotheses section, giving three front-matter blocks (Introduction →
Literature Review → Theoretical Background & Hypotheses) rather than
FIELD_SKILL's typical two (Introduction → Theoretical Background). PRACTICE
ONLY, observed in the spot-checked manuscript and consistent with the
"literature review lacks fundamental information" guest-editor comment in
the review history, which suggests reviewers actively police this as a
distinct, substantial section rather than a brief lead-in.

The Discussion section is explicitly subdivided into labeled subsections —
Theoretical contributions, Practical implications, Limitations and future
research directions — as separate numbered subsections rather than looser
prose treatment. This is a tighter, more formal version of the FIELD_SKILL
norm (which already expects a contributions/implications treatment but
doesn't mandate numbered subheadings for it). RELAXATION-compatible framing
does not apply here since this is a tightening, consistent with the
ABS-3-may-relax-but-not-redefine rule — treat as PRACTICE ONLY tightening
rather than a new norm.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Highlights (3-5 bullets, ≤85 characters each) are a hard submission
requirement not mentioned in FIELD_SKILL, which does not specify this
format at all. ENFORCED (stated in guide-for-authors and present in the
spot-checked submission package).

The journal explicitly welcomes practitioner/policy-oriented and
theory-only contributions as first-class submission types alongside
standard empirical research (per guideline text: "original research
papers," "significant theoretical contributions," "significant practical
contributions"), a somewhat broader tent than FIELD_SKILL's assumption of
empirical/design-science/conceptual as the three genres. GUIDELINE ONLY —
this is stated in scope material but not something a spot-check of
research articles can further confirm or disconfirm.

## Common Pitfalls
- Treating the literature review as a brief lead-in rather than a
  standalone section with its own citation base and an explicit
  synthesis table of prior studies and identified gaps — guest-editor
  review comments in the spot-checked manuscript specifically flagged an
  underdeveloped literature review and requested a gap-summary table.
- Writing hypotheses as combined/jumbled paragraphs instead of one
  self-contained argument-and-citation paragraph per hypothesis — flagged
  explicitly in reviewer comments in the spot-checked manuscript.
- Citing a theory via a secondary source instead of its original
  citation (e.g., citing public value theory via a later paper rather than
  Moore, 1995) — flagged explicitly by a reviewer in the spot-checked
  manuscript as a citation-accuracy concern.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

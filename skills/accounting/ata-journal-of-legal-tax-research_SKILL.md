# Journal Writing Skill: ATA Journal of Legal Tax Research

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ACCOUNT
- **Publisher:** American Accounting Association
- **Scope:** Double-blind peer-reviewed journal publishing creative and innovative studies employing legal research methodologies to identify, describe, and illuminate important current tax issues; propose improvements in U.S., state, local, or foreign tax systems; and critically analyze tax law changes and policy.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://aaahq.org/Research/Journals/The-ATA-Journal-of-Legal-Tax-Research/Guide-for-Authors
- **Field baseline:** account-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 10 articles from 2024-2025 (abstracts, frontmatter, titles, structures)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
FIELD_SKILL canon applies (Introduction, Hypothesis Development / Theory, Methods, Results, Discussion and Conclusion, References, Tables and Figures). However, this journal emphasizes legal research methodologies rather than quantitative archival/experimental design. Articles may employ doctrinal legal analysis, legislative history analysis, comparative law, and policy critique rather than the regression-based methods common in ABS 4* accounting journals.

### Length
No explicit word limit provided in author guidelines. Spot-checked articles range from 14 to 71 pages. No abstract length beyond 150 words specified; articles follow standard journal format.

### Abstract
**150 words exactly** on a separate page immediately preceding the text. Should concisely inform the reader of the manuscript's topic, method, and findings. Followed by four keywords for indexing.

### Citations
**Bluebook style (17th edition)** for all authorities cited in **consecutively numbered footnotes**. This is a CRITICAL OVERRIDE of FIELD_SKILL's author-date in-text format. Citations appear as superscript Arabic numerals with corresponding footnotes (not endnotes preferred, but footnote structure required). 

Sample citation formats from guidelines:
- Internal Revenue Code: I.R.C. § 61
- Tax Court opinions: [Case Name], [Volume] T.C. [Page]
- Treasury regulations: Treas. Reg. § 1.61-1
- Revenue Rulings: Rev. Rul. [Year]-[Number]
- Secondary sources (journal articles, books, websites): Author Last Name, Article Title, Journal Title [Volume] [Issue] [Page(s)] (Year)

### Headings
No explicit heading convention specified in author guidelines. Spot-checked articles use Arabic numerals with title-case headings (e.g., "1. Introduction", "2. Legislative Background", "3. Analysis"). Some use more discursive narrative section breaks.

### Tables and Figures
No explicit formatting rules provided. Standard academic journal practice applies.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- **Generative AI disclosure:** An "AI disclosure statement" must follow the abstract, describing all tools used, extent of use, and rationale. This is non-negotiable. Failure to disclose AI use violates journal policy.
- **Plagiarism:** Copying or paraphrasing without attribution (including self-plagiarism and text recycling) is prohibited.
- **Human subjects research:** IRB approval required for studies involving surveys, interviews, or experiments. Exemption documentation must be submitted before publication.
- **Data integrity:** Upon conditional acceptance, authors must submit a Data Description Disclosures list and all computer code used in analyses.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Citation and Reference System (ENFORCED)
**MAJOR DELTA:** ATA Journal uses Bluebook style (17th edition) with consecutively numbered footnotes, NOT author-date in-text citations. This is non-negotiable and reflects the journal's legal research tradition. Accounting field journals (JAE, JAR, TAR, CAR) all use author-date; JLTR diverges due to its position at the intersection of law and accounting, emphasizing legal citation conventions.

Footer/endnote format: citations to legal authorities (code sections, regulations, cases) predominate. Secondary scholarly sources appear in footnotes with full bibliographic information on first reference, shortened form thereafter.

Classification: **ENFORCED** (stated in guidelines; confirmed in all spot-checked articles).

### Methodology and Research Design (RELAXATION)
FIELD_SKILL norm emphasizes regression-based archival research, experimental design, and hypothesis testing. JLTR accepts a broader set of legal research methodologies:
- Doctrinal legal analysis (statutory interpretation, case law analysis)
- Legislative history analysis
- Comparative law (cross-jurisdictional tax policy analysis)
- Legal-policy critique and reform proposals
- Compliance and administrative law analysis

Spot-checked articles ("Legislative History" themes in several; "Chevron Deference," "Prefilled Returns," "Vagueness Doctrine" in titles) show heavy use of doctrinal analysis and policy analysis without empirical testing. Methods sections describe legal research procedures (statute review, case review, policy evaluation) rather than statistical procedures.

Classification: **RELAXATION** (articles follow this pattern; guidelines do not explicitly prohibit quantitative work, but tone and scope suggest legal analysis is primary).

### Voice and Person
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm: "we" dominates; active voice throughout. Spot-checked articles maintain formal, clinical tone consistent with accounting field baseline. No deviation observed.

Classification: **FIELD DEFAULT**.

### Technical Density
Lower than FIELD_SKILL norm. Accounting baseline assumes specialist reader familiar with regression specification, coefficient interpretation, statistical significance thresholds. JLTR articles assume specialist reader in tax law and legal analysis, but explanatory passages on statutory interpretation, legislative history, and constitutional doctrine are more extensive. Less mathematical notation; more legal citation.

Classification: **PRACTICE ONLY** (observed in articles; reflects disciplinary difference rather than journal-specific deviation).

## Dimensions With No Delta
- Voice and person: FIELD DEFAULT (we, active voice, formal register)
- Hedging and epistemic caution: FIELD DEFAULT (calibrated to identification strength; well-supported claims stated directly)
- Sentence and paragraph style: FIELD DEFAULT (medium to long sentences; dense paragraphs)
- Discussion and conclusion: FIELD DEFAULT (merged final section; restates findings and contributions; acknowledges limitations)

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Bluebook citation failure:** Submitting with author-date in-text citations (standard in ACCOUNT field baseline) instead of Bluebook footnotes. This violates journal style and requires major revision.

2. **AI disclosure omission:** Failing to include an AI disclosure statement following the abstract. Submissions are reviewed for AI use; omission raises compliance concerns and may delay review.

3. **Imprecise tax authority citations:** Citing tax law informally (e.g., "the tax code," "Treasury regulations") without Bluebook format for code sections and regulatory cites. Accounting research is more casual about citation; JLTR requires formal legal citation.

4. **Methodology-field mismatch:** Submitting quantitative-only empirical work without acknowledging legal research contributions (doctrinal analysis, statutory interpretation, or policy critique). Articles integrating legal analysis and empirical evidence are more successful than purely empirical work without legal grounding.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

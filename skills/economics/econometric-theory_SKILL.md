# Journal Writing Skill: Econometric Theory

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 4
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Cambridge University Press
- **Scope:** Publishes research strengthening the probabilistic and statistical underpinnings of econometric methodology — estimation theory, asymptotics, inference, and identification. Contributions that unify, extend, or reinterpret econometric theory through new perspectives, generalizations, or more elegant methods fall within scope. Purely theoretical/methodological; not an outlet for applied empirical economics papers.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/econometric-theory/information/instructions-contributors and https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/econometric-theory/information/author-instructions/submitting-your-materials
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 27 (FirstView batch of 20 plus Volume 42 Issue 3 batch of 8, spanning Feb-July 2025 publication dates; titles/authors recorded in Sources)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No mandated section architecture is specified in the guidelines (unlike empirical-econ conventions, there is no required Data/Identification/Results sequence). Two distinct article tracks exist: (1) **Articles** — full papers with multiple major contributions, new methods, extensive proofs, and supporting simulations or applications; (2) **Miscellanea** — short papers presenting one or two key innovations, results stated and proved concisely, typically under 20 pages (often around 15). The journal also periodically publishes an **"ET Interview"** feature (career/contributions interview with a senior econometrician), which is a distinct non-standard content type.

### Length
Miscellanea: typically fewer than 20 pages, often around 15. No explicit page limit stated for full Articles.

### Abstract
Short abstract, strictly under 150 words, placed on page 2 of the manuscript along with a running head (max 40 characters) and corresponding-author designation. This is a firm numeric ceiling, tighter than typical field practice.

### Citations
Author-year in-text citation. First citation of a reference must include **all** authors' names (no "et al." truncation on first mention) plus year; "et al." is permitted only on subsequent citations. Journal names must not be abbreviated in the reference list.

### Headings
Manuscript pages numbered consecutively. No specific heading-numbering scheme mandated beyond this.

### Tables and Figures
No journal-specific rules stated beyond standard embedding requirements (all fonts, tables, and graphics must be embedded in the submitted PDF).

### Explicit Prohibitions
Manuscripts must be original, not previously published or under review elsewhere. Personal-communication citations require the cited individual's approval. Authors must secure copyright permissions for any republished material. Editorial process now uses AI tools for administrative processing, but the guidelines explicitly state final editorial decisions are made by a human and submission data is not used for model training.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm for prose register, but the discipline skews further toward formal mathematical exposition than the FIELD_SKILL description of "theory papers" implies: essentially every spot-checked article is structured around theorem/proposition/lemma statements with proofs (often relegated to an appendix or supplementary material), and abstracts describe results in terms of asymptotic properties (consistency, asymptotic normality, convergence rates) rather than economic magnitudes. FIELD DEFAULT for sentence-level voice; PRACTICE ONLY (near-universal in this sample) for theorem-proof-centric exposition intensity exceeding general "theory paper" baseline.

### Statistical Reporting
Not applicable in the FIELD_SKILL sense (coefficients, economic significance, magnitudes) — this journal's "results" are mathematical/asymptotic properties (rates of convergence, limiting distributions, size/power properties of tests), not empirical estimates. Empirical illustrations, when present, are secondary to the theoretical contribution. This is a scope-driven deviation rather than a stylistic one: FIELD_SKILL's Results/Reporting norms (quantitative claims like "increased by X percent") essentially do not apply here. PRACTICE ONLY, driven by ENFORCED scope restriction.

### Citation Density
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.

### Structural Deviations
No standard Introduction-Data-Identification-Results-Conclusion architecture; papers are organized around model setup, assumptions, and formal results (theorems/propositions/corollaries with proofs), consistent with FIELD_SKILL's "Theory and structural papers" track but without any empirical papers appearing in this journal at all. The Miscellanea category is a journal-specific length/ambition tier not present in FIELD_SKILL. ENFORCED (stated in guidelines, confirmed by the existence of a distinct Miscellanea rubric across issues).

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Occasional non-article content type ("ET Interview") profiling a senior figure in econometrics — narrative/biographical prose rather than research reporting. PRACTICE ONLY / low-frequency, not a submission format authors would target, noted for completeness only.

## Common Pitfalls
- Exceeding the 150-word abstract cap — this is a hard numeric constraint stricter than typical field practice, where abstracts of 200+ words are common.
- Truncating author lists with "et al." on the first citation of a source; the journal requires all authors' names at first mention.
- Submitting an applied empirical paper: the journal's scope is explicitly theoretical/methodological, and reviewers will assess fit against theorem-proof-driven contributions, not empirical identification strategies.
- Misjudging length tier: a paper with one concentrated innovation padded to full-Article length may be redirected to Miscellanea; conversely, a multi-contribution paper submitted as Miscellanea (target ~15-20 pages) will be seen as overreaching that category's scope.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

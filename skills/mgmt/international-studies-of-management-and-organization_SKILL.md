# Journal Writing Skill: International Studies of Management and Organization

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN
- **Publisher:** Taylor & Francis (Routledge)
- **ISSN:** 0020-8825 (print) / 1558-0911 (online)
- **Frequency:** Quarterly
- **Scope:** International research on management and organization theory and practice, with focus on multinational enterprises, cross-cultural organizational behavior, ethics, CSR, sustainability, innovation, human resource management, and organizational design.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> Explicit requirements inferred from accessible article metadata and Taylor & Francis standard practices. Note: Official submission guidelines page (tandfonline.com) returned HTTP 403; constraints below are inferred from published articles and editorial practice.

### Structure
Follows FIELD_SKILL norms. Confirmed structures across spot-checked articles:
- Quantitative/empirical work: Abstract → Introduction → Theory/Literature → Methods → Results → Discussion → Conclusion
- Qualitative/conceptual work: Abstract → Introduction → Theoretical Framework → [Conceptual/Empirical sections] → Discussion → Conclusion
- Some articles (e.g., scoping reviews) use: Abstract → Introduction → Methodology → Synthesis sections → Research Agenda/Conclusions

### Length
No explicit word limit found in accessible guidelines. Observed range across 8 recent articles: 112-139 pages when typeset (suggesting 8,000–12,000 words typical). Inference: likely Taylor & Francis standard of 5,000–8,000 words for regular research articles.

### Abstract
- Unstructured prose format observed in all sampled articles
- Typical length: 150–250 words
- States research question, methodology, key findings, and contribution
- No citations in abstracts

### Citations
- APA style in-text format: (Author, Year)
- Observed citation density: Moderate. Introduction and theory sections citation-dense; results sections sparse; discussion re-engages citations
- Consistent with FIELD_SKILL (see mgmt baseline)

### Headings
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Hierarchical structure with numbered or unnumbered H2/H3 sections. Subheadings organized by substantive argument or method, not generic labels.

### Tables and Figures
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Tables report key findings with notes; figures used for frameworks and data displays. All labeled with captions.

### Explicit Prohibitions
None identified in accessible sources beyond standard journal ethics (no simultaneous submission, no plagiarism, no fabrication).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates or warrants emphasis.

### Research Topics and Scope Breadth
**Delta: PRACTICE ONLY — broader international scope emphasis**

This journal places explicit emphasis on international, cross-cultural, and comparative management research. Articles span:
- Multinational enterprises and subsidiary dynamics
- Cross-national organizational behavior and management
- Culture and context in leadership and organizational practices
- Expatriate management and international human resource management
- Ethics, CSR, and sustainability in international contexts

While FIELD_SKILL acknowledges management journals' breadth, ISMO's framing is distinctly international in mission. Authors are expected to frame theoretical and empirical contributions in relation to international/cross-cultural contexts, not solely US-based or domestic organization theory.

**Implication for authors:** Emphasize the cross-national or comparative dimension of your argument. If studying a single country, situate it against international literature or multi-country theory.

### Article Type: Scoping Reviews and Bibliometric Syntheses
**Delta: PRACTICE ONLY — scoping reviews and framework-mapping papers appear more frequently than in some management outlets**

Observed in sample: "Mapping the past, present, and future of ISMO: a scoping review" (2022); "Charting the future of digital servitization using CIMO framework" (2024, using systematic review of 83 articles). These are integrative/review articles with rigorous methodology (CIMO framework, systematic search protocols, propositional mapping) rather than pure conceptual theorizing.

**Implication for authors:** If proposing a review or framework article, ensure it includes explicit methodology (search protocol, inclusion/exclusion criteria, analytical framework) and propositional synthesis, not merely narrative literature review.

### Quantitative Methods: Multivariate and Moderation Analyses
**Delta: PRACTICE ONLY — moderation and mediation analyses, network analysis**

Observed patterns across empirical articles:
- Mediation and moderation models (e.g., "Context-based effect of shared leadership… on counterproductive work behavior" using PLS-SEM with team-type moderation)
- Social network analysis (e.g., faculty hiring network analysis using Gephi)
- Structural equation modeling (SEM) and partial least squares (PLS-SEM)

While FIELD_SKILL acknowledges diverse quantitative approaches, ISMO articles show consistent emphasis on conditional/contextual effects (moderation) and mechanistic pathways (mediation), fitting the journal's international/contextual focus.

**Implication for authors:** Quantitative papers should incorporate boundary conditions (moderation) or causal mechanisms (mediation) rather than main-effects models alone. Address "under what conditions" and "through what mechanisms."

### Qualitative and Mixed Methods
**Delta: FIELD DEFAULT — follows FIELD_SKILL norm**

Qualitative empirical work (case studies, interviews) and mixed-methods papers appear in the sample. Example: "Taking the bull by the horns" (case study of LGBT inclusivity in Sri Lankan conglomerate) with embedded ethnographic elements and policy analysis.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- **Voice and person:** FIELD DEFAULT — "we" throughout; active voice for argument, passive for methods/data collection
- **Hedging and epistemic stance:** FIELD DEFAULT — Moderate hedging for interpretive claims; direct assertion for established findings and procedures
- **Contribution framing:** FIELD DEFAULT — Explicit contribution statements in introduction; numbered or bullet-pointed; 2–4 contributions typical
- **Discussion structure:** FIELD DEFAULT — Opens with findings summary, develops theoretical implications, addresses limitations, closes with future research directions
- **Propositions vs. Hypotheses:** FIELD DEFAULT — Hypotheses (H1, H2…) for empirical work; propositions (P1, P2…) for conceptual work

## Common Pitfalls Specific to This Journal

1. **Missing international/cross-cultural context:** Submitting a paper that reads as purely US-domestic organizational research with no engagement with international literature or comparative framing. ISMO's mission demands explicit positioning within global management discourse.

2. **Insufficient mechanistic or conditional reasoning:** Empirical papers that report main effects without exploring moderation (when, for whom, under what conditions?) or mediation (through what mechanisms?) miss the journal's emphasis on context-contingent organizational phenomena.

3. **Vague or absent methodology in review articles:** If proposing a scoping review, bibliometric analysis, or framework synthesis, failing to specify search protocol, inclusion/exclusion criteria, or analytical framework. Recent ISMO articles model rigorous review methodology; narrative literature reviews without explicit procedure are disfavored.

4. **Underplaying practical relevance in multinational contexts:** ISMO values both theoretical rigor and managerial relevance in international settings. Papers that emphasize theory while ignoring or trivializing implications for multinational organizations or cross-cultural management practice miss the journal's applied orientation.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

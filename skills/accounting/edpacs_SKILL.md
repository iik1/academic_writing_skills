# Journal Writing Skill: EDPACS

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ACCOUNT
- **Publisher:** Taylor & Francis
- **Full Title:** EDPACS: The EDP Audit, Control, and Security Newsletter
- **Scope:** World's longest-running IT audit newsletter (since 1973), published monthly. Focuses on audit, control, and security issues. Publishes feature articles by leading practitioners providing guidance and pragmatic solutions to current issues in IT audit and information security.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.tandfonline.com/journals/uedp20 (full author guidelines not publicly accessible; formatted per Taylor & Francis standards)
- **Field baseline:** account-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 8 (via search results, abstracts, and metadata from Taylor & Francis and bibliometric analyses covering 2024-2026)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict. Note: Full author guidelines are paywalled; the following reflects Taylor & Francis standard practices and explicit journal mission statements found in public sources.

### Journal Type and Article Nature
EDPACS is a **practitioner-focused newsletter, not a research journal**. Unlike the ABS 4*/4 accounting journals in FIELD_SKILL (which publish empirical, archival, and experimental research), EDPACS publishes feature articles by practitioners offering guidance, advice, and pragmatic solutions to real-world audit and control challenges. This is a fundamental distinction: EDPACS does not expect hypothesis testing, formal empirical methods, or traditional research methodology sections.

### Structure
The canonical ACCOUNT field structure (Introduction → Hypothesis Development → Data/Methods → Results → Discussion/Conclusion) does **not** apply to EDPACS in the rigid form described in FIELD_SKILL. Articles are typically organized as:
- **Opening/Context section** — Establishes the practical problem or issue
- **Body/Guidance section(s)** — Presents advice, recommendations, case examples, or best practices (no formal hypothesis testing required)
- **Conclusion/Takeaways** — Summarizes key points and actionable insights

The exact section structure varies by article; editors appear to allow flexibility appropriate to the practitioner focus.

### Manuscript Length
EDPACS publishes **comprehensive articles** intended to allow topics to be explored in depth. Based on Taylor & Francis standard practices for practitioner publications and the characterization of articles as "comprehensive," expect a typical article to be approximately **5,000–8,000 words** (roughly 15–25 double-spaced pages). No explicit word limit found in public sources; this is inferred from journal characterization.

### Abstract
Not found in public guidelines. EDPACS likely requires a brief summary or abstract for database indexing purposes (standard Taylor & Francis practice), but specifics on length or structured vs. unstructured format are not publicly stated.

### Citations
Follows **author-date (Harvard/APA-style) in-text citations** typical of Taylor & Francis journals. Paraphrase is preferred; direct quotation from practitioner sources or case examples may be used when appropriate to illustrate a point. Full reference list required (alphabetical, author-date format).

### Headings
Taylor & Francis standard: Headings appear to use **title case** with hierarchical numbering. Specific conventions (e.g., all-caps vs. title case for level-1 headings) are not publicly specified for EDPACS; follow Taylor & Francis default style (typically Title Case for main headings, standard fonts, not all-caps).

### Tables and Figures
Standard Taylor & Francis practice applies. Tables and figures should include descriptive titles/captions and, if from external sources, proper attribution. Figures should be high-resolution and publication-ready.

### Submission System
Submissions handled through Taylor & Francis Online Editorial Manager system (standard for Taylor & Francis journals).

### Explicit Prohibitions
None found in public sources. Standard academic ethics apply (no plagiarism, data fabrication, dual submission).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Research Methodology and Empirical Requirements
**RELAXATION: FIELD_SKILL assumes empirical, archival, or experimental research with formal hypothesis testing; EDPACS does not.**

EDPACS is a practitioner newsletter. Articles do **not** require:
- Formal hypothesis statements
- Data collection and analysis sections
- Statistical tables and regression results
- Identification strategies or causal claims
- Robustness checks

Instead, articles present **practitioner experience, best practices, case examples, and guidance**. A typical EDPACS article might cite existing research or standards (e.g., COSO, COBIT) but does not present original empirical work. Authors are encouraged to draw on professional experience and organizational examples rather than formal study designs.

### Voice and Register
**PRACTICE ONLY: Slightly less technical density and more accessible register than ABS 4* baseline.**

While FIELD_SKILL specifies "high technical density" and a "clinical, precise register," EDPACS articles are written for **practicing IT auditors and security professionals**, not solely academic specialists. The language is professional but somewhat less dense with statistical notation and formal methodology terminology. The register remains formal; colloquialisms are not appropriate, but explanatory prose is more prominent relative to equations and technical notation.

First-person plural ("we") remains the dominant convention when authors refer to collective experience or recommendations. However, some articles adopt a more editorial tone appropriate to guidance columns.

### Citation Density
**RELAXATION: Lower than FIELD_SKILL in some sections.**

Citation density follows FIELD_SKILL norms in the opening sections (establishing the issue and prior guidance). However, the **body of the article** (guidance and recommendations) may have **lower citation density** than a research article, particularly if recommendations draw on practitioner experience rather than academic literature. The closing section cites prior work when positioning the contribution to professional practice.

### Structural Deviations
**PRACTICE ONLY: More flexible section organization; no mandatory hypothesis development section.**

EDPACS does not enforce the near-universal hypothesis development → data → results → discussion sequence of FIELD_SKILL. Instead, articles follow a more narrative flow appropriate to the genre: problem statement, exploration of the issue, practical guidance, and conclusion. Authors have flexibility to organize by topic, case example, or step-by-step recommendations rather than by hypothesis.

### Article Length and Depth
**PRACTICE ONLY: Published as "comprehensive articles allowing topics to be explored in depth."**

EDPACS explicitly aims for depth within each issue. Articles are typically longer and more exploratory than short research notes, allowing issues to be covered thoroughly. This aligns with the newsletter's mission to support practitioners with pragmatic solutions, not brief empirical findings.

### Statistical Reporting and Quantitative Results
**RELAXATION: Not required. Articles may reference statistics or industry data (e.g., benchmark statistics, regulatory data) but do not present original quantitative analysis.**

EDPACS is not a venue for presenting statistical results, regression tables, or hypothesis tests. If articles include quantitative data (e.g., industry surveys, benchmark figures), this is typically for illustrative or contextual purposes, not as the primary research contribution. Statistical reporting norms in FIELD_SKILL do not apply.

### Hedging and Epistemic Caution
**FIELD DEFAULT: Hedging conventions follow FIELD_SKILL.**

Recommendations and guidance are stated with appropriate confidence. Well-supported recommendations use direct language ("we recommend," "best practice is," "organizations should"). Interpretative or less certain guidance uses softened language ("may help," "consider," "suggest"). This aligns with FIELD_SKILL norms for calibrating hedging to the strength of the underlying support.

- **Citation style (author-date):** FIELD DEFAULT — Author-date in-text citations and alphabetical reference list follow ACCOUNT field convention and Taylor & Francis standard.
- **Discussion and limitations:** FIELD DEFAULT — Articles should acknowledge practical constraints and limitations of recommendations. A closing discussion section is expected.
- **Tone and professionalism:** FIELD DEFAULT — High formality maintained throughout; no colloquialisms or casual registers.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Over-research-fying the article.** EDPACS is not a peer-reviewed research journal. Do not attempt to frame practitioner guidance as a formal hypothesis test with methods, results, and statistical tables. Practitioners reading EDPACS expect actionable guidance, not empirical research.

2. **Under-citing relevant standards and prior guidance.** While empirical citation density is lower than FIELD_SKILL, EDPACS articles should cite relevant frameworks (COSO, COBIT, regulatory guidance, prior practitioner guidance) to ground recommendations. Failing to reference established guidance weakens the article.

3. **Insufficient depth and specificity in recommendations.** EDPACS publishes "comprehensive articles" intended to allow topics to be explored in depth. Brief, surface-level advice is not sufficient. Ground recommendations in concrete examples, case scenarios, or step-by-step guidance.

4. **Ignoring the practitioner audience.** Articles must be accessible to practicing IT auditors and security professionals with varying backgrounds. Over-technical jargon without explanation or assumption of advanced statistical literacy will alienate the intended readership. Balance rigor with accessibility.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

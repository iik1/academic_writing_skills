# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of the Economics of Ageing

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Elsevier
- **Scope:** International journal publishing original theoretical and empirical research on the interaction between demographic change and the economy, covering labor, health and family economics, social security, income distribution, migration, and economic growth.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/the-journal-of-the-economics-of-ageing/publish/guide-for-authors (access restricted; public information sourced via Paperpile and search results)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 12 distinct articles from 2025–2026 (details in Sources section)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Manuscripts should be divided into clearly numbered sections using Arabic numerals: Section 1, 2, 3, etc. Subsections are numbered as 1.1, 1.1.1, 1.1.2, then 1.2, and so forth. Subsections may be given brief headings. The journal follows standard economic article structure (Introduction, Data/Methods, Results, Conclusion) with sections optionally organized around theoretical or empirical content.

### Length
No explicit overall manuscript page or word limit was found in publicly accessible guidelines. The abstract is capped at **250 words** and must be a single, unstructured block.

### Abstract
- **Max length:** 250 words
- **Format:** Unstructured (single paragraph, not broken into purpose/methods/results/conclusion)
- **Requirement:** Must stand alone; avoid citations when possible
- **Content:** Should briefly state the purpose of research, principal results, and major conclusions
- **Abbreviations:** Define all non-standard or uncommon abbreviations at first mention

### Citations
Author-year format (parenthetical or in-text). References cited in text by name and year in parentheses with a corresponding bibliography. Format follows Elsevier conventions (author-year style as per Paperpile documentation).

### Headings
Numbered sections (1, 2, 3, ...) with numbered subsections (1.1, 1.2, 1.1.1, etc.). Subsections may carry brief headings. No prescribed naming convention for the Conclusion section.

### Tables and Figures
Standard economic journal practice applies. Tables should include notes explaining variable definitions, standard error clustering, and sample restrictions. Figures should be used to illustrate key outcome variation, distributional results, or model fit. No explicit word count or formatting restrictions were found in available guidelines.

### Keywords and Highlights
- **Keywords:** 1 to 7 keywords in English, required for indexing. Avoid multi-word keywords using "and" or "of" where possible.
- **Highlights:** 3 to 5 bullet points, each a maximum of 85 characters including spaces. These are promotional/summary statements, distinct from keywords.

### Acknowledgements
Acknowledgements section is placed directly before the reference list, not on the title page or as a footnote.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Generative AI Declaration required: Authors must declare use of generative AI in manuscript preparation upon submission. Ethical requirements per Elsevier Publishing Ethics Policy apply. Sex and gender-based analyses should be integrated into research design when relevant to human or animal subjects.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates.

### Abstract Format
**Delta: ENFORCED RELAXATION**
Unlike many ABS 4*/4 journals, Journal of the Economics of Ageing explicitly requires **unstructured abstracts** (single paragraph) rather than the structured format (objective/methods/results/conclusions) seen in some top-tier outlets. The 250-word limit is standard; the unstructured format is the journal's norm at this ABS 2 tier.

### Highlights Requirement
**Delta: ENFORCED (journal-specific addition)**
The journal enforces a "Highlights" section distinct from keywords and abstract: 3–5 bullet points of max 85 characters each. This is not part of FIELD_SKILL baseline and is specific to Elsevier's data submission model used by this journal.

### Keywords Requirement
**Delta: ENFORCED (slight variation)**
The journal requires 1–7 keywords (rather than variable journal norms). Keywords must be in English and should avoid multi-word phrases with "and" or "of". This is more prescriptive than typical FIELD_SKILL norms.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Exceeding 250 words in abstract:** Many authors from top-tier journals accustomed to longer abstracts (300–350 words) will need to compress. Cut to 250 words strictly; prioritize principal results and conclusions over methods detail.

2. **Omitting or undersizing Highlights:** First-time submitters often skip the Highlights section or treat it as a duplicate of keywords. Ensure 3–5 distinct bullet points (85 characters max) that serve as promotional summaries of key findings; these should be accessible to a non-specialist audience.

3. **Structured abstract when unstructured is required:** Submitters familiar with medical or public health journals may instinctively structure abstracts into Objective/Methods/Results/Conclusion sections. The Journal of the Economics of Ageing requires a single flowing paragraph.

4. **Failing to declare generative AI use:** Elsevier now requires explicit disclosure of generative AI use in preparation. Omitting this declaration can delay or reject a submission; include it proactively in cover letter or submission metadata.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

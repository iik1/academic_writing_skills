# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Competition Law & Economics

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Oxford University Press
- **Scope:** Peer-reviewed scholarship at the intersection of competition (antitrust) law and economics, covering US, EU, and other jurisdictions' enforcement, digital markets regulation (DMA/AI Act), cartel behavior, and industrial organization applied to competition policy.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://academic.oup.com/jcle/pages/author-guidelines and https://academic.oup.com/jcle/pages/General_Instructions
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 11 (title/abstract level: "Economic Effects and EU State Aid Control" (McMahon); "A Critical Evaluation of Ex-Ante Regulation and Ex-Post Antitrust..." (Lee & Saha); "Algorithms and Antitrust..." (Inderst & Thomas); "Access Pricing for App Stores Under the DMA" (Crémer et al.); "Access to Health Data, Competition, and Regulatory Alternatives" (Lianos); "Beyond Interchange Fee Caps..."; "Disadvantageous Semi-Collusion...Norwegian Airline Industry"; "Financial Performance Metrics in Cartelized Markets..."; "Digital Tying, Platform Envelopment..."; full-text or near-full-text reviewed: "On Corporate Cartels as Common Pool Resources" (Schmal, 2025, DOI 10.1093/joclec/nhaf017) and "Harnessing Platform Envelopment in the Digital World" (Condorelli & Padilla, 2020, working-paper version)). Full-text access was paywalled for most articles; abstracts, working-paper mirrors (SSRN/author sites), and one open PDF mirror were used.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No mandatory section template is specified in the guidelines beyond requiring an abstract. Observed articles use a conventional Introduction → body sections → Conclusion structure, consistent with FIELD_SKILL, but body section content is often organized around legal/institutional topics (e.g., "Cartels providing a common pool resource," "Varying time horizons...") rather than the Data/Empirical Strategy/Results architecture typical of empirical ABS 4/4* econ papers.

### Length
**Hard limit: 4,000–6,000 words**, inclusive of footnotes, diagrams, and tables. This is far shorter than typical ABS 4*/4 econ articles and materially shorter than FIELD_SKILL's implicit assumption of longer-form papers with separate online appendices.

### Abstract
Required, English, **maximum 200 words**, unstructured (no mandated subheadings/fields).

### Citations
Guidelines defer citation mechanics to a separate style document; that document was not directly accessible. However, spot-checked full-text articles consistently use **footnote-based citations** (numbered superscript footnotes with full bibliographic detail), not parenthetical author-year in-text citations. This is a law-review convention, not the economics author-year norm.

### Headings
Not explicitly mandated. Observed practice: Roman-numeral or plain-word section headings naming the analytical topic (legal/institutional framing) rather than the standardized empirical-econ labels (e.g., "Empirical Strategy," "Data").

### Tables and Figures
Alt text required for all images/figures for accessibility (submission-system requirement, not a stylistic norm). Permissions required for reproduced tables/figures. Tables/figures count toward the 4,000–6,000 word limit.

### Explicit Prohibitions
No ghost/gift/guest authorship. No undisclosed AI-tool use (must be acknowledged if used; AI tools cannot be listed as authors). No simultaneous submission elsewhere — must withdraw other submissions if accepted for review here. Must comply with ASCOLA 2025 Declaration of Transparency and Disclosure (disclose funding/support from "interested parties" over the past three years).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
PRACTICE ONLY / RELAXATION. Spot-checked articles blend economic analysis with legal-doctrinal argumentation more than FIELD_SKILL's econ baseline anticipates. Prose is more discursive and policy-oriented (e.g., "the article concludes by considering a range of possible policy interventions"), with less emphasis on the "we find/we show" quantitative-result-preview convention than at ABS 4*/4 empirical outlets. Reflects this journal's mixed law-and-economics readership rather than a stated norm.

### Citation Density
PRACTICE ONLY. Footnote-based citation (see Hard Constraints) drives much higher apparent citation density in footnotes than the in-text author-year density described in FIELD_SKILL. Legal authorities (cases, regulations, agency decisions) are cited alongside economics literature, which FIELD_SKILL does not address at all.

### Structural Deviations
RELAXATION. Given the strict 4,000–6,000 word cap, papers do not carry the full empirical architecture (standalone Data, Identification, Robustness sections) assumed as "near-universal" in FIELD_SKILL for empirical papers. Many published pieces are theoretical/policy essays or law-and-economics commentary rather than data-driven empirical papers; when empirical, methods sections are compressed rather than separately titled.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
GUIDELINE ONLY. Explicit instruction to minimize mathematical formalism: use math only where necessary, explain its necessity, and relegate technical derivations to appendices so the article stays intelligible to non-mathematical (i.e., legally trained) readers. This is stricter than FIELD_SKILL's assumption of a trained-economist reader for whom technical notation needs no justification.

## Common Pitfalls
- Writing a full ABS 4*-style empirical paper (Data/Identification/Robustness as separate sections plus online appendix) and exceeding the hard 6,000-word cap — this journal expects a compressed, essay-length treatment.
- Using parenthetical author-year citations throughout instead of the footnote convention observed in published articles.
- Including heavy mathematical/econometric formalism in the main text rather than an appendix, when the guidelines explicitly ask authors to justify and minimize math for a mixed law/economics readership.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Accounting History

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** BUS HIST & ECON HIST
- **Publisher:** SAGE
- **Scope:** International peer-reviewed journal publishing high-quality historical research on the advent, development, and impacts of accounting as both a social and technical practice. Encourages traditional, critical, and interpretative historical research across all organisational forms.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/author-instructions/ach
- **Field baseline:** business-economic-history-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 5 (titles from Vol. 30, 2024-2025; abstracts and structural patterns via search results)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No rigid section requirements beyond those expected by the field baseline. Authors have flexibility in organization (chronological, thematic, or hybrid). Headings must be formatted with strict conventions: Major headings flush left, bold, lowercase, two font sizes larger than main text; second-level headings flush left, bold, lowercase, same size as text; third-level headings flush left, italics, lowercase, same size as text. Unnumbered. Headings have initial capital + remaining words lowercase unless proper names.

### Length
- Target: 11,000 words (excluding abstract, acknowledgements, references)
- Maximum: 12,000 words if justified by topic complexity, analysis depth, and research quality
- Abstract: unstructured, single paragraph, 150 words maximum
- Text: 12-point font, 1.5 line spacing, 3cm left/right margins, 5cm top/bottom margins

### Abstract
Unstructured 150-word abstract required. Must state purpose, major findings, and conclusions. Cannot exceed 150 words; submissions not meeting this requirement rejected. Placed between title and main body.

### Citations
Notes appear as endnotes (not footnotes), numbered consecutively. Endnotes begin on new page before references. Implied citation style is SAGE Harvard (author-date with endnote format). Full reference list required; exact Harvard formatting conventions apply per SAGE standards. Journal titles not abbreviated.

### Headings
Strictly formatted three-level hierarchy (see Structure). No numbered headings. Initial capital in first word; remaining words lowercase unless proper names.

### Tables and Figures
Figures, tables, diagrams, and appendices numbered consecutively in order of appearance in text; all must have titles. Figure resolution 300dpi. Color figures appear in color online free of charge; color in print incurs costs quoted at acceptance.

### Quotation Marks
Single quotes throughout, with double quotes within single-quoted material. Long quotations (40+ words) displayed and indented with line space above and below, separate from text.

### Numbers and Language
- Spell out numbers one to nine; use numerals for 10+
- Exception: at sentence beginning, spell out the number
- Percentages: "per cent" (not %), use numerals with percentages
- Exception: in tables, figures, appendices, and brackets within text, use %
- Centuries: write out in full (e.g., "nineteenth century" not "19th century")
- UK spelling except in quoted material

### Abbreviations
Prohibited in: title, abstract, keywords. Define all abbreviations on first use (except in abstract) as: full term followed by abbreviation in parentheses. Use consistently thereafter, including at sentence start.

### Keywords
Minimum 5 keywords required, listed after abstract. Lowercase except for proper names. Should be specific to research topic.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- No abbreviations in title, abstract, keywords
- No ChatGPT or AI chatbots listed as authors
- Preprint servers accepted, but no updates to preprint while manuscript under review

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Citation Format: Endnote System (ENFORCED)
FIELD_SKILL allows flexibility in citation format. Accounting History enforces endnotes (not footnotes), numbered consecutively, placed before references. This is journal-specific and stricter than field practice.

### Heading Conventions: Strict Three-Level Hierarchy (ENFORCED)
FIELD_SKILL permits varied heading styles. Accounting History mandates specific formatting: level 1 flush left, bold, lowercase, +2 font sizes; level 2 flush left, bold, lowercase, same size; level 3 flush left, italics, lowercase, same size. All unnumbered.

### Number Representation: Specific Rules (ENFORCED)
FIELD_SKILL allows flexible number formatting. Accounting History specifies: spell 1–9, numerals 10+; "per cent" not "%"; centuries written in full. These rules are strict.

### Abstract Format: Unstructured, 150-word Ceiling (ENFORCED)
FIELD_SKILL notes field abstracts are unstructured. Accounting History enforces strict 150-word limit; submissions exceeding this are rejected without review.

### Margin and Spacing Specifications (ENFORCED)
FIELD_SKILL does not specify margins or line spacing. Accounting History requires: 1.5 spacing, 3cm left/right, 5cm head/foot, 12-point font. Strict formatting requirement.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Exceeding abstract word limit.** Submissions with abstracts >150 words are desk-rejected without review. Count carefully; Accounting History enforces this strictly.

2. **Incorrect heading formatting.** Many authors fail to apply the three-level heading hierarchy correctly. Level 1 must be bold, +2 sizes; level 2 bold, same size; level 3 italics. Check all headings before submission.

3. **Using abbreviations in title or abstract.** Even common field abbreviations (e.g., "IFRS," "MNE") are prohibited in title/abstract/keywords. This catches many submissions at desk review.

4. **Footnotes instead of endnotes.** The journal requires endnotes only. Word processors default to footnotes; manually convert all notes to endnotes before submission, or use Sage Track's template.

5. **Exceeding word limit without justification.** The 11,000-word guideline is firm; longer submissions (up to 12,000) need explicit justification in the cover letter tied to topic complexity and analysis depth. Generic lengthy submissions are returned.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Field Writing Style: BUS HIST & ECON HIST

## Scope and Intended Use
This baseline is derived exclusively from ABS 4 journals in Business
History & Economic History (no ABS 4* journals exist in this field under
the AJG 2024 ratings): Business History, Business History Review, and
Economic History Review. It describes the writing conventions expected at
the top of this field: narrative-analytical history grounded in archival
or quantitative primary sources, engaging business, economic, and social
history literatures. Journal-specific skills in this field should record
only deviations from this baseline; where a journal-specific skill is
silent on a dimension, this baseline applies.

## Typical Article Structure
Leading journals in this field expect an opening section that sets out
the historical puzzle, states the article's argument or contribution, and
positions the paper against existing historiography — functioning as an
introduction even when not formally labelled "Introduction." A literature
or historiographical review is near-universal, though it is often woven
into the introduction and early sections rather than isolated into its
own heading. A sources-and-methods section — describing archives, firm
records, government data, or quantitative datasets used and their
limitations — is expected, particularly for empirically dense work; for
narrative or interpretive pieces it may be folded into the introduction.
The core of the article consists of substantive sections organized
either chronologically (by period or event sequence) or thematically (by
mechanism, actor, or issue), reflecting the narrative-analytical mode
dominant in the field. A conclusion that restates the contribution,
situates findings within broader historiographical or economic debates,
and often gestures toward implications for present-day understanding is
near-universal. Formal literature reviews as separate headed sections are
optional; explicit "hypotheses" or "results" sections in the social-science
sense are rare outside quantitative economic history work, where a more
econometric structure (data, methods, results, robustness) is expected
similarly to applied economics. Abstracts are compact, non-technical
summaries of the puzzle, argument, and contribution rather than
structured (background/methods/results) abstracts.

## Voice, Person, and Register
At the ABS 4 level, register is formal and scholarly but narrative in
character, closer to historical writing than to social-science reporting.
First-person plural ("we argue," "we show") appears and is fully
accepted, particularly in more quantitative or economics-oriented pieces
and in multi-authored articles, but many articles — especially
single-authored, archivally grounded business history — favor impersonal
or third-person constructions ("this article argues," "this paper shows").
Practice varies: the balance between first-person and impersonal framing
differs by article type and sub-tradition within the field. No consensus
norm exists on this point, though impersonal framing is somewhat more
common in narrative business history and first-person plural more common
in quantitative economic history. Active voice dominates in narrative and
argumentative passages; passive voice appears more often in descriptions
of data construction or institutional processes. Overall tone favors
measured, interpretive claims over bold assertion.

## Sentence and Paragraph Style
Sentences are typically medium to long, accommodating subordinate clauses
that carry historical qualification (dates, actors, causal chains,
contingent circumstances). Paragraphs are substantial and often
organized around a single episode, actor, or mechanism, developed with
supporting evidence and citation before transitioning to the next.
Signposting is present but lighter than in mainstream economics — readers
are guided through chronology and argument via narrative transitions as
much as through explicit meta-commentary ("in this section, we...").
Long qualifying and contextualizing clauses are normal and not treated as
a stylistic weakness in this field, unlike in more terse social-science
writing traditions.

## Argumentation and Rhetorical Moves
The field follows a recognizable CARS-like pattern: establish the
historical or economic phenomenon and its significance, show that
existing historiography or economic literature has treated it
incompletely, inaccurately, or not at all, and stake out the article's
distinct contribution — whether new archival evidence, a new
interpretation of known events, a new dataset, or a challenge to a
received narrative. Gaps are framed historiographically as often as they
are framed as literature gaps in the social-science sense: authors argue
that prior accounts have been incomplete, teleological, insufficiently
attentive to specific actors (labour, women, non-Western firms), or
have overlooked particular sources. Contributions are frequently framed
in terms of "recovering," "reassessing," or "complicating" existing
narratives rather than "filling a gap" in a stylized empirical
literature. Counter-narratives and revisionist framing are common and
valued: leading journals reward articles that challenge received wisdom
about a firm, industry, policy, or period. Explicit hypothesis-testing
language is rare outside quantitative economic history; qualitative and
narrative pieces argue through accumulation of evidence and interpretive
synthesis rather than formal hypothesis confirmation/rejection.

## Methods Conventions
Sources and methods are described with substantial procedural detail:
which archives, firm records, government reports, newspapers, or
databases were consulted, how gaps or biases in the sources are handled,
and how quantitative data (where used) were constructed or coded. Past
tense dominates methods and sources descriptions, consistent with
historical narration. For quantitative economic history, conventions
converge with applied economics: description of data sources, variable
construction, and empirical strategy in reasonably formal terms,
including robustness discussion. For qualitative and archival business
history, methods discussion is less formalized and more embedded in
prose, but still expected to make source provenance and interpretive
choices transparent. Reflexivity about the limits and silences of the
archive (e.g., what perspectives are absent, whose records survive) is
valued, particularly for topics involving marginalized actors.

## Results and Reporting Norms
Practice varies: narrative and archival articles integrate evidence and
interpretation throughout the piece rather than separating "results" from
"discussion," while quantitative economic history articles more closely
follow social-science convention with distinct empirical results
sections, tables of estimates, and separate interpretive discussion. No
single consensus norm governs this dimension across the field. Where
tables and figures are used (time series, firm-level statistics,
regression output), they are expected to be clearly sourced and
accompanied by prose interpretation rather than left to stand alone.
Statistical precision (standard errors, significance levels, confidence
intervals) is expected in quantitative economic history to a standard
comparable with applied economics; narrative business history typically
reports magnitudes and trends in prose without this apparatus. Direct
quotation from primary sources (correspondence, minutes, testimony,
newspapers) is a valued and common evidentiary practice, used to
substantiate interpretive claims about motive, perception, or contested
events.

## Discussion and Conclusion Norms
Conclusions are expected to restate the article's contribution clearly,
connect the specific case or dataset back to broader historiographical or
economic debates, and address why the finding matters beyond the
particular firm, industry, or period studied. Leading journals reward
conclusions that draw out generalizable implications — for theory,
for other national or sectoral contexts, or for present-day
understanding — without overclaiming direct lessons for current policy or
practice. Acknowledgment of the limits of the evidence base (archival
gaps, generalizability of a single case) is expected in the conclusion or
discussion rather than omitted.

## Citation and Literature Engagement
Citation density is high throughout the article, not concentrated solely
in a literature review section, reflecting the evidentiary and
historiographical character of the field: claims about facts, dates,
figures, and interpretations are routinely footnoted or cited to primary
and secondary sources. Paraphrase dominates over direct quotation when
engaging secondary scholarly literature; direct quotation is reserved
primarily for primary sources and for capturing distinctive phrasing from
historical actors. Disagreement with prior scholarship is expressed
directly but courteously — authors state that previous accounts
"overlooked," "underestimated," "have not fully explained," or "assumed"
particular things, rather than using harsher confrontational language.
Engagement with historiography is expected to be genuinely international
and interdisciplinary, drawing on economic history, business history,
and relevant social science literatures as appropriate to the topic.

## Hedging and Epistemic Caution
Hedging intensity is moderate: authors qualify causal claims about
historical events with language such as "suggests," "appears to have,"
"likely reflects," particularly where the evidentiary record is
incomplete or contested, but confident, direct statements of the
article's central argument are also expected and valued — excessive
hedging that obscures the thesis is not rewarded. Correlational or
suggestive quantitative findings are hedged more heavily than
qualitative interpretive claims grounded in strong primary-source
evidence, where authors write with more assurance. Uncertainty about
counterfactuals (what would have happened otherwise) is routinely and
explicitly flagged.

## What This Baseline Does NOT Specify
This baseline does not specify word or page limits, abstract length
limits, footnote or endnote formatting conventions, reference list style
(e.g., Chicago vs. author-date), submission requirements, or any
publisher-specific formatting rules — these are journal-specific and must
be obtained from individual journal author guidelines. It also does not
specify discipline-specific quantitative methods choices (e.g., which
econometric estimator to prefer) beyond noting that quantitative economic
history converges toward applied-economics norms; those choices depend on
the empirical question and data at hand.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: History of Political Economy

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Duke University Press
- **Scope:** History of economic thought, methodology of economics, intellectual history of economics, biographical and contextual studies of economists and schools of economic thought
- **Guidelines URL:** https://hope.econ.duke.edu/hope-journal/guidelines
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 1 full abstract (Fontaine 2024, "Behavioral Consistency in Economics and Sociology"); limitations: paywall access restricted access to full texts; spot-check based on available guideline document and one published abstract

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No mandatory section ordering or naming is specified in guidelines. Based on Fontaine 2024 example, articles follow a narrative structure appropriate to historical-analytical content rather than the standardized empirical architecture of ABS 4*/4 journals. Sections are organized thematically and chronologically according to intellectual-historical argument, not data-analysis sequence.

### Length
Guidelines do not specify word or page limits. Manuscript length appears flexible, determined by argument scope.

### Abstract
Guidelines do not specify abstract format, length, or required fields. Abstracts are not mandated as structured/unstructured; their presence and length vary.

### Citations
**Chicago Manual of Style (17th edition)** is the mandatory style guide. Note: guidelines reference 17th edition; verify current policy. This differs from FIELD_SKILL's near-universal author-year (Harvard) convention in economics. Chicago style as used in history typically permits flexibility in note format (footnotes or endnotes).

### Headings
No explicit convention stated. Numbered or Roman numeral headings (per FIELD_SKILL) are not mandated.

### Tables and Figures
Graphics must meet these specifications: minimum 300 dpi, 4.25 inches wide, TIFF or EPS file format. No explicit frequency or function rules stated.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Simultaneous submission to other publications prohibited
- Only original, unpublished papers accepted
- Single-blind refereeing process applies

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Scope and Paper Type
**DELTA - PRACTICE ONLY:** This journal publishes historical and intellectual-historical analysis, not empirical or theoretical economics in the FIELD_SKILL sense. The canonical empirical or theory-paper structures described in FIELD_SKILL (Introduction → Data/Model → Estimation/Analysis → Results → Robustness → Conclusion) do not apply. Instead, papers follow narrative-analytical structures typical of history of ideas: archival research, textual interpretation, genealogy of concepts, biography of thinkers, and historical contextualization of economic doctrines.

### Citation Style
**DELTA - ENFORCED:** Chicago Manual of Style (footnotes/endnotes) replaces FIELD_SKILL's author-year parenthetical. This is a field-wide convention difference between economics (Harvard/author-year) and history (Chicago/notes). The field baseline does not cover this; this journal enforces it explicitly.

### Structure and Argument Architecture
**DELTA - RELAXATION:** FIELD_SKILL's near-universal sections (Introduction with explicit research question, standalone Methods, quantitative Results section, Robustness checks) are not applicable to the intellectual-historical register of this journal. Introduction, Literature, Narrative/Thematic sections, and Conclusion suffice. Fontaine 2024 exemplifies: the paper examines how economists and sociologists converged on a definition of commitment (1956–1969). Structure is organized by disciplinary perspective and chronology, not by identification strategy or table sequence.

### Voice and Register
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. First-person plural ("we") remains standard even at ABS 2.

### Statistical Reporting
**DELTA - RELAXATION:** FIELD_SKILL's expectation of quantitative results reporting, regression tables, and statistical significance testing does not apply. Papers in this journal rarely report primary statistical results. Historical and philological evidence (quotations, archival documents, published texts) replaces tables of coefficients.

### Citation Density by Section
**DELTA - PRACTICE ONLY:** FIELD_SKILL predicts high citation density in introduction and results sections. This journal maintains high citation density throughout but in service of historical argument (evidence of intellectual genealogy, attribution of ideas, support for doctrinal claims) rather than positioning within a current empirical literature. Citations anchor claims to primary sources and prior historiography equally.

### Hedging and Epistemic Caution
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Claims are defended through archival evidence, textual analysis, and transparent handling of competing interpretations, not through verbal hedging.

### Quoted Material
**DELTA - PRACTICE ONLY:** Direct quotation from primary and secondary sources is common and expected. FIELD_SKILL notes that direct quotation is "extremely rare" in economics; this journal reverses that norm in service of historical authentication and argumentative precision. Quotations from original texts (economists' published work, correspondence, archived materials) are standard.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- **Sentence and paragraph style:** Long, complex sentences with high technical vocabulary density (FIELD DEFAULT)
- **First-person plural voice:** "We" as the near-universal subject in active voice (FIELD DEFAULT)
- **Formality register:** High formality, no contractions or colloquialisms (FIELD DEFAULT)
- **Conclusions:** Short relative to introduction; synthesizes main findings rather than summarizing (FIELD DEFAULT)
- **Novelty and positioning:** Explicit statement of contributions and positioning relative to prior historiographical work (FIELD DEFAULT)

---

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Expecting FIELD_SKILL's empirical structure:** Do not apply Introduction → Data → Methods → Results → Robustness → Conclusion architecture. Organize sections around historical argument, not statistical design.
2. **Using author-year citations:** Chicago footnote/endnote style is required, not ABS economics author-year. A paper citing as "(Smith 2020)" instead of superscript footnotes will face reformat requests.
3. **Avoiding direct quotation:** Contrary to FIELD_SKILL norm, quotation from primary sources is expected and strengthens historical argument. Paraphrasing alone without substantive quotation may weaken authenticity.
4. **Assuming quantitative evidence is primary:** Econometric results, data tables, and statistical reporting are not the evidentiary currency of this field. Archival evidence, textual close-reading, and philological precision are.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Research in the Sociology of Organizations

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** ORG STUD
- **Publisher:** Emerald Publishing Limited
- **Scope:** An edited book series (not a standard rolling-submission journal) publishing theoretical and empirical work that advances understanding of organizations and organizing. Each volume is thematically focused around a specific empirical phenomenon (e.g., digital transformation, entrepreneurship, organizational wrongdoing) or theoretical conversation (e.g., institutional logics, actors and agency), compiled by guest volume editors under series editor Michael Lounsbury.
- **Guidelines URL:** No single standing "author guidelines" page for RSO specifically; contributor requirements are set per-volume by guest editors' individual calls for papers, layered on Emerald's general book-series author guidelines (https://www.emeraldgrouppublishing.com/sites/default/files/2019-11/author_guidelines.pdf — 403 on direct fetch, content recovered via search cache) and https://www.emeraldgrouppublishing.com/book-series/research-sociology-organizations (403 on direct fetch, content recovered via search cache).
- **Field baseline:** organisation-studies-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 13 chapter titles/abstracts across 4 volumes (Vol. 90 "Sociological Thinking in Contemporary Organizational Scholarship," Vol. 92 "Necessity Entrepreneurship," Vol. 83 "Digital Transformation and Institutional Theory," Vol. 84/85 "Organizational Wrongdoing"); full text inaccessible (paywalled/CAPTCHA-gated), assessed via publisher chapter-listing pages, volume blurbs, and chapter-abstract snippets.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Submission is by invitation/selection into a themed volume, not open rolling submission to a standing journal. Many calls use a two-stage process: an extended abstract (~1,000 words) is submitted first; a subset of authors is then invited to submit a full chapter. Chapter order per Emerald's general book-series guidelines: Title page (title, authors, affiliations) → Running head (max 65 characters) → Abstract → Main text → Acknowledgements (if applicable) → Appendix (if applicable) → References → Figures → Tables.

### Length
Full-chapter length varies by volume call but a representative recent call specified 8,000–10,000 words including references, tables, figures, and endnotes. Treat this as indicative rather than fixed — always confirm against the specific volume's call for papers, since guest editors set limits per volume.

### Abstract
250 words maximum, unstructured (no Purpose/Methodology/Findings sub-headings) — this is Emerald's book-series abstract format, distinct from the structured abstract format Emerald uses for its standalone journals. Up to six keywords required.

### Citations
APA 6th Edition (not 7th). Note the specific et al. convention: two-author references cite both names at every citation; three-to-five-author references cite all authors at first citation, then surname of first author + "et al." thereafter; six-or-more-author references use first author + "et al." from the first citation onward.

### Headings
No explicit deviation found from FIELD_SKILL beyond the general Emerald chapter-order requirement above (Title page → Running head → Abstract → Main text...).

### Tables and Figures
Appendix/figure/table numbering follows Emerald's series-wide convention: multiple appendices are labeled Appendix A, Appendix B, etc.; figures/tables/equations within an appendix are numbered A1, A2, A3 (or A.1, A.2 for equations), even with only one appendix.

### Explicit Prohibitions
None found beyond standard Emerald copyright/originality requirements (copyright held by Emerald Publishing Limited).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
RELAXATION/PRACTICE ONLY: Because RSO is an edited, themed-volume series rather than a standing journal, chapters are far more heterogeneous in article type than FIELD_SKILL's empirical-article template. Volumes routinely mix substantive empirical chapters with explicitly conceptual, review, or theory-synthesis chapters (e.g., "Organizational Sociology and Organization Studies: Past, Present, and Future"; "Why Organization Sociologists Should Refer to Tarde and Simmel More Often"). A chapter need not follow the Introduction → Methods → Findings → Discussion empirical template at all if it is framed as a theoretical or field-review contribution — this is normal for this venue, not a deviation to flag in review, and is a genuine relaxation of the FIELD_SKILL empirical-article expectation given the ABS 3 tier and the venue's book-chapter format.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
PRACTICE ONLY: Chapter titles frequently use a colon-delimited "broad frame: specific case/mechanism" construction more liberally than is typical of empirical-journal titling (e.g., "Organizations as Carriers of Status and Class Dynamics: A Historical Ethnography of the Emergence of Bordeaux's Cork Aristocracy"), consistent with the venue's tolerance for longer, more discursive, review-oriented pieces. Not a stated rule, observed across multiple sampled volumes.

## Common Pitfalls
- Treating RSO as a standard journal with open rolling submission: it is not. Contributions require identifying an active volume call from the current guest editors and following that specific call's process (often extended-abstract-first).
- Using Emerald's structured journal-abstract format (Purpose/Methodology/Findings headings): RSO book-series abstracts are unstructured, 250 words max.
- Forcing a strict empirical Methods/Findings structure onto a conceptual or review chapter: many RSO chapters are legitimately theory-synthesis pieces without a standard empirical results section.
- Using APA 7th edition conventions: Emerald's book-series guidelines specify APA 6th edition, which has different et al. thresholds than APA 7th.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

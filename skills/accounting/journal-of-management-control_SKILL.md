# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Management Control

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ACCOUNT
- **Publisher:** Springer Nature
- **Scope:** International journal on formal, information-based management routines and procedures. Covers management control systems, planning systems (strategic and operational), management accounting, and the roles of management accountants in companies and nonprofit organizations.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://link.springer.com/journal/187/submission-guidelines
- **Field baseline:** account-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 5-7 recent articles examined via abstracts and structural information (full texts paywalled); supplemented with journal scope documents and Springer formatting guidance

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Decimal heading system with no more than three levels (e.g., 1., 1.1, 1.1.1). Section structure follows a general empirical pattern but is flexible depending on research method: case studies, literature reviews, meta-analyses, and analytical papers are explicitly welcomed alongside hypothesis-driven empirical studies. The canonical structure (introduction, methods, results, discussion) is NOT mandated for all submission types.

### Length
No explicit overall word limit stated in publicly accessible guidelines. Abstract is the primary stated length constraint.

### Abstract
Structured, 150–250 words. Should not contain undefined abbreviations or unspecified references. Keywords (4–6) appear as a separate field following the abstract.

### Citations
Author-date format throughout. In-text citations: "(Author Year)" or "Author (Year)". Multiple citations separated by semicolons. References list follows the journal's house style (author-date with full bibliographic details).

### Headings
Decimal system: 1. Introduction, 1.1 Subheading, 1.1.1 Further subdivision. Avoid nested levels beyond 1.1.1. No ALL CAPS headings; use sentence case or title case per journal house style (observed in published examples to favor title case).

### Tables and Figures
Standard academic presentation. Tables should include clear labels, variable definitions in notes, and explicit reporting of statistical choices (e.g., standard-error clustering, test statistics). Figures and tables may appear in-text or at end of manuscript depending on journal format version.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Abbreviations must be defined at first mention.
- Footnotes must not consist solely of a reference citation; they provide supplementary information only.
- Footnotes should not contain figures or tables.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Methodological Scope and Structure
**Delta: RELAXATION.** FIELD_SKILL baseline reflects ABS 4* quantitative/archival tradition with hypothesis-testing structure as the canonical form. Journal of Management Control explicitly welcomes empirical AND analytical papers, literature reviews, meta-analyses, and case studies. Section structure is therefore more flexible: hypothesis-testing articles may follow the FIELD_SKILL pattern, but case studies, qualitative papers, and literature reviews are explicitly encouraged and do not follow the hypothesis-driven structure. This represents a RELAXATION of the field baseline's narrow focus on quantitative methods and hypothesis testing.

### Research Method Acceptance
**Delta: RELAXATION.** FIELD_SKILL does not emphasize diversity of methods beyond the archival-experimental quantitative mainstream. Journal of Management Control's explicit scope welcomes archival, case, experimental, field, survey, and analytical modelling equally. This reflects a broader methodological tent than the field baseline.

### Citation Density and Theoretical Grounding
**Delta: PRACTICE ONLY (potential).** While FIELD_SKILL emphasizes high citation density in introduction and hypothesis development, management control research (this journal's focus) often integrates organizational theory, institutional theory, and management science. Preliminary evidence suggests citation patterns may vary more by theoretical tradition (e.g., institutional theory-grounded papers may cite more extensively in theoretical framing; archival papers may cite more sparsely in methods). No enforcement identified; this is PRACTICE ONLY variation.

## Dimensions With No Delta

The following dimensions follow FIELD_SKILL norms without identified deviation:

- **Voice and person:** First-person plural ("we") is standard; active voice dominates; high formality maintained. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Sentence and paragraph style:** Complex sentences, dense paragraphs, technical vocabulary. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Statistical reporting and hedging:** Results are stated with precision; mechanistic interpretations are hedged ("suggest," "indicate," "appear to"); causal language reserved for well-identified studies. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Discussion and conclusion:** Merged section is common; contributions are restated; limitations acknowledged explicitly. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Argumentation patterns:** Introduction follows recognizable CARS model (establish significance, state gap, describe study, enumerate results, state contributions). FIELD DEFAULT.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Assuming hypothesis-testing structure is required.** This is a lower-tier (ABS 2) journal that explicitly welcomes case studies, literature reviews, and qualitative analyses. Do not force a hypothesis-testing frame onto case study or interpretive work if it does not fit. Structure your paper to match your method.

2. **Overstating causal claims in archival or observational studies.** The journal publishes diverse methodologies; reviewers will scrutinize causal language closely. Reserve "causes" or "influences" for designs with plausible identification; use "is associated with" or "predicts" for observational data without exogenous variation.

3. **Treating this as a pure financial accounting journal.** This is a MANAGEMENT ACCOUNTING and CONTROL journal. Scope is operational and strategic management control systems, planning systems, and management accounting roles—not financial reporting, auditing, or taxation. Ensure your research question and contribution are clearly framed in that domain.

4. **Neglecting supplementary materials.** The journal encourages authors to share supplementary materials (coding sheets, questionnaires, raw data) to support transparency and future research. Consider including these even if not required; it signals methodological rigor.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

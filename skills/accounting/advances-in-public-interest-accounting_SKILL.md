# Series Writing Skill: Advances in Public Interest Accounting

## Series Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Type:** Book series (edited volumes), not a journal
- **Field:** ACCOUNT
- **Publisher:** Emerald Group Publishing
- **ISSN:** 1041-7060
- **Scope:** Forum for critically appraising and transforming conventional accounting theory, practice, teaching, and research. Examines accounting's social role, accountability, sustainability, and ethical dimensions in public interest contexts.
- **Series Editor:** Dr. Jorge Romero (Towson University)
- **Indexing:** Scopus, Web of Science
- **Recent volumes:** Volume 22 "Resistance and Accountability" (2020)
- **Guidelines URL:** Not publicly available; rely on Emerald Group Publishing general book series guidelines
- **Field baseline:** account-field-writing-style
- **Chapters spot-checked:** Volume 22 (2020) table of contents; 8 chapters across empirical, qualitative, and theoretical traditions

## Hard Constraints (Publisher Guidelines via Emerald)
> These apply to all chapters submitted to this series and override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure and Order of Material
1. Title with author affiliations
2. Running head (up to 65 characters)
3. Abstract (required; see below)
4. Keywords (required; see below)
5. Main text with numbered or formatted headings
6. Acknowledgments (if applicable)
7. Appendices (if applicable, lettered A, B, etc.)
8. References (in-text citations in APA format)

### Length
- Approximately 7,000–10,000 words per chapter, inclusive of abstract, main text, and references
- No page limit specified, but formatted page length will be proportional to word count

### Abstract
- Maximum 250 words
- Single paragraph, unstructured format
- Must concisely state the chapter's research question or focus, approach, and key contributions
- No subsections (e.g., no "Background," "Methods," "Results" subheadings within the abstract)

### Keywords
- Exactly up to 6 keywords
- Must encapsulate the principal topics covered in the chapter
- No standardized format specified; presented as a comma-separated list

### Citations and References
- **Format:** Author-date in-text citations, e.g., (Author Year) or Author (Year)
- **Reference list style:** APA 6th Edition (increasingly moving toward APA 7th Edition)
- **Reference list placement:** At the end of the chapter (not per journal as in some accounting venues)
- **Multiple citations:** Separated by semicolons, typically in alphabetical or chronological order
- **Direct quotations:** Must include page numbers in in-text citation

### Headings and Hierarchy
- **Level 1 headings:** Bold format, initial caps (not all caps)
- **Level 2 headings:** Medium italics, initial caps
- **Level 3 and beyond:** Not explicitly specified; follow cascading style
- **Numbering:** No mandatory numbering system specified; Emerald series varies in chapter numbering format

### Tables and Figures
- Placement: At the end of the chapter or following the reference list, not embedded in text
- Table/figure numbering: In appendices, use A1, A2, A3 format
- If multiple appendices: Name as Appendix A, Appendix B; equations within Appendix A named A.1, A.2, etc.
- Must include clear titles, source attribution, and any explanatory notes

### Explicit Prohibitions
- No direct quotations from scholarly literature (standard field norm—paraphrase and cite instead)
- No supplementary online materials unless explicitly requested; all essential content in chapter
- Permissions required: Must obtain written permission for any copyrighted text, illustrations, or figures
- Copyright: Authors must ensure work is original; Emerald requires non-exclusive rights (print and electronic, worldwide, English language)

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structure: Flexible Across Research Traditions
FIELD_SKILL describes a canonical structure suited to ABS 4*/4 empirical papers: Introduction → Hypothesis Development → Methods → Results → Discussion & Conclusion. Advances in Public Interest Accounting accommodates multiple research traditions:
- **Empirical quantitative chapters** (e.g., "Management Entrenchment and Stock Option Backdating") follow near-canonical structure (Introduction, conceptual framing, methods/data, regression results, discussion).
- **Qualitative chapters** (e.g., "Women Accountants in Academic and Research Spaces in Colombia") adopt case-study/fieldwork structure: Introduction, research context, methodology, findings organized thematically, discussion.
- **Interpretive and critical chapters** (in keeping with the series' public interest tradition) may use extended theoretical framing, thematic analysis, and reflective discussion without hypothesis testing.

**Delta classification:** FIELD DEFAULT. FIELD_SKILL already notes AOS qualitative variation; this series extends that range toward explicitly critical and social-justice-oriented work.

### Voice and Reflexivity
FIELD_SKILL emphasizes first-person plural ("we") and clinical formality. Advances in Public Interest Accounting chapters show more heterogeneous voice:
- Empirical chapters use canonical "we find," "we test" conventions (matching FIELD_SKILL).
- Qualitative and critical chapters (particularly those addressing "public interest" themes explicitly) employ more reflexive language, sometimes acknowledging the author's positionality, values, or theoretical commitments. E.g., a chapter framing accounting as "violence" or examining gender and power explicitly situates itself ethically.
- Still formal, but less uniform clinical tone; more varied sentence rhythm and paragraph length in interpretive work.

**Delta classification:** PRACTICE ONLY. Not mandated by guidelines but observable in Volume 22. The series' intellectual mission (critically appraising conventional practice) permits and sometimes encourages a more engaged, ethically explicit voice.

### Scope and Audience
FIELD_SKILL assumes an audience of specialist accounting researchers familiar with econometric methods, archival design, and top-tier theory. Advances in Public Interest Accounting, as an ABS 1 series, broadens scope:
- Chapters address practitioners, policy makers, and educators as well as researchers.
- Some chapters engage non-quantitative evidence (ethnographic, archival, interview-based).
- Technical density is lower on average; explanatory prose is more accessible.
- Implicit assumption that readers may not be specialist accountants but are concerned with accounting's social and ethical role.

**Delta classification:** FIELD DEFAULT. FIELD_SKILL already notes that AOS qualitative work serves a broader audience; this series institutionalizes that scope.

### Hedging: Mission-Driven Claims
FIELD_SKILL calibrates hedging to identification strength. Advances in Public Interest Accounting chapters add a layer of ethical or social-justice framing that sometimes warrants declarative language even without full quantitative identification. E.g., chapters on gender, inequality, or accountability in development may state normative positions ("accounting perpetuates…," "we must address…") alongside empirical claims.

**Delta classification:** PRACTICE ONLY. Not explicit in guidelines; observed in critical chapters in Volume 22.

### Robustness and Sensitivity
FIELD_SKILL expects 3–5 robustness checks as a field norm for archival papers. Advances in Public Interest Accounting chapters (especially qualitative and theoretical) do not uniformly include formal robustness testing. Sensitivity analysis is chapter-dependent, not a series-wide expectation.

**Delta classification:** FIELD DEFAULT. The series accommodates diverse methodologies; robustness is not uniformly expected outside empirical-archival chapters.

## Series-Specific Conventions

### Edited Volume Context
Unlike solo-authored papers, Advances in Public Interest Accounting chapters appear in edited volumes. This affects:
- **Cross-chapter consistency:** Chapters vary in structure, length (within 7,000–10,000 words), and methodology. Editors expect some stylistic diversity.
- **Engagement with volume theme:** Chapters should connect to the volume's organizing theme (e.g., "Resistance and Accountability") without need for explicit cross-citation between chapters.
- **Editor communication:** Contact the volume editor (via Emerald commissioning editor) early for guidance on chapter positioning and scope.

### Public Interest Framing
The series' distinctive mission is to engage accounting's role in serving the public interest. Chapters should explicitly:
- Frame the research question in relation to public interest, accountability, equity, or social responsibility (not required to be normative, but the framing matters).
- Acknowledge stakeholders beyond shareholders and auditors (e.g., workers, communities, policymakers).
- Discuss implications for practice or policy, not just theory.

**Delta classification:** GUIDELINE ONLY. Chapters submitted to the series should address public interest dimensions; observed in all recent chapters but not formalized in publisher guidelines.

### Volume-Specific Call for Chapters
Each volume of the series operates as an edited collection with a specific theme (e.g., "Resistance and Accountability," "Parables, Myths and Risks"). Prospective authors typically respond to a call for chapters specifying:
- Desired chapter topics and scope
- Expected word count and deadline
- Volume theme and editor contact

Authors should obtain and follow the specific call for the volume to which they are submitting.

## Common Pitfalls

### 1. Exceeding Word Limit
The 7,000–10,000 word cap is strictly enforced. Chapters should draft tightly; avoid lengthy literature reviews or exhaustive case descriptions that can crowd out analysis. Prioritize novel insights over comprehensive coverage.

### 2. Insufficient Abstract Clarity
The 250-word abstract is the reader's first contact. Abstracts in some chapters (particularly qualitative) bury the contribution. State upfront: What is the research question? What is novel about the approach or finding? Avoid abstracts that read like introduction teasers.

### 3. Mismatch Between Chapter and Volume Theme
Chapters that are technically sound but fail to engage the volume's organizing theme or the series' public interest mission may face editorial rejection. Connect your work explicitly to the call for chapters.

### 4. Over-Reliance on Tables in Qualitative Chapters
The field norm (FIELD_SKILL) is tables as the primary vehicle for results. Qualitative chapters in this series should privilege extended analytical prose and interview/archival quotations. Avoid force-fitting qualitative data into tabular format.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

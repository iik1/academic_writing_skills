# Journal Writing Skill: Australian Journal of Agricultural and Resource Economics

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Wiley-Blackwell
- **Scope:** Empirical and theoretical research in agricultural and resource economics with emphasis on quantitative, novel, and policy-relevant work
- **Guidelines URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/page/journal/14678489/homepage/guide.htm (paywalled; public excerpts via secondary sources)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6+ articles from Vol. 70 (2026) and Vol. 69 (2025) via IDEAS/RePEc and journal TOCs

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
- Standard empirical or theory structure required (follows FIELD_SKILL norms for ECON field)
- Required sections: Abstract, Keywords, main body (Introduction, Methods/Theory, Results, Conclusion), References
- No explicit mandate for section numbering convention; journal offers "Free Format" submission option for streamlined review
- Anonymity enforced: all author names and Acknowledgements must be removed from main text file before submission

### Length
- Word/page limits not publicly available in accessible author guidelines; specification withheld by paywalled Wiley page
- Estimated from field and ABS tier norms: likely 8,000–12,000 words for full articles
- CONSTRAINT: Manuscript must be submitted anonymously to pass initial review

### Abstract
- **Hard constraint:** 200 words maximum
- Must state purpose, basic procedures, main findings, and principal conclusions
- **Prohibition:** No abbreviations, no references in abstract
- **Format:** Unstructured (single paragraph assumed; no explicit four-part structure required)

### Citations
- **Format:** Parenthetical author-date system (e.g., "Smith (2000)" or "(Smith 2000)")
- Multiple citations: "(Smith 1999, Jones 2005)" or "(Smith *et al.* 2015)"
- **In-text examples (from Paperpile AJARE style):**
  - Single: Lowe (2005)
  - Multiple: (Lowe 2005, Cheng and Xie 2015)
  - 3+ authors: (Schroeder *et al.* 2000)

### Reference List Format
- **Journal articles:** Author(s) (Year). Article title. *Journal Name* Volume, page range.
  - Example: Dixson D.L., Abrego D. and Hay M.E. (2014). Reef ecology. *Science* 345, 892–897.
- **Books:** Author(s) (Year). *Book Title*. Publisher, Location.
- **Book chapters:** Author(s) (Year). Chapter title. In: Editor(s) *Book Title*, pp. range. Publisher, Location.
- **Web sources:** Include URL and access date.
- **Requirement:** References may be submitted in any style *if consistent throughout*; author-date is standard and strongly preferred

### Headings
- No explicit numbering mandate in public guidelines
- Field baseline (FIELD_SKILL) permits Arabic or Roman numerals; AJARE likely permits both or unnumbered
- Follows standard capitalization: title case for section headers

### Tables and Figures
- Tables must be self-contained and complement (not duplicate) text content
- Figures and tables may be submitted as separate files or embedded in main document
- No explicit resolution or formatting requirements published in accessible guidelines
- Captions should clarify variable definitions and data source

### Keywords
- **Hard constraint:** Exactly 5 keywords or terms
- Must be in alphabetical order
- Used for indexing purposes

### Explicit Prohibitions
- No abbreviations in abstract
- No references in abstract
- No author names in main text (anonymity requirement for double-blind review)
- Do not duplicate information between text and tables

### JEL Codes
- **Compulsory:** All submissions must include JEL classification codes
- Required as part of submission metadata
- Ensures proper indexing in economic literature databases

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates.

### Citation Flexibility
**RELAXATION:** FIELD_SKILL states "author-year (parenthetical or in-text) is near-universal" and footnote citations are not the norm. AJARE permits *any consistent reference style*, relaxing this to be more flexible than field norm. This is a journal-specific accommodation, likely reflecting Wiley's multi-discipline publishing practices. In practice, author-date remains standard at this journal.

### Free Format Submission
**PRACTICE ONLY:** AJARE has introduced a "Free Format" submission option alongside traditional formatted submissions. This appears to be a Wiley-wide initiative to reduce revision burden, not a requirement. Eliminates some formatting prescriptions (margins, spacing, font) but content structure and anonymity requirements remain.

### Anonymity Requirement Emphasis
**ENFORCED:** FIELD_SKILL does not address anonymity, as ABS 4*/4 journals vary. AJARE explicitly requires removal of all author identifiers from the main text file for double-blind review. This is a hard constraint not mentioned in the field baseline.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Forgetting anonymity:** Leaving author names, affiliation clues, or self-citations in the main text; these will cause desk rejection before review.
2. **Abstract violations:** Including abbreviations, references, or exceeding 200 words will delay manuscript processing.
3. **Missing JEL codes:** Omitting required JEL classification on submission form will cause rejection of manuscript as incomplete.
4. **Inconsistent citation style:** Mixing author-date with numbered or other formats violates the "consistency" requirement; stick to one format throughout.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

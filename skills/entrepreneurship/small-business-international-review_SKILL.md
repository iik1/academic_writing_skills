# Journal Writing Skill: Small Business International Review

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ENT-SBM (Entrepreneurship and Small Business Management)
- **Publisher:** Spanish Accounting and Business Administration Association (AECA) and Universidad Politecnica de Cartagena (UPCT)
- **Scope:** Open-access journal publishing empirical, conceptual, and systematic review research on small and medium enterprises (SMEs), entrepreneurship, business support, and sustainability. Diamond Open Access (no APCs).
- **Guidelines URL:** https://sbir.upct.es/index.php/sbir/about/authorguidelines
- **Field baseline:** entrepreneurship-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 5 (all from Vol. 10 No. 1, 2026)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Required section order: Introduction → Theory and Research Hypotheses → Research Methodology → Results → Discussion and Conclusions → References. Maximum three hierarchical levels, numbered as: 1, 1.1, 1.1.1. Each heading on separate line. Include Practical Implications as near-universal subsection in Discussion.

### Length
Maximum 8,000 words including references and supplementary material.

### Abstract
150–200 words. Must state research objective, main results, and primary conclusions. Must not contain undefined abbreviations or unspecified references.

### Citations
APA 7th edition. All cited works must appear in references; all references must be cited in text. Include DOI for all references that have one. Recent sources preferred (ideally last 5 years). Avoid excessive self-citations. Excessive quotations prohibited; use sparingly and only when essential.

### Headings
Maximum three hierarchical levels (1, 1.1, 1.1.1), numbered. Each heading on separate line.

### Tables and Figures
Numbered consecutively with Arabic numerals. Figures in .jpg or .png format, minimum 300 DPI. Tables in editable text format. All sources must be correctly cited.

### Formatting
2.5 cm margins all sides. Double-spaced text including headings. First-line paragraph indent: 1 cm. Font options: Times New Roman 12pt, Arial 11pt, or Georgia 11pt. Page numbers on each page. Use footnotes (not endnotes).

### Language
English only. Consistent spelling (either American or British English) throughout. Avoid colloquialisms and idioms. Gender-neutral language required. Grammatical errors may result in rejection. Abbreviations must be defined at first mention.

### Explicit Prohibitions
No previously published material. No simultaneous submission to other publishers. No undefined abbreviations. Use footnotes rather than endnotes.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates from the field baseline.

### Voice and Register: ENFORCED DELTA — Passive Voice Dominates

**FIELD_SKILL specifies:** First-person plural voice ("we test," "we argue," "we find") throughout, including in theory and hypothesis sections, alongside active voice as the default. Passive constructions appear selectively in methods reporting.

**Small Business International Review practice:** Passive and third-person constructions dominate throughout the manuscript, including abstract, introduction, results, and discussion. Examples from articles: "data were collected," "the results indicate," "the study demonstrates," "multivariate statistical techniques were applied." First-person plural appears only selectively and sparingly in methodology ("we used," "we examine"), functioning as an optional rather than default voice. All five spot-checked articles employ predominantly passive/third-person voice.

**Classification:** ENFORCED (stated implicitly in guidelines emphasizing "professional distance" and formal tone; confirmed across all sampled articles as consistent practice).

### Tone and Professionalism

**FIELD_SKILL specifies:** Register is formal but not stiff; rhetorical framing devices (explicitly naming "contributions," numbering them, using signposting phrases) are expected.

**Small Business International Review:** More formal and restrained than field baseline. Signposting phrases ("we contribute in three ways") are notably absent or muted. Contributions are typically embedded in discussion sections rather than enumerated explicitly. This more formal, less rhetorical approach aligns with passive-voice dominance and creates greater authorial distance.

**Classification:** PRACTICE ONLY (observed consistently but not explicitly mandated in guidelines; may reflect broader journal culture of formality).

### Hedging and Epistemic Caution

**FIELD_SKILL specifies:** Hedging concentrated in specific locations—causal claims softened in Discussion and Limitations, while Results sections report findings in "direct, unhedged declarative language."

**Small Business International Review:** Hedging language ("suggests," "may," "appears," "indicates," "plausible") appears throughout results and discussion sections, not concentrated. Results sections present coefficients and significance levels without unhedged claims; interpretation is deferred and qualified. This reflects more conservative scholarly practice and cross-sectional design limitations acknowledged transparently.

**Classification:** PRACTICE ONLY (observed but not prescribed; may reflect methodological caution in SME research context where experimental designs are rare).

### Practical Implications Orientation

**FIELD_SKILL specifies:** "Practical/Managerial Implications" subsection is near-universal, reflecting field's applied orientation.

**Small Business International Review:** Practical implications are present but often embedded as "research scope," "governance implications," or "practitioner considerations" rather than a distinct labeled subsection. Articles emphasize policy and practitioner-facing recommendations within discussion sections. This reflects the journal's explicit focus on SME support and business practice.

**Classification:** PRACTICE ONLY (observed but structure varies; guidelines require Discussion and Conclusions but do not mandate a distinct Practical Implications subsection).

### Null Findings and Transparency

**FIELD_SKILL:** Silent on null findings reporting.

**Small Business International Review:** Null findings (e.g., non-significant hypotheses) receive substantive discussion rather than dismissal or downplaying. One sampled article explicitly states "only one hypothesis received support (H3)" and discusses non-significant results with reference to prior literature precedent. This reflects mature scientific transparency.

**Classification:** PRACTICE ONLY (observed across multiple articles; reflects journal ethos of research integrity).

### Contextual and Institutional Grounding

**FIELD_SKILL:** Silent on context specificity.

**Small Business International Review:** Articles integrate institutional and regional context deeply—references to specific country entrepreneurship ecosystems (Armenia, Kenya, Mexico), policy environments, and local SME challenges ground findings in concrete realities rather than abstract universals. This reflects the journal's focus on geographically and economically diverse SME contexts.

**Classification:** PRACTICE ONLY (observed but not mandated; appears to reflect editorial preference for contextually situated research).

## Common Pitfalls for This Journal
1. **Overuse of first-person plural voice:** Articles using "we argue," "we propose," "we find" throughout may clash with journal's formal, passive-voice norm. Use first-person selectively and only in methods/procedural sections.
2. **Failing to address limitations and null findings transparently:** Downplaying non-significant results or underexamined limitations signals weakness to this journal's editors. Discuss what didn't work and why it matters.
3. **Neglecting context and geographic grounding:** Generic, universalizing claims without reference to specific SME contexts, regions, or policy environments reduce relevance to the journal's scope. Ground findings in concrete institutional and economic realities.
4. **Excessive emphasis on theoretical novelty over practical applicability:** This ABS 1 journal prioritizes actionable insights for SME support practitioners and policymakers. Ensure discussion sections emphasize governance, implementation, and practitioner-facing implications, not solely theoretical contribution.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

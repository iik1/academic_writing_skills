# Journal Writing Skill: Review of Industrial Organization

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Springer Nature
- **Scope:** Peer-reviewed journal covering all aspects of industrial organization—competition and monopoly in their many forms and their effects on efficiency, innovation, and social conditions. Heavy focus on antitrust policy, regulation, mergers, and competition economics.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://link.springer.com/journal/11151/submission-guidelines (requires authentication)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 13 (2025–2026 articles from IDEAS/RePEc)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

### Abstract
- **Required length:** 150–250 words
- **Format:** Unstructured (single paragraph)
- **Content:** Standard economic abstract (motivation, approach, main findings)

### AI Tool Usage
- **Disclosure required:** Yes, in Methods section (or suitable alternative section if no Methods exists)
- **Exception:** AI-assisted copy editing for readability/style does not require disclosure

### Manuscript Structure
Follows standard disciplinary norms (FIELD_SKILL). Empirical papers: Introduction, Background/Data, Methods, Results, Conclusion. Theory papers: Introduction, Literature, Model/Setup, Analysis, Results, Conclusion. Both types commonly include appendices for proofs, robustness checks, and data description.

### Length
No explicit word or page limits identified in publicly accessible guidelines. Articles in the journal range from ~15 to ~55 pages, suggesting flexible page limits with no strict upper bound.

### Citations
Author-year format (parenthetical or in-text). Follows FIELD_SKILL norms.

### Tables and Figures
No special requirements identified beyond FIELD_SKILL norms. Both are common; figures illustrate key variation, model predictions, and merger effects.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Undefined abbreviations in abstract
- Unspecified references in abstract
- Undeclared LLM use in manuscript preparation (except copy-editing)

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Thematic Concentration and Scope
**Delta classification:** PRACTICE ONLY / JOURNAL-SPECIFIC FOCUS

The journal exhibits a narrower, more specialized thematic focus than the general ECON field baseline. While FIELD_SKILL applies to economics broadly (macro, micro, development, labor, etc.), Review of Industrial Organization concentrates on industrial organization narrowly: mergers, market structure, competition policy, antitrust, and regulation. Articles on healthcare mergers, airline pricing, platform economics, and tech acquisitions dominate recent issues. This is not a style delta but a scope expectation: drafts for this journal should address industrial organization questions, not general economic phenomena.

### Policy-Oriented Editorial Voice
**Delta classification:** PRACTICE ONLY / LOWER-TIER ABS 2 RELAXATION

The journal publishes regular special issues and editor-authored "Antitrust and Regulatory Update" editorials (e.g., "Recent Developments at the FTC," "Recent Developments at the CMA") that adopt a more informal, policy-review tone than typical FIELD_SKILL practice. These pieces are explicitly non-research articles and serve as journal infrastructure. Regular research papers, however, maintain the FIELD_SKILL voice (formal, first-person plural, active voice dominant). As an ABS 2 journal, it tolerates more variation in article types than ABS 4*/4 outlets.

### Diversity of Article Types
**Delta classification:** PRACTICE ONLY / LOWER-TIER ABS 2

The journal explicitly publishes not only empirical and theoretical papers but also case studies, regulatory assessments, and literature reviews. The sample includes merger case analyses (Facebook-Giphy, Medicare Advantage), policy reviews, and special-issue editorials. FIELD_SKILL emphasizes disciplined empirical or theory papers; Review of Industrial Organization is more eclectic. Writers should be prepared for heterogeneous article formats to be accepted, though research papers themselves maintain FIELD_SKILL structure.

### Merger and Competition Focus
**Delta classification:** PRACTICE ONLY / FIELD-SPECIFIC EMPHASIS

Nearly all articles examined address mergers, market concentration, pricing, entry, or competitive behavior. This represents the journal's editorial identity, not a style delta: papers use standard FIELD_SKILL methods and voice but are selected for their relevance to competition and antitrust. Expect reviewers to evaluate identification and causal claims stringently (consistent with FIELD_SKILL), but in the context of competition policy questions.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- Voice and person: FIELD DEFAULT (first-person plural dominant; "we" used in empirical and theory papers alike)
- Sentence and paragraph structure: FIELD DEFAULT (medium-to-long sentences, high paragraph density, technical vocabulary)
- Argumentation and rhetorical moves: FIELD DEFAULT (explicit research question, announcement of results in introduction, clear contributions, conclusion restates findings without new results)
- Methods and identification: FIELD DEFAULT (mixed tense, sufficient procedural detail, identification strategy required for empirical work, robustness checks expected)
- Results reporting: FIELD DEFAULT (tables primary vehicle, prose highlights magnitudes and significance, integration of interpretation)
- Citation format and density: FIELD DEFAULT (author-year, high in introduction, medium in methods, low in results)
- Hedging and epistemic caution: FIELD DEFAULT (clear claims preferred; hedging only for external validity, mechanism uncertainty, and data limitations)

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Unclear connection to competition/antitrust questions.** The journal will reject papers with sound methods and results if the industrial organization relevance is not made explicit and compelling. Frame empirical findings around market structure, pricing, entry, or merger effects.

2. **Inadequate identification of competitive mechanisms.** As an ABS 2 outlet in a specialized subfield, the journal's reviewers are expert in IO economics. Causal claims require explicit attention to identification strategy (instrumental variables, natural experiments, quasi-random shocks). Reduced-form evidence without clear mechanisms is less persuasive.

3. **Undisclosed LLM use.** Springer requires disclosure of LLM use in the Methods section. Failure to disclose can result in desk rejection or retraction. Copy-editing exceptions are explicitly permitted, but substantive use (analysis, writing) must be documented.

4. **Abstract with undefined terms.** The guidelines explicitly prohibit undefined abbreviations or unspecified references in the abstract. Check that all acronyms and citations in the 150–250-word abstract are either expanded inline or removed.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

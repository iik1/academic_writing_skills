# Journal Writing Skill: Human Resource Development Review

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** HRM&EMP
- **Publisher:** SAGE Publications
- **Scope:** International quarterly journal devoted exclusively to theory development, foundations of HRM, theory-building methods, and integrative literature reviews. Does NOT publish empirical field-based studies (quantitative, qualitative, or mixed methods).
- **Guidelines URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/author-instructions/hrd
- **Field baseline:** hrm-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 4 (Ekuma 2024 AI and Automation review; Lee & Lee 2024 People Analytics review; Pandey & Mahesh 2025 People-Centric HRM review; journal scope documentation)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
ENFORCED: The journal accepts only non-empirical work: theory and conceptual articles, integrative literature reviews, theory-building research methods, and foundations of HRM. Field-based studies (empirical quantitative, qualitative, or mixed-method research) are explicitly not considered for publication. Structure varies by article type (integrative review structure differs from theory article structure). No single mandatory section order applies; however, heading levels must be clear and consistent.

### Length
ENFORCED: Initial submissions must not exceed 40 pages total, including abstract, keywords, references, tables, and figures. This is a hard page limit.

### Abstract
ENFORCED: Unstructured abstract of approximately 150 words. Concisely states the purpose of the work, major findings or contributions, and conclusions.

### Keywords
ENFORCED: Minimum 4–5 keywords, listed after the abstract. Keywords should be as specific as possible to the research topic.

### Citations
ENFORCED: APA reference style. Every in-text citation must have a corresponding reference, and vice versa. Corresponding citations must have identical spelling and year. Authors should update references to preprints when peer-reviewed versions are available; citations to preprints are discouraged.

### Formatting
ENFORCED: Preferred format is Word. Text and abstract must be double-spaced. Submission via Sage Track (manuscript management system).

### Headings
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.

### Tables and Figures
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.

### Explicit Prohibitions
ENFORCED: Field-based empirical studies (data-driven quantitative, qualitative, or mixed-method research conducted on populations, organizations, or individuals) are not accepted. The journal is not an outlet for empirical research findings or empirical methodological contributions.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Article Type and Scope (CRITICAL DELTA)
ENFORCED: This journal publishes ONLY non-empirical, theory-focused work. The FIELD_SKILL baseline assumes a diversity of article types in HRM&EMP journals, including empirical studies (quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-method), conceptual work, and reviews. HRDR accepts none of the empirical categories. Acceptable work includes: syntheses of existing theory, new substantive theories, exploratory conceptual models, taxonomies and typologies as foundations for theory, treatises in formal theory construction, papers on the history of theory, critique of theory with alternative propositions, metatheory, and integrative literature reviews with strong theoretical implications. Authors writing for HRDR must ensure their manuscript is positioned as a theoretical or conceptual contribution, not a report of primary or secondary data collection from a field or organizational sample.

### Argument Structure
PRACTICE ONLY / LOW-PREVALENCE: FIELD_SKILL describes a CARS-like structure (context, establishing gap, stating solution) typical of empirical and many conceptual papers. HRDR articles exhibit more variation depending on type. Integrative review articles (the most common type observed) typically establish a conceptual problem or gap in theory rather than an empirical gap, conduct systematic or narrative review of existing scholarship, synthesize across sources, and conclude with implications for future theory-building. Theory articles may forgo a formal "gap" and instead present a new model or framework explicitly. No single rhetorical structure is enforced; journals' flexibility on structure reflects the diversity of non-empirical article types.

### Methods Section (MAJOR DELTA)
FIELD DEFAULT (N/A for this journal): FIELD_SKILL details empirical methods sections (sample construction, measures, estimation strategy, robustness checks, ethical approval). HRDR does not publish empirical research, so methods sections as described in FIELD_SKILL do not apply. Integrative reviews and theory articles may include a "Approach" or "Process" section describing the systematic or narrative review method (e.g., databases searched, inclusion/exclusion criteria, synthesis approach), but this is not the same as empirical methods.

### Results/Findings Presentation (MAJOR DELTA)
FIELD DEFAULT (N/A for this journal): FIELD_SKILL describes quantitative results sections with tables and prose narration, and qualitative findings sections organized thematically with quotations. HRDR articles do not report empirical findings. Integrative reviews present synthesized themes, patterns, and conceptual insights from reviewed literature rather than new data. Theory articles present new theoretical propositions, models, or frameworks. No results tables or participant quotations are typical.

### Voice and Register
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Formal, impersonal register with first-person plural permitted. The field baseline's observation that HRM&EMP tolerates more narrative and interpretive prose than economics applies equally to HRDR.

### Citation Density
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Highest in introduction and theory/literature sections, lower in discussion/implications. The baseline practice of citing seminal frameworks (e.g., AMO, social exchange theory) alongside recent empirical work applies to HRDR's integrative reviews and theoretical discussions.

### Hedging and Epistemic Caution
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Moderate to high hedging, especially when generalizing from theoretical synthesis or making prescriptive claims. Causal language is cautious, consistent with the field baseline.

### Practical Implications
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Practical implications for HR practitioners, organizations, and policymakers are expected and treated as a required component, reflecting the field's applied orientation.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Submitting empirical research.** The most frequent rejection reason is likely submission of quantitative, qualitative, or mixed-method studies with original data collection. HRDR does not publish field-based empirical work. Reframe as a conceptual, integrative, or theoretical contribution, or submit to an outlet that accepts empirical HRM research (e.g., Human Resource Management Journal, British Journal of Industrial Relations).

2. **Misunderstanding article type expectations.** Authors may submit work labeled as "literature review" that is primarily a critical essay on one theory or a narrative summary of the field without systematic method or strong synthesis. HRDR's integrative reviews expect explicit methodological clarity (databases searched, selection criteria, synthesis approach) or, alternatively, a conceptual framing that is clearly novel or critical, not merely descriptive.

3. **Overstating empirical claims.** When drawing on existing empirical literature to build theory, avoid framing findings from other studies as if they are novel findings of your own. Make clear the distinction between synthesized prior empirical evidence and your theoretical contribution.

4. **Exceeding 40 pages.** Initial submissions over 40 pages (including all elements) may be desk-rejected without review. Conceptual and integrative work can be dense; prioritize clarity and concision. Remove redundancy between sections.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of the History of Economic Thought

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** BUS HIST & ECON HIST
- **Publisher:** Cambridge University Press
- **Scope:** Peer-reviewed journal publishing original research in the history of economic thought, accepting submissions of PhD chapters, dissertation excerpts, and journal articles from broader research projects.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-history-of-economic-thought/information/author-instructions/preparing-your-materials
- **Field baseline:** business-economic-history-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 12 articles across volumes 46–48 (2024–2026) including "Frank H. Knight's Last Lecture" (Emmett), "Post-Keynesian Economics as Defense Mechanism" (Düppe), "Adam Smith on the Wretched Spirit of Monopoly" (Donoghue), "Before NBER: Warren Nutter's Soviet Research" (Kuehn), "Hazel Kyrk and the Rise of Empirical Research in Interwar America" (Alberti & Asso), "Family Planning and Poverty" (Bankovsky et al.), "Economic Knowledge as Public Knowledge" (Välikangas), and others accessed via Cambridge Core and open repositories.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Section headings must be numbered with **Roman numerals** (I, II, III, etc.) and presented in capitalized form. Subsections, if used, may employ decimal notation but are less common in practice. Lead with an opening section establishing the historical puzzle and argument; a sources-and-methods section is expected; a conclusion is required. Article structure typically follows the narrative-analytical pattern outlined in FIELD_SKILL (introduction, historiographical engagement, sources/methods, substantive analysis organized chronologically or thematically, conclusion).

### Length
Manuscripts must be **less than 11,000 words**. This word count includes footnotes, tables, figures, references, and any appendices. There is no explicit lower limit stated, but articles typically range from 8,000–10,500 words in published form.

### Abstract
An **abstract of 150 words or fewer** is required. The abstract is unstructured (not divided into background/methods/results sections) and should provide a compact summary of the puzzle, argument, and contribution. Place the abstract on the first page below the author name and affiliation.

### Citations
**In-text citations must be in round parentheses** with page numbers preceded by "p." (single page) or "pp." (multiple pages). Example: "(Author Year, p. 45)". Full reference list follows alphabetically by author surname. This is parenthetical author-date style, not Chicago notes-bibliography. Paraphrase when engaging secondary literature; reserve direct quotation for primary sources and for capturing distinctive phrasing. Double quotation marks for all quoted material in the text; single quotation marks for quotes within quotes.

### Headings
Section headings are numbered with **Roman numerals and capitalized**. Do not use italics or underlining for headings. Use italics (not underlining) for book titles and emphasis throughout the text.

### Formatting
- **Line spacing:** 1.5 throughout, including indented quotations, footnotes, and references
- **Font:** Times New Roman 12 pt, black, with ragged right margin
- **Emphasis:** Italics only; no underlining

### Footnotes vs. Endnotes
Notes must be **footnotes, not endnotes**, embedded in the article with superscript numbering in the text.

### Tables and Figures
All figures and tables must be **numbered consecutively with Arabic numerals** and cited in the manuscript text. Submit figures as electronic files (1200 dpi TIFF/EPS for diagrams; 300 dpi+ TIFF for photographs). Tables and figures count toward the 11,000-word limit.

### Numbers and Spelling
- Numbers from one to ninety-nine should be spelled out in text unless they denote percentages or currency units. Example: "thirty-five books" but "35% of inventory" and "£35 per unit".
- **American English spelling throughout** (color, not colour; maximize, not maximise).

### Explicit Prohibitions
The journal considers for publication **original research not published elsewhere in any language**. Simultaneous submission to multiple journals is not permitted. The journal does accept PhD chapters and dissertation excerpts if they are unpublished and represent original work.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Citation Format: Parenthetical In-Text Style
**ENFORCED** (stated in guidelines, confirmed in sample articles). FIELD_SKILL does not specify whether citations should use author-date parenthetical style or Chicago notes-bibliography. JHET requires parenthetical author-date with round brackets and p./pp. prefix on page numbers. This is a departure from the Chicago style (which permits both notes-bibliography and author-date, but notes are more traditional in historical work); JHET's use of parenthetical style is notable for a humanities-inflected field. Example observed in Düppe (2025): citations within paragraphs appear as "(Author Year, p. X)" embedded in flowing text.

### Heading Format: Roman Numerals
**ENFORCED** (stated explicitly). FIELD_SKILL does not mandate Roman numeral headings; it notes that structure is "narrative-analytical" but does not specify numbering convention. JHET requires Roman numerals, which is visible in the Düppe article (sections I, II, etc.). This is a formalization not required by the field baseline.

### Word Limit: 11,000 Strict Ceiling
**ENFORCED**. FIELD_SKILL places no word limit. JHET's 11,000-word cap (including all text, notes, and references) is a hard constraint and notably tighter than many ABS 2 journals in the field, reflecting an emphasis on concision over the longer archival narratives sometimes seen in top-tier business history journals.

### Abstract Word Limit and Unstructured Format
**ENFORCED**. The 150-word cap and unstructured (non-segmented) abstract format are stated explicitly. FIELD_SKILL describes abstracts as "compact, non-technical" but does not specify a word ceiling. JHET's 150-word limit is typical but worth noting for authors accustomed to longer abstracts.

### Footnotes Required, Not Endnotes
**ENFORCED**. FIELD_SKILL does not address this choice. JHET explicitly requires footnotes embedded with superscript numbering, not endnotes. This is standard in historical writing but worth flagging for authors unfamiliar with the convention.

### Formatting Precision: 1.5 Spacing, Times New Roman 12pt
**ENFORCED**. FIELD_SKILL does not specify font or spacing. These are technical publisher requirements and should be observed to avoid desk-desk rejections before peer review.

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Voice and register:** FIELD DEFAULT. Abstracts employ third-person academic voice; article bodies vary between impersonal framing ("this article argues") and first-person plural ("we show"), consistent with FIELD_SKILL norms and without journal-specific deviation.
- **Sentence and paragraph style:** FIELD DEFAULT. Medium to long sentences with subordinate historical qualification and substantial, evidence-rich paragraphs are consistent with FIELD_SKILL and observed in sample articles (Emmett, Düppe, etc.).
- **Argumentation and historiographical framing:** FIELD DEFAULT. Articles examined (particularly Bankovsky et al., Välikangas, Donoghue) employ CARS-like patterns ("recover," "reassess," "challenge"), counter-narratives, and historiographical engagement entirely consistent with FIELD_SKILL expectations.
- **Methods and sources description:** FIELD DEFAULT. FIELD_SKILL expects procedural detail on archives, datasets, and source limitations; this is observed in all sampled articles and no journal-specific deviation exists.
- **Discussion and conclusion norms:** FIELD DEFAULT. Conclusions restate contributions, situate findings in historiographical debates, and gesture toward broader implications, exactly as FIELD_SKILL describes.
- **Hedging and epistemic caution:** FIELD DEFAULT. Sample articles (Emmett, Kuehn, Duppé) employ moderate hedging ("appears," "likely," "suggests") balanced with confident thesis statements, consistent with FIELD_SKILL norms.
- **Direct quotation and primary-source evidence:** FIELD DEFAULT. Use of primary-source quotations to substantiate interpretive claims is observed and valued, consistent with FIELD_SKILL expectations.
- **International and interdisciplinary engagement:** FIELD DEFAULT. Articles sampled draw on business history, economic history, philosophy (Bréban & Jaffro), and social science literatures as appropriate, consistent with FIELD_SKILL.

## Common Pitfalls Specific to This Journal

1. **Exceeding 11,000 words:** Authors accustomed to longer journal articles or monograph chapters often overrun this ceiling. Every element (introduction, notes, tables, appendices, references) counts. Audit word counts early and plan cuts by section; the limit is firm.

2. **Using endnotes instead of footnotes:** Submissions with endnotes may trigger a desk rejection or request for revision. Ensure all notes are embedded as footnotes with superscript numbering in the text.

3. **Employing Chicago notes-bibliography citations:** Historians often default to full citations in notes (Author, Title, Publication). JHET requires parenthetical author-date in the text with a reference list. If you draft in notes-bibliography style, convert early to avoid last-minute reformatting.

4. **Non-Roman numeral section headings:** Submitting with decimal headings (1.1, 2.3) or plain text headings without numerals will require revision. Use I, II, III, etc., and capitalize them.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

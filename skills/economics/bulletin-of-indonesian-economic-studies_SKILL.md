# Journal Writing Skill: Bulletin of Indonesian Economic Studies

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Taylor & Francis (Routledge), on behalf of the ANU Indonesia Project
- **Scope:** The leading journal on Indonesia's economy and society. Publishes original papers and notes in economics and political economy, plus related analyses in law, public policy, political science, demography, geography, environment, education and health — but every submission must have Indonesia as its primary empirical/topical focus, regardless of disciplinary lens. Published three times yearly since 1965.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.tandfonline.com/journals/cbie20/about-this-journal (full "Instructions for Authors" page returned 403/paywalled; scope and policy details drawn from the About page and the ANU Indonesia Project's journal page)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 13 (titles/abstracts across Vol 25 No 2 (1989) through Vol 62 No 1 (2026), including full abstracts for "Indonesia's Fiscal Policy in the Aftermath of the Pandemic" (60(1), 2024) and "Does Tax Simplification Motivate Small Businesses to be More Compliant?" (60(2), 2024)). Full text was inaccessible (tandfonline.com returned 403 for all direct article/instructions URLs); sample relies on RePEc/IDEAS-hosted abstracts, EconPapers listings, and publisher table-of-contents/about pages. This is a lower-tier (ABS 1) journal with a thin public access chain, so this smaller good-faith sample is used per instructions.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Every issue opens with a specially commissioned "Survey of Recent Developments" — a standing, non-standard-article feature that records and analyses recent Indonesian economic trends, policy changes, and events. This is distinct from the regular research-article slate (3-4 specialist articles per issue) and from notes/book reviews. Authors writing a "Survey" contribution are writing to a different brief than a standard empirical/theory paper; this skill's guidance otherwise concerns the regular research-article slate.

### Length
No general word limit stated in publicly accessible guidance. One documented exception: COVID-19/policy-focused submissions were solicited as brief pieces (5,000-7,000 words) during the pandemic period — RELAXATION relative to standard full-length empirical papers, not necessarily a standing rule.

### Abstract
Abstracts are published in both English and Bahasa Indonesia online (ENFORCED — confirmed as standing publisher policy, though the Indonesian translation is a publication-layer requirement, not something authors need to draft in Indonesian themselves per visible guidance).

### Citations
No delta found; guidelines page did not specify a style beyond what's typical of Taylor & Francis economics journals. Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (author-year).

### Headings
No delta found in accessible guidance.

### Tables and Figures
No delta found in accessible guidance.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Submissions lacking a clear Indonesia focus are out of scope regardless of disciplinary quality — this is a scope constraint stated directly in the journal's About page, not merely a soft preference.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
The journal explicitly aims for articles "reasonably accessible to non-specialists," reflecting a diverse readership spanning academia, government, business, and the broader public — not just professional economists. This is broader than the FIELD_SKILL assumption that "readers are assumed to be trained economists." Titles and abstracts in the sample (e.g. "Prabowonomics: Can Indonesia Really Grow at 8%?", "Ideas in Action: LPEM, the Think Tank that did not Just Think") skew toward plain-language, policy-relevant framing rather than technical economics phrasing, more so than would be typical at ABS 4/4* outlets. GUIDELINE-supported and PRACTICE-confirmed: treat jargon reduction and accessible framing of motivation/contribution as a real journal norm, not just field default.

### Structural Deviations
The standing "Survey of Recent Developments" feature (see Hard Constraints > Structure) has no analogue in FIELD_SKILL, which only covers standard empirical/theory paper structures. ENFORCED for that specific article type.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Sampled abstracts (fiscal policy retrospective, tax-compliance RDD study) integrate policy recommendations directly into the abstract's closing lines ("areas needing improvement," specific policy reform suggestions) more explicitly and more prominently than is typical at ABS 4*/4 outlets, where abstracts more often stop at reporting the finding and its magnitude. PRACTICE ONLY, observed in 2 of 2 full abstracts sampled — worth flagging as a plausible pattern but not confirmed against guidelines text.

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting a paper with only tangential Indonesia relevance (e.g., a general Southeast Asia or cross-country comparative paper without Indonesia-specific analysis) — explicitly out of scope per the journal's stated focus.
- Writing at the technical density typical of ABS 4*/4 outlets without moderating jargon; this journal's stated non-specialist readership rewards clearer, more accessible framing of motivation and contribution than the field default.
- Treating "Survey of Recent Developments" as a normal research-article submission type — it is a separately commissioned, differently structured feature, not an open submission category for standard articles.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

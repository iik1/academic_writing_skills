# Journal Writing Skill: Management and Labour Studies

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** HRM&EMP
- **Publisher:** SAGE Publications, India (in association with XLRI School of Business and Human Resources, Jamshedpur)
- **Scope:** Quarterly refereed journal publishing research on management, labour, and related subjects in psychology, economics, history, international relations, media, mass communication, and sociology.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/author-instructions/mls
- **Field baseline:** hrm-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 5 (via IDEAS/RePEc listing and SAGE journal pages; full-text access limited by paywall)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Articles should be organized according to standard IMRaD structure. The journal accepts four article types:
- **Original research** (empirical or conceptual)
- **Review articles** (thematic or content analysis of existing research)
- **Commentary** (novel insights grounded in theory and supported by empirical data)
- **Practitioner perspective** (applied focus)

### Length
**Word limit: 8,000 words maximum (strict).** This includes title, abstract, keywords, all body text, and references. This is significantly stricter than field baseline conventions. Plan accordingly.

### Abstract
- **Unstructured format** (not explicitly specified as structured or unstructured in guidelines; inferred unstructured from scope)
- **Word count: 150–200 words**
- **Keywords: 4–6** (not separated from abstract guidance)

### Citations
- **Style: APA author-date method** (e.g., Smith 2020; Jones & Brown 2021)
- Every in-text citation must have a corresponding reference list entry; corresponding citations must have identical spelling and year
- Use APA reference formatting per SAGE APA guidelines

### Headings
- Maintain hierarchical structure without skipping levels (H1 → H2 → H3)
- Standard SAGE guidance applies (see field baseline for general conventions)

### Tables and Figures
- Create as text in Word, never as images
- Always include header rows/columns
- Add number and title
- Avoid using color to convey meaning alone
- Simplify complexity where possible
- Provide raw data accompanying visualizations if created outside Word; use TIFF, JPEG, or EPS format, minimum 300 dpi

### Font and Formatting
- **Font:** Times New Roman
- **Software:** Microsoft Word (submissions in hard copy and soft copy, identical)
- **Quotes:** Single quotes throughout; double quotes only within single quotes

### Language
- **Spelling: British English mandatory** (e.g., 'programme' not 'program', 'labour' not 'labor', 'centre' not 'center')

### Statements and Declarations
- **Required section** with specific subsection headings (Funding, Conflicts of Interest, Author Contributions, Data Availability, Ethics Approval, etc.)
- Must include even if declaring "Not applicable"
- Appears after Acknowledgements on title page

### Anonymization
- **Double-anonymized peer review** (double-blind)
- Author names appear only on cover page
- No author identifying information on manuscript pages

### Submission Format
- Submit in duplicate (hard copy and soft copy, identical)
- Cover page bears title, author names, designations, official addresses, phone/fax, email
- No author name on any other page

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Word Limit Enforcement
**ENFORCED.** This journal enforces an 8,000-word ceiling. FIELD_SKILL does not specify word limits, but typical ABS 4*/4 journals allow 12,000–20,000 words. This journal is significantly more restrictive. This has downstream implications: introductions must be tighter, literature reviews more concise, and results/discussion sections must prioritize key findings over comprehensive coverage. The constraint favours practitioner-oriented framing over extensive theoretical elaboration.

### Citation Style Explicitness
**ENFORCED.** APA author-date style is mandatory and explicit in guidelines. FIELD_SKILL notes "no consensus norm exists" on citation style variation. This journal is explicit and non-negotiable on APA. In-text citations and reference formatting must follow APA conventions precisely.

### Article Types and Scope
**ENFORCED.** The journal explicitly accepts and equally values four article types: original research, review, commentary, and practitioner perspective. FIELD_SKILL assumes a hierarchy of empirical and conceptual work at top-tier outlets. This journal explicitly encourages **practitioner perspective** articles alongside academic research, reflecting its applied and Indian management context orientation. This is a clear signal that papers with strong HR/organizational practitioner implications are valued.

### Statements and Declarations Section
**ENFORCED.** FIELD_SKILL mentions ethics statements as standard back matter. This journal requires a dedicated **Statements and Declarations section** with mandatory subsection headings (even if marked "Not applicable"). This is stricter than field norms and requires explicit planning during manuscript preparation.

### British English Requirement
**ENFORCED.** FIELD_SKILL does not specify spelling conventions (though ABS 4* UK journals favour British spellings). This journal mandates British English throughout. Check spelling carefully: 'programme', 'labour', 'centre', 'organisation', 'recognised', etc.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Word limit overrun.** This journal is strict on 8,000 words. Manuscripts exceeding this are returned for revision before peer review. Plan literature review and results discussion sections with this ceiling in mind. Omit tangential citations and focus on core contributions.

2. **Omitting Statements and Declarations section or failing to include subsection headings.** Even if all declarations are "Not applicable", the section and its headings must appear on the title page. This is non-negotiable for desk-review gatekeeping.

3. **American English spelling.** Reviewers and editors flag 'program', 'labor', 'center', 'recognize' as errors. Use a spellchecker set to British English and proofread carefully.

4. **Non-APA citation formatting.** In-text citations must be (Author Year) format; reference lists must follow APA conventions exactly. Inconsistent or incomplete citations are grounds for return to author.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

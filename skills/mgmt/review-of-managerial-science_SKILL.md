# Journal Writing Skill: Review of Managerial Science

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN
- **Publisher:** Springer Nature
- **Scope:** Forum for high-quality, innovative research in all areas of business administration. Open to various methodologies: analytical modeling, empirical research, experimental work, and methodological reasoning. Covers accounting, auditing, banking, business strategy, corporate governance, entrepreneurship, financial structure and capital markets, health economics, human resources management, information systems, innovation management, insurance, marketing, organization, production and logistics, risk management, and taxation.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://link.springer.com/journal/11846/submission-guidelines (Springer authentication required; guidelines excerpts accessed via public sources)
- **Field baseline:** mgmt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 7 articles from 2024–2025 (mixed empirical, systematic review, and case study)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows standard MGMT templates (see FIELD_SKILL for deductive, inductive, theoretical, and review structures). No explicit manuscript structure deviation from field baseline documented.

### Length
No explicit page or word limits stated in publicly accessible guidelines. This is standard for Springer journals at submission; length is assessed during review. (RELAXATION: Top-tier management journals often impose 10,000–15,000 word targets; RMS does not state this constraint).

### Abstract
**Format:** Unstructured or structured; both appear in published articles.
**Length:** 150–250 words (ENFORCED).
**Content:** Should be informative and self-explanatory.
**Citations:** No citations in abstract (ENFORCED).

### Headings
**System:** Decimal numbering with no more than three levels (ENFORCED, GUIDELINE ONLY: Observed in published articles; field baseline does not mandate decimal headings, so this is a journal-specific requirement).
**Font:** Headings and subheadings bold, with each word capitalized (ENFORCED per guidelines).
**Text formatting:** Body text Times New Roman 12pt, single-line spacing, justified (ENFORCED).

### Citations
**Style:** APA in-text citations (Author, Year) or Author (Year) (FIELD DEFAULT; consistent with MGMT baseline).
**Reference list:** Follows APA format.

### Tables and Figures
Follows FIELD_SKILL conventions. No journal-specific deviations noted in guidelines or sample articles.

### Page Margins
Top: 0.5"; Left: 0.7"; Right: 0.7"; Bottom: 0.5" (ENFORCED).

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Authorship changes (adding/deleting authors, reordering, or changing corresponding author) not accepted after acceptance (ENFORCED).
- Abbreviations must be defined at first mention (ENFORCED).
- Footnotes allowed for additional information or references but should not consist solely of a reference citation (GUIDELINE ONLY).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Article Types and Structure Emphasis
Systematic literature reviews, integrative reviews, and bibliometric analyses are major article types in this journal (observed in 2024–2025 sample: "The burgeoning role of literature review articles in management research," "Enhancing top managers' leadership with artificial intelligence," "Multi-stakeholder marketing: mapping the field"). Field baseline mentions reviews but does not emphasize them as heavily. (PRACTICE ONLY: Field-appropriate but more prominent in RMS).

### Decimal Heading System
Field baseline does not mandate decimal headings; FIELD_SKILL describes generic section naming. Review of Managerial Science explicitly requires decimal numbering with max three levels. (ENFORCED: Journal-specific structural requirement).

### Relaxation of Page/Word Limits
Field baseline notes typical lengths (10,000–15,000 words empirical; 12,000–18,000 qualitative; 8,000–14,000 theoretical; 15,000–25,000 review). RMS does not state explicit limits in public guidelines, suggesting flexibility. (RELAXATION: Lower-tier journal; less prescriptive on length).

### Quantitative Methods Diversity
Sample included advanced econometric techniques (Exponential Random Graph Models, machine learning classification trees) alongside standard regression. Field baseline notes "regression-based models (OLS, logit, probit, fixed-effects panel) are standard." RMS welcomes broader methodological innovation. (PRACTICE ONLY: Field-aligned but slightly more permissive of novel quantitative approaches).

## Dimensions With No Delta
- **Voice and person:** FIELD DEFAULT (First-person plural "we" standard; moderate hedging; direct assertions for methods and findings).
- **Citation density:** FIELD DEFAULT (Introduction and Theory/Background dense; Results/Findings sparse; Discussion engages citations).
- **Argumentation and propositions vs. hypotheses:** FIELD DEFAULT (Empirical papers use hypotheses; theoretical/review papers use propositions or thematic arguments; explicit hypothesis testing or framework development expected).
- **Methods section tense and clarity:** FIELD DEFAULT (Past tense for procedures; present tense for data descriptions; identification of confounds and robustness checks expected).
- **Discussion structure:** FIELD DEFAULT (Summary of findings, theoretical implications, practical relevance, limitations, future research).
- **Prose interpretation of results:** FIELD DEFAULT (Authors interpret key coefficients and revisit hypotheses/questions explicitly; tables are vehicles, not substitutes, for findings).
- **Literature engagement:** FIELD DEFAULT (Critical engagement with prior work; notation of gaps, tensions, and contested claims; footnotes for tangents).
- **Conclusion form:** FIELD DEFAULT (Restatement of contribution; forward-looking agenda or practical implications; no new findings introduced).

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Failing to number headings decimally:** The journal explicitly requires decimal heading numbering (1, 1.1, 1.1.1, etc.). Many authors from other journals omit this. Ensure your structure adheres to this format even if other management journals do not require it.

2. **Exceeding abstract word limit or including citations:** Abstracts must be 150–250 words with no citations. Common mistake: exceeding length or citing references within the abstract.

3. **Authorship changes after acceptance:** Once accepted, reordering authors or adding/removing authors is not permitted. Clarify authorship and order at submission.

4. **Insufficient abstract self-containment:** Abstracts must be informative and stand alone. Avoid vague references to "findings" or "results" without stating what they are.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

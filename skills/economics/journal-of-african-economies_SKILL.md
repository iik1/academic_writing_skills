# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of African Economies

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Oxford University Press
- **Scope:** Peer-reviewed economics journal publishing rigorous empirical and theoretical studies on all economic issues relating to sub-Saharan Africa; published by Oxford University Press in partnership with the Centre for the Study of African Economies (CSAE), University of Oxford.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://academic.oup.com/jae/pages/General_Instructions
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 (from Volumes 34-35, 2025-2026)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows canonical empirical structure. No mandatory section-naming convention stated; section headings may use Roman or Arabic numerals per FIELD_SKILL baseline.

### Length
**Maximum 10,000 words** (excludes abstract, title page, references, endnotes, figures, and tables). Supplementary material not counted toward limit.

### Abstract
- **Maximum 250 words**
- Must be comprehensible without reading full paper
- **Explicit constraint: No citations in abstract**
- Format: Single paragraph synthesizing purpose, methodology, and findings
- Often structured as: problem statement → methodology → quantified results

### Citations
- **Author-year format required** (parenthetical or in-text)
- **All references must be cited in text; all cited references must appear in reference list**
- Must follow Journal of African Economies reference style (author-date, alphabetical order)
- **JEL classification codes required** at submission

### File Formats
Accepted formats: `.doc`, `.tex`, `.rtf`, `.ps` (NOT PDF for manuscript text or tables)

### Headings
No specific naming mandate; standard abbreviations discouraged in headings.

### Tables and Figures
**Figures:**
- Low-resolution submission: `.jpg`, `.tif`, `.gif`, `.eps` (embedded or separate)
- **Publication format: High-resolution `.tif` files required** (1200 dpi for line drawings; 300 dpi for color/halftone)
- **Alt text descriptions required for all images** (accessibility); include "Alt text:" directly under figure legends
- No charge for color figures
- Ensure embedded text is legible

**Tables:**
- **Editable format only** (Word, Excel, PowerPoint)—NOT image or PDF format
- Placed on separate pages with titles and footnotes
- Numbered with consecutive Arabic numerals
- **Formatting restrictions: No vertical lines, colored text, or shading**
- Units in parentheses at column headers

### Explicit Prohibitions
- **Prior publication:** Journal does not accept papers previously published elsewhere, except working papers (if submitted to working paper series before acceptance by JAE; working paper may remain online post-publication)
- **Multiple submissions:** Only one submission per corresponding author under review at any time
- **Integrity screening:** Manuscripts screened for integrity issues including image alteration and "papermill" activity

### Acknowledgements and Declarations
- **Placement:** End of text before references (NOT in footnotes)
- **Structure:** Personal acknowledgements precede institutional/agency acknowledgements
- **Separate sections required:** Distinct Funding and Conflict of Interest sections
- Declarations regarding non-financial support must be explicit

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Passive Voice Frequency
Observed pattern: Articles use passive constructions more frequently than FIELD_SKILL baseline suggests. While FIELD_SKILL describes "active voice is strongly preferred," JAE articles employ passive voice notably in methodology and results sections (e.g., "models are enhanced to encapsulate...," "weather shocks are documented"). This may reflect author preference or subject-matter requirements.

**Classification:** PRACTICE ONLY (observed in 4 of 6 articles; not stated in guidelines as requirement)

### Abstract Structure and Content
JAE abstracts are longer and more structured than typical FIELD_SKILL practice. Rather than prose narrative, abstracts often employ:
- Explicit problem statement opening
- Methodology section (sometimes with specific model acronyms: SVAR, DSGE)
- Numbered or bulleted findings with quantitative specificity
- Example: "Weather shocks account for approximately 10% of GDP; household vulnerability is measured by X"

**Classification:** ENFORCED (explicit in guidelines: max 250 words, must synthesize purpose/methodology/findings; observed in all 6 articles)

### Quantitative Specificity in Results Presentation
All sampled articles present results with precise numerical magnitudes (percentage points, basis points, GDP impacts, consumption increases). This exceeds FIELD_SKILL's expectation of "approximate" language and aligns with its norm that "economic significance is expected alongside statistical significance."

**Classification:** FIELD DEFAULT (FIELD_SKILL baseline already emphasizes quantitative precision; no deviation, merely emphasized in practice)

### Citation Density by Section
Matches FIELD_SKILL pattern: high in introduction, medium in methods, low-to-medium in results, low in conclusion. No delta observed.

### Hedging Language in Abstracts vs. Results
Abstracts employ cautious phrasing ("approximately," "account for," "suggest") more than results sections. Results sections rely on coefficient estimates and confidence intervals without additional verbal hedging. This aligns with FIELD_SKILL principle that "confidence interval carries the uncertainty; additional verbal hedging is redundant."

**Classification:** FIELD DEFAULT

### Mixed Tense Convention
Observed across all articles: past tense for data/estimation procedures, present tense for model implications and theoretical results. Matches FIELD_SKILL exactly.

**Classification:** FIELD DEFAULT

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Voice and person:** Field baseline (first-person plural "we" expected, but passive voice more frequent in practice than FIELD_SKILL suggests—practice-level variation)
- **Sentence and paragraph length:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (medium-to-long sentences, high-density paragraphs in body sections)
- **Argumentation structure (Introduction):** Canonical sequence (motivation → research question → approach → results preview → contributions → roadmap) observed in all articles; matches FIELD_SKILL
- **Positioning relative to prior work:** Required and specific; matches FIELD_SKILL
- **Citation format:** Author-year, no footnote citations; matches FIELD_SKILL
- **Discussion and Conclusion norms:** Conclusions synthesize findings without new results; shorter than introduction; matches FIELD_SKILL
- **Handling disagreement:** Direct and precise; no deviation from FIELD_SKILL
- **Null results handling:** Published when well-powered and question is important; matches FIELD_SKILL
- **Methods sections:** Sufficient procedural detail for replication; mixed-tense convention; matches FIELD_SKILL

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Exceeding word limit:** At 10,000 words (excluding front matter, tables, references), JAE imposes a tighter constraint than many ABS 2 journals. Empirical papers with extensive robustness checks and supplementary analyses risk overrun; prioritize main results in body text and relegate supplementary checks to appendix.

2. **Incorrectly formatted tables and figures:** Submitting tables as images or PDFs causes desk rejection. Tables must be in editable format (Word/Excel/PowerPoint); figures must be submitted low-resolution (.jpg/.tif/.gif/.eps) with publication-ready metadata. Ensure high-resolution files are prepared in advance for acceptance phase.

3. **Citations in abstract:** Explicit guideline violation; many journals permit citations in abstracts, but JAE does not. Strip all parenthetical references from abstract; rely on problem framing and methodological names (e.g., "DSGE model," "RCT") for context instead.

4. **Missing JEL codes or Funding/Conflict of Interest sections:** Desk rejection risk if JEL codes are omitted at submission. Ensure separate Funding and Conflict of Interest sections at end of text, not buried in acknowledgements.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

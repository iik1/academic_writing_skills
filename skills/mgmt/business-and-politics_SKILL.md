# Journal Writing Skill: Business and Politics

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN
- **Publisher:** Cambridge University Press
- **Scope:** Research on the relationship between private firms and public governance institutions; business strategy, regulation, non-market strategy, lobbying, private governance, political economy of firms and industries.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/business-and-politics/information/author-instructions
- **Field baseline:** mgmt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 (2023–2025 issues; full-text abstracts and section headings examined)
- **Open Access:** Gold OA from Volume 27, Issue 1 (January 2025)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

### Structure
No prescribed article template (unlike FIELD_SKILL, which specifies quantitative/qualitative/theoretical templates). Authors have flexibility in section organization and labeling. Structure examples from recent articles: Introduction → Theory → Methods → Results → Discussion (empirical); Introduction → Conceptual Framework → Case Analysis → Discussion (conceptual-empirical hybrid); Introduction → Theoretical Framework → Empirical Analysis → Conclusion (regulatory analysis). Section headings reflect the content logic, not generic labels.

### Length
- **Articles:** Abstract <200 words (strict limit; submitted separately in form field, not in manuscript)
- **Notes:** Abstract <80 words
- **No page limit stated.** Word counts in recent articles range 6,000–10,000 words.
- **Manuscript format:** Single-spaced, 12pt Times New Roman (or equivalent), 1.5-inch margins on all sides, 8.5×11 inch page size, single column, justified left and right
- **No page numbers, headers, or footers permitted**

### Abstract & Keywords
- Unstructured abstract (no problem-method-findings-contribution labels)
- Submitted via form field during submission, not embedded in manuscript
- Must include 3–5 keywords provided separately in submission form
- No citations in abstract

### Citations
**Critical delta from FIELD_SKILL (which uses APA in-text):**

Business and Politics uses **footnote-author-date style** (Chicago Manual of Style, 14th ed.). This is ENFORCED.

- **In-text:** Footnotes numbered sequentially; format is author surname, year, page (e.g., footnote: "Heinrich 2024, 45")
- **Bibliography:** Alphabetical by author surname, then chronological. Full publication details required; include first names where available.
- **Examples:**
  - Book: "Thelen, Kathleen. 2014. Varieties of Liberalization and the New Politics of Social Solidarity."
  - Journal article: "Weber, Steven, & Mehandru, Nikita. 2022. 'Examining Network Effects in International Trade.'"
  - Newspaper: Reference in footnotes only; no bibliography entry

### Headings
Follows standard management article hierarchies (numbered or lettered subheadings) but with flexibility in naming. Headings reflect the substantive argument, not generic labels. Examples from recent articles: "Beyond formal politics: Exploring epistemic business power," "Institutional platform power shapes influence over digital governance," "Demand side: Political heads, top civil servants and political advisers."

### Tables and Figures
- Figures preferred in encapsulated PostScript (.eps) format; high resolution required
- Figures and tables integrated into main manuscript (not appendix) where possible
- Figure captions and table notes must be clear and self-contained
- Alt-text and accessibility descriptions required for all figures and tables
- Data displays (regression tables, figure panels, typologies) are central to empirical articles; clearly labeled and interpreted in text

### Language & Tone
American English spelling and conventions. Minimal capitalization. Formal academic register, direct assertions with appropriate hedging. Phrases like "We argue," "We find," "Our results suggest" are standard. Tone is measured and analytical; authors avoid sensationalism while maintaining critical distance from subject matter.

### Manuscript Preparation
- Title page required (separate from main document); include competing interests declaration for all authors
- Copyedit before submission
- Include ORCID identifiers when available
- Provide alt-text and accessibility descriptions for figures/tables
- Supplementary materials (appendices, data) submitted as separate files
- List all contributing authors with affiliations

### Explicit Prohibition & Declaration Requirements
- **AI tool use declaration:** If generative AI was used for text generation, image generation, or data analysis, authors must declare use in relevant section (acknowledgments for text, methods for analysis, figure captions for visuals). Minimal uses (spell-checking) do not require declaration. This is ENFORCED.
- **Competing interests:** All authors must declare financial, professional, contractual, or personal relationships on title page that could influence the work. ENFORCED.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Citation System: Footnote-Author-Date (vs. APA in-text)
**Classification:** ENFORCED (stated in guidelines, confirmed in all articles examined)

Business and Politics mandates footnote citations rather than APA in-text citations (FIELD_SKILL baseline). Authors provide sequential footnotes with author, year, and page number. Full bibliographic details appear in a closing bibliography ordered alphabetically. This is a structural requirement, not a stylistic preference.

**Example from article:**
Observed in Heinrich (2024) and Kausche & Weiss (2024): Footnotes reference foundational work (Lindblom, Lukes, Bachrach & Baratz) and contemporary scholarship with precision. Footnotes also contain substantive clarifications and secondary arguments, not just citations.

### Article Structure Flexibility
**Classification:** RELAXATION (journal relaxes FIELD_SKILL template prescription)

Unlike FIELD_SKILL, which specifies rigid templates (quantitative deductive, qualitative inductive, theoretical, integrative review), Business and Politics permits flexible structuring organized by substantive logic. Recent articles show varied architectures:

- Heinrich (2024): Introduction → Business Power Scholarship → Epistemic Business Power Framework → Empirical Case (BlackRock) → Conclusion
- Kausche & Weiss (2024): Introduction → RIT Model Framework → Institutional/Ideational Power Theory → Empirical Case (DSA) → Conclusion
- Eun et al. (2023): Introduction → Literature → Hypotheses → Methods → Empirical Results → Discussion

**Impact:** This flexibility permits authors to emphasize theory, data, or case logic without forcing conformity to predetermined templates. Subsection naming reflects the argument, not category (e.g., "Why platforms prevail" vs. "Results").

### Empirical Methods Reporting
Follows FIELD_SKILL norms for logistic regression, fixed-effects models, robustness checks, and qualitative methods (process-tracing, discourse analysis, content analysis). No deviations observed.

### Voice and Hedging
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm: first-person plural ("we"), moderate hedging ("suggest," "may," "appear"), direct assertions for established findings.

### Abstraction Level and Citation Density
Theory sections are citation-dense and engage named frameworks (exchange theory, institutional theory, power dynamics) consistent with FIELD_SKILL. Introduction and theory sections heavily cited; results/findings sections cite sparingly; discussion engages citations to situate findings. Consistent with FIELD_SKILL.

### AI Tool Use Declaration
**Classification:** ENFORCED (new guideline not in FIELD_SKILL baseline)

Business and Politics now requires declaration of generative AI use for text, images, or data analysis. Authors must note use in acknowledgments (text generation), methods (analysis), or figure captions (visuals). This is a recent addition to author guidelines reflecting evolving journal practice.

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Prose style and register:** FIELD DEFAULT. Formal, academic, direct assertions with measured hedging.
- **First-person plural ("we"):** FIELD DEFAULT across co-authored work.
- **Quantitative methods rigor (regression, fixed effects, robustness):** FIELD DEFAULT. Consistent with management journal expectations.
- **Qualitative methods (process-tracing, content analysis, interviews):** FIELD DEFAULT. Adequately described with data collection and analytic procedures.
- **Discussion structure (summary → theoretical implications → practical implications → limitations → future directions):** FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Hypotheses vs. propositions:** FIELD DEFAULT. Empirical articles use numbered hypotheses (H1, H2…); theoretical pieces use propositions (P1, P2…).
- **Literature engagement (critical, not just approbatory):** FIELD DEFAULT. Authors note where prior work is limited or contested.
- **Conclusion:** FIELD DEFAULT. Restates core contribution; does not introduce new findings.

## Common Pitfalls Specific to Business and Politics

1. **Using APA in-text citations instead of footnotes.** The journal mandates Chicago-style footnotes (author, year, page). Authors trained in APA (the FIELD_SKILL baseline) frequently submit with (Author, Year) parentheticals, which must be converted to footnotes. This is the single most common formatting rejection reason.

2. **Embedding abstract in the manuscript.** Business and Politics requires abstract submission via a separate form field; including it in the main document triggers formatting errors in the submission system. Abstracts must be <200 words (articles) or <80 words (notes).

3. **Failing to declare AI tool use when applicable.** With the shift to Gold Open Access in 2025, the journal has strengthened its AI transparency requirement. Even minor uses (ChatGPT for phrasing, Claude for structure editing, or machine learning for text analysis) must be declared in acknowledgments or methods.

4. **Omitting competing interests declaration.** All authors must include a competing interests statement on the title page. Omission delays review and can result in desk rejection.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

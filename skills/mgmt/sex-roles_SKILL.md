# Journal Writing Skill: Sex Roles

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN
- **Publisher:** Springer Nature
- **Scope:** Global, multidisciplinary journal with a feminist perspective publishing original empirical research, theoretical papers, and conceptual reviews on gender, with emphasis on how gender organizes people's lives and their worlds.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://link.springer.com/journal/11199/submission-guidelines (requires authentication; public details extracted from indexed sources)
- **Field baseline:** mgmt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 5 article guideline/advisory papers; access constraints limited empirical sample

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Abstract
- **Length:** 150–250 words (ENFORCED)
- **Format:** Unstructured (no explicit subheadings required)
- **Content:** Must highlight key aspects of the study: sample size and composition, methods (e.g., survey, online recruitment), analyses, main results, and implications
- **Prohibitions:** No citations, no abbreviations without definition (ENFORCED)

### Citation Style
- **Format:** APA in-text citations (parenthetical name-year format)
- **In-text:** (Author Year) or Author (Year) when part of sentence; multiple citations ordered alphabetically or chronologically: (Jones, 2010; Smith & Lee, 2015)
- **Et al.:** Used from first citation for three or more authors
- **Bibliography:** Follows APA reference list conventions (Author(s). (Year). Article title. *Journal*, *Vol*(Issue), pages.)
- (ENFORCED per Springer standard)

### Structure (Quantitative Empirical)
- **Order:** Abstract → Introduction → Methods → Results → Discussion [→ Conclusion]
- **Standard sections:** Introduction must establish territory and niche; Methods must detail procedures; Results organized around hypotheses; Discussion interprets findings
- **(ENFORCED per Springer/field standard)**

### Methods (Quantitative-Specific)
- **Assumption testing:** Must report whether standard assumptions for planned analyses were satisfied (e.g., univariate/multivariate normality for structural equation models, sample size adequacy)
- **Detail level:** Authors must provide sufficient detail for statistically proficient readers to replicate findings
- **(ENFORCED per journal guideline)**

### Results (Quantitative-Specific)
- **Organization:** Results should follow the order of hypotheses presented in the Introduction, using the same organizing principles
- **Hypothesis confirmation format:** Hypothesis explicitly revisited with direction stated and coefficient + p-value reported (e.g., "H1 predicted X; the coefficient is positive and significant (β = 0.32, p < .01), supporting H1")
- **(ENFORCED per journal guideline)**

### Qualitative Methods and Results
- **Coding requirement:** Formal coding of data is required (not optional)
- **Results format:** Report counts and percentages of material within each coding category
- **Statistical test:** Results from qualitative data typically analyzed using chi-square tests
- **Mixed methods:** If combining quantitative and qualitative data, authors must articulate how both datasets and findings are placed in conversation with one another
- **(ENFORCED per journal guideline)**

### Headings and Structure
- Follows standard Springer format (FIELD DEFAULT)

### Tables and Figures
- Follows standard Springer format (FIELD DEFAULT)

### Explicit Prohibitions
- No citations in abstract (ENFORCED)
- Undefined abbreviations in abstract prohibited (ENFORCED)
- Authors should not add/delete authors during revision without explicit justification; changes not permitted after acceptance (ENFORCED)

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Feminist Perspective Requirement
**Delta: ENFORCED**
The journal is explicitly "written from a feminist perspective." This is not a stylistic suggestion but an editorial stance. Authors should ensure their framing, interpretation, and choice of findings emphasize gender as an organizing principle in social life, not as a peripheral variable. This is a higher-level positioning delta than FIELD_SKILL assumes (which remains neutral on ideological framing).

### Quantitative Research Assumption-Testing Emphasis
**Delta: ENFORCED**
Beyond the general methods rigor assumed in FIELD_SKILL, Sex Roles *requires* explicit reporting of whether statistical assumptions were met *before* reporting results. This includes univariate/multivariate normality checks, sample size adequacy for planned models, etc. Omitting this is a submission-readiness issue specific to Sex Roles. FIELD_SKILL does not emphasize assumption-checking prominence.

### Hypothesis-to-Results Correspondence Requirement
**Delta: ENFORCED**
Sex Roles mandates that Results section organization *exactly mirror* the order and phrasing of hypotheses introduced earlier. FIELD_SKILL expects hypothesis-driven structure but allows reorganization for clarity; Sex Roles enforces parallel structure more strictly. Authors must state the hypothesis, then report the coefficient and p-value that confirms or disconfirms it in the same passage.

### Qualitative Coding and Chi-Square Requirement
**Delta: ENFORCED**
Qualitative work in Sex Roles must employ formal coding with numerical tallies (counts/percentages) and chi-square analysis. This is more structured than typical qualitative work in FIELD_SKILL, which allows grounded theory and thematic narrative without quantification. Purely narrative qualitative findings are less favored; they must be counted and tested.

### Mixed Methods Articulation
**Delta: ENFORCED (for mixed-methods papers)**
Sex Roles explicitly requires mixed-methods authors to articulate how qualitative and quantitative datasets and findings are integrated. FIELD_SKILL assumes integrative review capability but does not mandate explicit statement of integration strategy. Sex Roles treats this as a hard requirement, not a nice-to-have.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Omitting assumption-testing summary in Methods/Results:** Failure to report whether normality, homogeneity of variance, or sample size assumptions were met is a readiness issue. Always include a brief statement of assumption checks before results.
2. **Reorganizing hypothesis order in Results:** Do not rearrange the order of hypotheses in the Results section for narrative flow; Sex Roles enforces parallel order between Introduction and Results.
3. **Ignoring feminist framing requirement:** Submitting purely technical papers without engaging gender as an organizing lens (or as a central conceptual frame) risks rejection or desk-reject; feminist perspective is editorial, not optional.
4. **Qualitative without counts:** If qualitative, do not report themes only narratively; Sex Roles expects counts, percentages, and chi-square tests even for grounded theory work.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

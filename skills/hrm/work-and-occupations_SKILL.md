# Journal Writing Skill: Work and Occupations

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** HRM&EMP
- **Publisher:** SAGE
- **Scope:** Sociological and social-scientific research on work, occupations,
  employment, and labor, published quarterly, drawing on an international,
  interdisciplinary readership (sociology, labor economics, management,
  industrial relations, psychology).
- **Guidelines URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/author-instructions/wox
  (page is JS-rendered; content below drawn from indexed guideline text and
  corroborated against a full-text article)
- **Field baseline:** hrm-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 10 (Kowalski 2025 "Unfulfilled: Collaborative

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Research articles require an introduction, well-reported methodology,
results, and a discussion of results in the context of the published
literature. In practice (confirmed by full-text spot-check), quantitative
papers integrate the literature review into a continuous, thematically
subheaded run of sections leading into the research question, rather than
isolating a standalone "Literature Review" section — theoretical framing
and hypotheses/research questions are woven together, with research
questions often posed as open questions rather than as a numbered
hypothesis list.

### Length
No explicit word/page limit found in accessible guideline text.

### Abstract
Unstructured abstract of 150-250 words placed between title and body,
stating purpose, major findings, and conclusions. Confirmed in full text
(single unstructured paragraph, ~200 words).

### Keywords
Guideline text found is inconsistent (one source says "4-5," another
"minimum of 3"); the spot-checked full-text article used 9 keywords,
suggesting practice runs higher than any single guideline figure implies.
Treat the guideline minimum loosely and default to more keywords (7-9)
rather than fewer if unsure.

### Citations
APA style (guideline text specifies the 6th edition; confirmed author-date
in-text citation format in full-text spot-check, e.g., "(Kellogg et al.,
2020; Wood, 2021)").

### Headings
No distinctive heading conventions beyond standard APA-style section
headings; thematic subheadings are used freely within the literature/theory
build-up (e.g., "Implications for Job Quality," "The Situation in
Warehousing") rather than being confined to a single "Literature Review"
heading.

### Tables and Figures
No journal-specific rules found beyond standard SAGE/APA table and figure
formatting. Sole-authored empirical article in the spot-check used
numbered tables for descriptive statistics and regression results, and
figures for predicted-value/marginal-effects plots — standard practice,
no deviation from field norms.

### Explicit Prohibitions
No simultaneous submission to another journal; manuscript must not have
been published elsewhere in substantially similar form.

### Other
A ~50-word biographical paragraph per author (current affiliation, research
interests, recent publications) is required as back matter. Confirmed in
full text: a short (~35-word) author biography follows the references.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
Sole-authored quantitative papers use first-person singular ("I") heavily
and consistently throughout, including in methods and findings framing
("I address these questions using data on..."; "I compare job
characteristics and worker attitudes..."). This goes beyond FIELD_SKILL's
description of first-person plural being "increasingly common": WOX
tolerates confident first-person voice (singular or plural depending on
authorship) as a default authorial stance even in heavily quantitative,
regression-based work, not just in qualitative or interpretive pieces.
PRACTICE ONLY (not stated in guidelines, but consistent across the
spot-checked quantitative full text and abstracts).

### Structural Deviations
Rather than the FIELD_SKILL pattern of a distinct literature-review section
culminating in a hypothesis list, WOX quantitative articles more often
integrate theory-building into a sequence of thematic subheaded sections
that motivate open research questions, closing with an explicit statement
of the paper's contribution just before the methods section. Numbered
"Hypothesis 1/2/3" formatting was not observed even in a fully quantitative
regression paper; research questions are instead posed in prose
("Chief among them is whether this breed of smart machine represents a
break from past means of managerial control..."). RELAXATION of the
FIELD_SKILL norm that a standalone literature review section culminating in
explicit hypotheses is "near-universal in quantitative papers" — at this
ABS 3 outlet, that structure is not obligatory.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Explanatory footnotes/endnotes carrying supplementary methodological or
theoretical asides (e.g., caveats about measurement, brief tangential
points) are used in addition to in-text citations — a lightweight practice
not mentioned in FIELD_SKILL. PRACTICE ONLY, observed in the one full-text
article available; treat as optional rather than expected.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- Discussion and conclusion norms: FIELD DEFAULT (revisits contributions,
  distinguishes theoretical from practical implications, closes with
  limitations and future research)
- Hedging and epistemic caution: FIELD DEFAULT (causal language qualified
  in observational designs; correlational limitations explicitly flagged)
- Citation density and engagement style: FIELD DEFAULT (paraphrase-heavy,
  diplomatic engagement with prior literature, seminal theory cited
  alongside recent empirical work)
- Results and reporting norms: FIELD DEFAULT (tables for precise
  coefficients, prose for substantive interpretation)
- Ethics/methods rigor expectations: FIELD DEFAULT

---

## Common Pitfalls
- Do not force a standalone "Literature Review" section with a terminal
  hypothesis list for quantitative submissions; WOX readily accepts
  integrated theory-building sections that pose open research questions
  instead.
- Do not underuse keywords — evidence suggests the journal's practice runs
  higher (up to 9) than the lowest published guideline figure (3).
- Remember the required ~50-word author biographical paragraph; it is easy
  to omit since many HRM&EMP journals do not require one.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

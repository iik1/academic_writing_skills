# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Transport History

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** BUS HIST & ECON HIST
- **Publisher:** SAGE
- **Scope:** Fully refereed biannual journal publishing scholarly articles on the history of transport, travel, tourism, and mobility, including their relationship with planning and policy. Also publishes Surveys & Speculations, Research Notes, Museum and Exhibition Reviews, and book reviews.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/author-instructions/jth
- **Field baseline:** business-economic-history-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 3 accessible examples (titles: "Lift industry and vertical transport development in Poland," "Steamers, refugees, and empires: The maritime displacement of Circassians in the Eastern Mediterranean," and Susan Jones May 2026 article); limited by paywall access; supplemented with author guideline analysis.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure and Submission Format
Manuscripts must include a separate **title page** containing: paper title, author name(s), abstract, keywords, affiliation(s), and professional biography. The main manuscript should follow with clear, defined section headings. Word or LaTeX format is preferred. **All text, including footnotes and quotations, must be double-spaced.** Double-blind peer review applies.

### Length
Original research articles: maximum **8,000 words including numbered footnotes/endnotes**. Surveys and Speculations: up to 8,000 words. Museum and exhibition reviews: maximum 1,200 words.

### Abstract
Must be **less than 150 words** and should focus on **findings, argument, methodology, and sources**—not research background or context. Abstract format is not formally structured (no labeled background/methods/results sections).

### Keywords and Biography
Identify **at least three keywords** that enable meaningful electronic search; do not repeat words from the paper title. Professional biography on the title page must be **less than 100 words**.

### Citations
**Numbered footnotes** (not author-date parenthetical citations) with **superscript note numbers following punctuation** (e.g., "xyz." not "xyz"). Use the **short-title system** for footnotes. A **final bibliography** lists all sources, including archival and oral sources. Archival sources are listed by **institution name**; oral sources by **interviewee surname**. Every in-text citation must have a corresponding entry in the reference list and vice versa; citations must have identical spelling and year.

### Other Required Elements
- **Funding statement** required as a separate heading, even if no funding was received.
- All manuscripts not conforming to these guidelines may be returned.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Manuscript Structure
ENFORCED DELTA: Journal of Transport History enforces a formal submission structure (separate title page with metadata) that is not explicitly required by the FIELD_SKILL baseline. While the baseline describes typical article organization (opening, literature, sources/methods, substantive sections, conclusion), JTH adds a procedural requirement for a distinct title page and specifies that section headings must be clearly defined, suggesting slightly more rigid structure than the field norm allows.

### Citation Style
ENFORCED DELTA: JTH mandates **numbered footnotes with short-title system**, whereas FIELD_SKILL does not specify a citation format. This is a stricter, more formal requirement than is typical in the field baseline, which is agnostic to footnote vs. author-date systems. The requirement for superscript numbers following punctuation and the short-title system are journal-specific prescriptions not covered by FIELD_SKILL.

### Abstract Format and Word Limit
ENFORCED DELTA: JTH specifies a strict **<150-word limit** with explicit focus on **findings, argument, methodology, and sources** (excluding background/context). FIELD_SKILL describes abstracts as "compact, non-technical summaries of the puzzle, argument, and contribution" but does not specify word limits or exclude background. JTH's focus on methodology and sources reflects its emphasis on archival and empirical transparency; FIELD_SKILL allows more flexibility in what the abstract should emphasize.

### Formal Metadata Requirements
ENFORCED DELTA: JTH requires a professional biography (<100 words) on the title page, which FIELD_SKILL does not mention. The separate title page with all metadata is a procedural requirement specific to JTH's submission system (Manuscript Central) and does not reflect a field-level norm.

### Spacing and Formatting
ENFORCED DELTA: JTH explicitly requires **double-spacing throughout, including footnotes and quotations**. FIELD_SKILL does not specify spacing conventions. This is a typographical requirement specific to the journal's submission and peer-review process.

### Funding Statement
ENFORCED DELTA: JTH requires an explicit funding statement as a separate heading, even if no funding was received. FIELD_SKILL does not address this; it reflects current SAGE publishing standards for transparency and disclosure, not a field-level convention.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- **Voice and Register:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Narrative-analytical register, formal but not terse; first-person plural acceptable alongside impersonal framing.
- **Sentence and Paragraph Style:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Medium-long sentences with subordinate clauses; substantial, evidence-grounded paragraphs.
- **Hedging and Epistemic Caution:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Moderate hedging; confident statements of central argument expected.
- **Citation Density:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. High density throughout; claims about facts and interpretations routinely cited to sources.
- **Direct Quotation from Primary Sources:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Valued and common evidentiary practice for substantiating claims about motive, perception, and contested events.
- **Archival and Methods Transparency:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. JTH articles expected to describe archive provenance, data construction, and source limitations; abstract requirement to state methodology/sources reinforces field-level practice.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Oversized abstracts:** Abstracts exceeding 150 words are commonly rejected; authors should ruthlessly edit to fit, focusing only on findings/argument/method/sources and omitting background framing.
2. **Background-heavy abstracts:** Including literature context or "why this question matters" in the abstract instead of leading with the argument and evidence will weaken impact; JTH reviewers expect the abstract to preview the research contribution directly.
3. **Parenthetical citations:** Submitting author-date or parenthetical citations instead of numbered footnotes; all citations must be converted to the short-title footnote system before submission or the manuscript will be returned.
4. **Missing or incomplete title page:** Omitting affiliation, professional biography, or funding statement on the separate title page will delay processing; all metadata must be complete before peer review.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

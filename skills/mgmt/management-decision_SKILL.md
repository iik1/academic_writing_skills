# Journal Writing Skill: Management Decision

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN (management)
- **Publisher:** Emerald Publishing Limited
- **Scope:** International general business journal emphasizing application of research to organizational implementation of managerial decision-making. Founded in 1967; the oldest and longest-running scholarly publication focused specifically on the area of management. Emphasis on bridging theory and practice through theoretically sound intervention tools for managerial problem solving.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.emeraldgrouppublishing.com/journal/md (requires institutional/direct access; use ScholarOne submission system)
- **Field baseline:** mgmt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 3 articles (limited access due to paywall; details from article abstracts and metadata from ScienceDirect/Emerald)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows standard MGMT field structure (quantitative deductive, qualitative inductive, theoretical, or integrative review templates per FIELD_SKILL). No journal-specific deviations stated in guidelines. **ENFORCED (stated in guidelines).**

### Length
- **Target:** 1,000–4,000 words (substantially shorter than ABS 4* journals, which typically range 8,000–18,000 words).
- **Word count includes:** Structured abstract, all text in tables and figures, and references.
- **Additional allowance:** Add 280 words for each figure or table beyond the base count.
- **Classification:** ENFORCED (stated in author guidelines).

### Abstract
- **Required format:** Structured abstract only; unstructured abstracts not accepted.
- **Sub-headings (4–7, with mandatory fields):**
  - Purpose (mandatory)
  - Design/Methodology/Approach (mandatory)
  - Findings (mandatory)
  - Research Limitations/Implications (if applicable)
  - Practical Implications (if applicable)
  - Social Implications (if applicable)
  - Originality/Value (mandatory)
- **Word limit:** Under 250 words.
- **Classification:** ENFORCED (stated in guidelines).

### Citations and References
- **Style:** Harvard (Author, Year) in-text; full author names and initials in reference list; journal titles in full.
- **Classification:** ENFORCED (Emerald standard for all journals).

### Headings
- **Title:** Maximum 8 words.
- **Section headings:** Follow FIELD_SKILL conventions (no journal-specific override stated). Use substantive labels (e.g., "Organizational Justice and Ethical Decision-Making") rather than generic labels in theoretical work.
- **Subsection numbering:** Not mandated by guidelines; practice varies.
- **Classification:** FIELD DEFAULT for section structure; ENFORCED for title length.

### Tables and Figures
- **Formatting:** Supply succinct, clear captions for all tables, figures, and plates.
- **Numbering:** Consecutive (Table 1, Table 2; Fig. 1, Fig. 2).
- **Appendices:** Number as A1, A2, A3, etc. Equations in appendices as A.1, A.2, A.3.
- **File format:** Accepted in Microsoft Word (preferred), LaTeX (with PDF), or other standard formats.
- **Classification:** ENFORCED (Emerald standard).

### Keywords and Author Information
- **Keywords:** Maximum 12.
- **Author biographies:** If included, no more than 100 words per author.
- **Blind review requirement:** Omit all author names, previous work references phrased as "(previous research has shown)" not "(our previous research showed)"), and unpublished co-author work from the submission.
- **Classification:** ENFORCED.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Do not reference your own unpublished work by name in the blind review phase.
- Do not include author names in figures, captions, or acknowledgments during submission (upload acknowledgments separately).
- All biographies, author notes, and funding statements must be uploaded as separate files.
- **Classification:** ENFORCED (blind review protocol).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Manuscript Length
**Delta:** Management Decision targets 1,000–4,000 words—substantially shorter than the 8,000–18,000 word range typical of ABS 4* management journals covered in FIELD_SKILL (Academy of Management Journal, Academy of Management Review, ASQ, Organization Science, JOM). This reflects the journal's ABS 2 positioning and emphasis on concise, practice-applicable insights over in-depth theoretical development.

**Classification:** RELAXATION (lower-tier journal relaxes length expectations).

### Abstract Structure
**Delta:** Management Decision mandates structured abstracts with 4–7 labeled sub-headings (Purpose, Design/Methodology/Approach, Findings, etc.), whereas FIELD_SKILL notes that "structured or unstructured; both appear across outlets." The mandatory structure is more rigid here.

**Classification:** ENFORCED (more prescriptive than field norm).

### Emphasis on Practice and Application
**Delta:** While FIELD_SKILL notes that management journals "expect some managerial relevance" in discussion sections, Management Decision's scope explicitly positions application and practical intervention as primary. Articles are expected to demonstrate translatable insights for managerial decision-making, not only theoretical contribution.

**Classification:** PRACTICE ONLY (observed in journal scope statement; not always made explicit in individual articles).

### Voice and Accessibility
**Delta:** Given the shorter word count (1,000–4,000 vs. 10,000–18,000) and applied focus, Management Decision articles tend toward more concise, direct argumentation and less extensive literature engagement than ABS 4* outlets. Introductions are shorter and background literature is less comprehensively cited.

**Classification:** PRACTICE ONLY (inferred from word-limit constraint and applied positioning).

- **Methods reporting:** Follows FIELD_SKILL standards (quantitative: regression, fixed-effects panels, robustness checks; qualitative: ethnography, interviews, coding, saturation; tense in past).
- **Results/Findings presentation:** Follows FIELD_SKILL (tables primary for quantitative, theoretical categories for qualitative; explicit hypothesis revisiting; effect sizes reported).
- **Discussion and conclusion:** Follows FIELD_SKILL structure (summary, theoretical implications, practical implications, limitations, future directions).

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Exceeding word count:** Authors often exceed the 1,000–4,000 word target by including excessive literature review or methods detail. Prioritize concision and defer exhaustive background to the discussion section.

2. **Unstructured abstract:** Submitting a narrative or unstructured abstract will be rejected. All abstracts must use the seven sub-headings (Purpose, Design/Methodology/Approach, Findings, Research Limitations/Implications, Practical Implications, Social Implications, Originality/Value) even if some are marked "N/A."

3. **Author identification during blind review:** Leaving author names in acknowledgments, figure captions, or biographical paragraphs violates blind review protocol. Upload all identifying material separately.

4. **Lengthy introductions relative to empirical sections:** The compressed word count (vs. top-tier journals) means introductions should be tighter (800–1,200 words vs. 1,200–1,500 typical in ABS 4*). Establish niche quickly and move to method/findings.

5. **Insufficient emphasis on managerial implications:** Even theory-focused papers should include a pragmatic angle—how can managers or organizations act on these insights? Generic "practical implications" sections weak on specifics are insufficient for this journal's positioning.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

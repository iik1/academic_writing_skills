# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Economic History

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** BUS HIST & ECON HIST
- **Publisher:** Cambridge University Press (for the Economic History Association)
- **Scope:** Economic phenomena in historical context across all regions, periods,
  and methodologies (growth, inequality, political economy, labor, money and
  banking, trade, transport, agriculture, technology, health, demography,
  government and regulation), addressed to economic historians, economists,
  social scientists, and historians generally.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-economic-history/information/instructions-contributors
  and https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-economic-history/information/instructions-contributors/style-sheet
- **Field baseline:** business-economic-history-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 8 (front-matter/abstracts/headings): "Growing,
  Shrinking, and Long-Run Economic Performance" (Broadberry & Wallis, 2025);
  "Institutions, Local Agency, and Allegiance: Healthcare Provision in
  Colonial India" (2025); "The Her in Inheritance: How Marriage Matching Has
  Always Mattered, Quebec 1800–1970"; "How Extractive Was Russian Serfdom?
  Income Inequality in Moscow Province"; "How Britain Unified Germany: Trade
  Routes and the Formation of the Zollverein"; "Napoleonic Administrative
  Reforms and Development in the Italian Mezzogiorno"; a 2025 article on RFC/PWA
  railroad bailouts (1932–1939); "Cross-Cultural Trade and the Slave Ship the
  Bonne Société" (Loango slave market). Full text largely paywalled; relied on
  abstracts, headings, and reference-list conventions.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Title page with competing-interest declarations, authorship statement, author
affiliations, and ORCID for the corresponding author. Body text has no
required named sections beyond what the argument demands (no mandated
"Introduction," "Literature Review," "Methods," "Results" labels).

### Length
Articles: maximum 40 double-spaced pages / under 14,000 words total,
including footnotes, references, tables, and figures; minimum 11-point font.
Notes and Comments: under 7,500 words. Online appendices unlimited length.

### Abstract
Absolute maximum 99 words (counting hyphenated segments), single unstructured
paragraph, double-spaced, indented from margins, no author names. Notes and
Comments do not require an abstract.

### Citations
Author-date (Harvard) system, not footnote/endnote citation — a departure
from the archival-footnote-heavy citation style implied as typical in
FIELD_SKILL. In-text format "(Author Year, p. X)"; maximum three authors
named, "et al." beyond three. Legal cases cited in footnotes only, not in
the reference list. No ibid., op. cit., or loc. cit. Footnotes reserved for
substantive asides, not extended discussion.

### Headings
Section headings are titles only, unnumbered — no numbered or lettered
sections, and no numbered/lettered subheadings for hypotheses or results.

### Tables and Figures
Every table numbered with a descriptive title; complete source line and
interpretive notes below; horizontal rules only, no vertical lines or bold
fonts; italicized mathematical variables/parameters; dashes for missing data;
full date ranges spelled out (e.g., "1813–1822"). Figures must be
computer-generated (no hand-drawn elements), consistent styling across all
figures in a paper, source/notes below, legends inside figure boundaries,
no borders. Alt-text descriptions required for accessibility (WCAG 2.1 AA).

### Explicit Prohibitions
No contractions anywhere in the text. No indented lists, bullet points, or
numbered hypotheses in the body. Data and materials must be deposited with
openICPSR for replication. Double-anonymized peer review — manuscripts must
be blinded of identifying information at submission.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
Headings across sampled articles are consistently short, unnumbered
thematic phrases (e.g., "Why Do Economies Stop Shrinking?," "Proximate
Causes of the Decline in Shrinking") rather than numbered sections or
formal "Methods"/"Results" labels, confirming the guideline is followed in
practice (ENFORCED). This is tighter than FIELD_SKILL, which allows
occasional econometric-style numbered sections (data, methods, results,
robustness) for quantitative economic history; at JEH such formal
numbering does not appear even in quantitative pieces — headings stay
narrative-thematic regardless of method (RELAXATION/refinement of the
FIELD_SKILL allowance, consistent with ABS 3 not redefining the baseline).

### Citation Density
The shift from footnote-based citation (implied as the archival norm in
FIELD_SKILL) to author-date parenthetical citation is ENFORCED — confirmed
in guidelines and consistent with reference-list conventions visible in
sampled articles. This changes in-text texture: claims are supported with
"(Author Year)" rather than dense footnote apparatus, even though footnotes
remain available for substantive asides.

## Common Pitfalls
- Using footnote/endnote-style citation instead of author-date parenthetical
  citation — a common default for economic historians used to the field norm.
- Numbering sections or subsections (e.g., "1. Introduction," "3.2 Data") —
  JEH headings must be unnumbered titles only.
- Writing an abstract over 99 words or in structured (background/methods/
  results) format; JEH abstracts are single unstructured paragraphs under
  the strict word cap.
- Using contractions or bullet-pointed hypothesis lists in the body text.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

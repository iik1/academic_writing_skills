# Journal Writing Skill: Work Organisation, Labour and Globalisation

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** HRM&EMP
- **Publisher:** Pluto Journals
- **Scope:** Bi-annual peer-reviewed journal focusing on the new global division of labour across multiple disciplines (political economy, sociology of work, organisational theory, economic geography, development studies, industrial relations, comparative social policy, communications studies, technology policy, and gender studies). Combines theoretical analysis with empirical research in accessible language for both academic and policy audiences.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.plutojournals.com/wolgguidelines/
- **Field baseline:** hrm-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 4 (Tech Work special issue 2025, Logistical Labour special issue 2025, plus journal homepage scope guidance and guidelines documentation)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Articles are not required to follow a rigid IMRaD structure. The journal encourages thematic and narrative organization suited to the research rather than formulaic section ordering. Special issues and introductions are explicitly part of the publication stream alongside empirical research articles.

### Length
**Target: 5,000-6,000 words** (excluding footnotes and bibliography). This is notably shorter than field baseline for ABS 4*/4 journals and reflects the ABS 1 tier.

### Abstract
**Approximately 100 words.** No structured abstract format specified (unlike some field journals); unstructured abstract is acceptable.

### Citations
**Parenthetical author-date style:** (Author, Year). For direct quotations, include page numbers after colon: (Smith, 2001:41-42). Multiple sources separated by semicolons: (Smith, 2001; Jones, 2002).

**Bibliography alphabetical by surname;** formatted as:
- **Books:** Surname, Initials. (Year). *Title in italics*. City: Publisher.
- **Journal articles:** Surname, Initials. (Year). Article title (no italics). *Journal Title*, volume(issue):page-range.
- **Online sources:** include full URL and access date.

### Headings
Use word processor heading styles, not manual formatting. Avoid numbered headings (e.g., no "1. Introduction," "2. Methods"). Employ subheadings sparingly; the preference is for narrative prose with section breaks rather than frequent subdivisions.

### Tables and Figures
Minimize use. Supply graphs in Excel format with underlying data; diagrams in Adobe Illustrator-compatible formats. Use black/grayscale only—colour figures are not accepted. All figures and tables must have captions.

### Language and Style Requirements
- **UK English spelling and grammar required.**
- Single quotation marks (except for quotes within quotes, which use double).
- Hyphens avoided except for linked adjectives (e.g., "well-known," not "well known").
- Italics reserved for uncommon non-English phrases and journal/book titles only; avoid italicizing for emphasis.
- Use "%" symbol rather than "per cent."
- Accessible writing style explicitly encouraged; not aimed exclusively at specialist academic audiences.

### Format and Submission
- Submit in Word or RTF format.
- 1.5 line spacing.
- Hyphenation and justification disabled.
- Include anonymized version (substitute **\*\*\*\*\*** for author names; remove self-citations or mask them).
- Author biographical statements required: name(s), position/title, institution(s).

### Explicit Prohibitions and Preferences
- Articles are "not reports" and "taxonomies do not necessarily constitute arguments" (per guidelines). Avoid numbered lists and bullet points; use prose to make sequential points.
- Footnotes are acceptable only for factual explanations; publication references must appear in-text as (Author, Year).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Manuscript Length and Structure
**ENFORCED RELAXATION.** The 5,000–6,000 word target is significantly shorter than the implicit expectation in ABS 4*/4 baselines (which typically accommodate 8,000–12,000-word empirical articles). This ABS 1 journal trades depth for accessibility and reflects its policy-oriented scope. Additionally, strict IMRaD sequencing is not mandatory; thematic, narrative, or other logical orderings are explicitly acceptable, which is a relaxation from field baseline's near-universal IMRaD preference at higher tiers.

### Abstract Format
**FIELD DEFAULT.** Unstructured 100-word abstract is simpler than some ABS 4 journals but aligns with field norm tolerance for variation.

### Heading and List Style Preference
**ENFORCED.** The explicit prohibition on numbered headings and numbered lists, with preference for prose-based narrative structure, is stricter than field baseline. The baseline allows flexible heading conventions; this journal enforces a specific anti-enumeration aesthetic aligned with accessibility and narrative flow.

### Accessible/Public-Facing Writing Tone
**ENFORCED.** The journal's stated commitment to "an accessible style of writing" and openness to "submissions from contributors working outside the academic sphere" (e.g., policy makers, trade unions, civil society) imposes a lower threshold for jargon and specialist language than ABS 4*/4 field norms. This is explicit in the journal's aims; field baseline does not emphasize this constraint.

### Citation Density and Engagement Style
**PRACTICE ONLY / LOWER DENSITY.** While the field baseline describes moderate-to-high citation density, the shorter word limit (5,000–6,000 vs. 8,000–12,000) mechanically reduces opportunity for dense citations. Moreover, the interdisciplinary and practitioner-inclusive scope suggests citation engagement may be more selective (citing key works rather than comprehensive literature mapping), though guidelines do not explicitly state this. Observed in special issue introductions: citations are present but less encyclopedic than ABS 4 norms.

### Anonymization Protocol
**ENFORCED.** The journal requires explicit anonymization by asterisks (not omission), which is more prescriptive than field baseline norms. This is stated in guidelines.

### Colour and Formatting in Figures
**ENFORCED.** The explicit prohibition on colour in figures/diagrams is stricter than field baseline and reflects typographic/printing constraints at this publisher tier.

### Word Limit Enforcement
**ENFORCED (SOFT).** The guideline reads "Please aim for 5,000–6,000 words," suggesting flexibility, but this is still a firm target. Field baseline does not specify length; ABS 4*/4 journals typically impose 8,000–12,000-word minima/maxima with more tolerance for overrun in complex empirical work. This journal's lower limit reflects its ABS 1 tier and policy-informed scope.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Exceeding word limit.** Authors accustomed to ABS 4 norms (8,000–12,000 words) routinely overshoot 6,000 words. Prioritize synthesis and selective citations; omit comprehensive literature reviews if space is tight.

2. **Numbered headings and bullet-point lists.** The journal explicitly discourages these in favour of narrative prose. Rewrite "1. Key findings: A, B, C" as "Three key findings emerged: first, A; second, B; third, C."

3. **Jargon and specialist language without explanation.** The journal seeks dialogue with policy makers and practitioners. Define field-specific concepts on first use; avoid acronyms without expansion.

4. **Colour figures and complex formatting.** Figures must be grayscale or black-and-white; avoid fancy table formatting or colour coding. Simplify diagrams for Adobe Illustrator conversion.

5. **Insufficient anonymization.** Ensure all author names are replaced with asterisks, including in citations of your own prior work. Reviewers are blinded; identify your paper in ways other than author name if you must reference your prior work.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: MIT Sloan Management Review

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN
- **Publisher:** MIT Sloan School of Management / MIT Press
- **Scope:** A practitioner-facing management magazine (not a conventional peer-reviewed research journal in structure) that translates management research and executive experience into actionable guidance for business leaders, covering strategy, leadership, AI, sustainability, and organizational change.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://sloanreview.mit.edu/authors/ (supplemented by https://support.mitsmr.com/support/solutions/articles/60000701661)
- **Field baseline:** mgmt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 5 (front-matter/full text via web): "Why AI Demands a New Breed of Leaders"; "Eight Essential Leadership Tips for 2024"; "10 Urgent AI Takeaways for Leaders"; "The Top 10 MIT SMR Articles of 2024/2025" (index pages); "Research Highlights" listing page (multiple abstracts: allyship, employee ownership, AI coding tools, startup sustainability)

**Access/status note:** As of this research (mid-2026), MIT Sloan School has announced it will cease publishing MIT SMR and is no longer accepting new submissions (pipeline full). This does not change the style record below, which reflects the journal's established format.

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict. IMPORTANT: MIT SMR is not a conventional academic research article outlet — it publishes short, practitioner-oriented essays and "research highlights," not full empirical papers with the standard IMRaD/theory-hypotheses structure.

### Structure
No required academic sections. No Abstract/Introduction/Theory/Methods/Results/Discussion template. Articles open with a short summary/framing box, develop the argument in prose (often with numbered takeaways, bolded subheads, or bullet lists), and close without a formal "Conclusion" or "Limitations" section. "Research Highlights" is a distinct content type: short pieces that translate a specific study's findings into practitioner takeaways.

### Length
1,000–2,500 words for essays and research highlights (roughly one-tenth the length of a standard empirical MGMT article per FIELD_SKILL).

### Abstract
No structured academic abstract. A short (roughly 40–60 word) summary/teaser box precedes the article, stating the central takeaway in plain language — functions as an abstract but is written as marketing copy, not a scholarly summary (no method, no sample, no citations).

### Citations
No reference list, no APA/author-date in-text citations. Sources are attributed via hyperlinks embedded in the prose and named attributions ("a 2024 Foundry survey found…", "Read the full article, '[Title],' by [Author]"). Underlying academic studies are named informally, not formally cited.

### Headings
Numbered-list and bolded-subhead formats are common (e.g., "8 leadership tips," "10 AI takeaways"), rather than the generic Introduction/Methods/Results headings of FIELD_SKILL.

### Tables and Figures
Not part of the standard format for essay-length pieces; data points are reported inline as prose statistics rather than in regression/data tables.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- No prior publication elsewhere; cannot be under review at another outlet.
- Authors must disclose financial relationships with any companies discussed/cited.
- Authors must disclose AI tool use and how it was used in producing the piece.
- Full submissions must be Word documents (.doc/.docx); PDFs not accepted.
- Submission is not accepted at present (pipeline closed / publication ceasing) — noted for currency, not a style rule.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
RELAXATION: Register is conversational and direct-address ("If you're one of those leaders…"), not the hedged academic "we/I" voice of FIELD_SKILL. Tone is advisory/mentor-like rather than argumentative-scholarly.

### Statistical Reporting
RELAXATION: Findings are reported as isolated inline statistics with informal source attribution (e.g., "57% of companies…, Wavestone research") rather than FIELD_SKILL's coefficient/effect-size/significance reporting conventions. No hypothesis-testing language ("H1 predicted…") appears.

### Citation Density
RELAXATION: Citation density is very low and non-academic — hyperlinks and named attributions replace the dense parenthetical (Author, Year) citation practice FIELD_SKILL describes for Introduction/Theory sections. No reference list.

### Structural Deviations
ENFORCED (via guidelines, confirmed in articles): No Theory/Hypotheses, Methods, or Results sections; no propositions or hypotheses numbering. Numbered-tip and takeaway-roundup formats are a recurring, journal-specific structural pattern not present in FIELD_SKILL.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
PRACTICE ONLY: "Research Highlight" pieces exist specifically to summarize a single academic study's finding in one paragraph-level takeaway for a managerial audience — a content type not addressed by FIELD_SKILL at all.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- Practical/managerial relevance emphasis: overlaps with FIELD_SKILL's expectation that management journals value some practical implications, though MIT SMR makes this the *entire* focus rather than a secondary discussion point — FIELD DEFAULT in spirit, amplified in degree.

---

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting a conventional academic manuscript (with Abstract, Theory, Hypotheses, Methods, Results) will be rejected outright — MIT SMR is not that format; content must be rewritten as a practitioner essay or a short research-highlight translation.
- Including an academic reference list or APA in-text citations is inappropriate; use hyperlinked attribution instead.
- Exceeding roughly 2,500 words defeats the format; drafts must be substantially cut relative to a typical MGMT empirical paper.
- Failing to disclose AI-tool use or financial ties to companies mentioned in the piece is an explicit rule violation, not just a norm.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

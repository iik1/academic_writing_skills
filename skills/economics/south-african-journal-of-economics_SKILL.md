# Journal Writing Skill: South African Journal of Economics

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Wiley-Blackwell
- **Scope:** Generalist economics journal covering all fields in economics, with particular focus on developmental and African contexts. African and Southern African development challenges receive particular attention.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.essa.org.za/saje/guidelines-contributors/
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 10 (abstracts/metadata from 2024–2025 issues; direct access to full PDFs limited by publisher paywall; working paper versions and INDEX abstracts consulted)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No explicit mandatory section structure published. Standard empirical papers observed to follow IMRAD (Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion/Conclusion) but this is not formally required. Theory and structural papers likely follow field baseline.

### Length
No explicit word limit published in publicly accessible guidelines. Submission guidelines state manuscripts should be "in final form" but do not state a page or word cap.

### Abstract
- **Format:** Unstructured (no required headings specified)
- **Length:** 100 words maximum
- **Required metadata:** JEL classification code(s); keywords

### Citations
- **Style:** Harvard referencing (author-year, parenthetical or in-text)
- **No explicit limit stated** in public guidelines
- Examples given:
  - Journal articles: Author surname, initials (year). Article title. *Journal Name*, volume, pages.
  - Books: Author surname, initials (year). *Book Title*. Place: Publisher.

### Headings
No explicit convention published. Observed practice follows standard IMRAD hierarchy but numbering system not specified in guidelines.

### Tables and Figures
- **Constraint:** "Tables, equations and diagrams should be kept to a minimum"
- **Figure format:** "Clear and professionally drawn" — diagrams and figures should be created in Excel and embedded into manuscript
- **Caption requirement:** All illustrations require legends with numbers, titles, and sources

### Language
- **English variant:** British English (Oxford English Dictionary; avoid Americanisms)
- **Abbreviations:** Minimize abbreviations; use clear, concise, and accessible writing
- **Tone:** Not explicitly stated; field baseline applies

### Submission Format
- Blind format (anonymized; author names and affiliations removed)
- Double-blind peer review standard
- Submitted via ScholarOne Manuscripts at http://mc.manuscriptcentral.com/saje
- 1.5 line spacing
- Page and line numbers required in submission
- File format: DOC, DOCX, or RTF (Microsoft Word documents must not be locked or protected)

### Explicit Prohibitions
- No prohibition of contractions stated
- No prohibition of first-person stated
- Standard academic integrity expectations (competing interests, author contributions, funding disclosures, data availability statements all required as separate sections)

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
**RELAXATION:** Unlike ABS 4*/4 journals (which have a near-universal disciplined section architecture), SAJE does not publish an explicit mandatory structure. Empirical articles observed follow IMRAD, but this is practice-level, not stated as a requirement. Theory papers' structure not visible in sample. This represents a relaxation of the field baseline's expectation of strict structural discipline.

### Citation Density
**FIELD DEFAULT** — Observed articles follow standard field norms: high citation density in introduction and literature; lower in methods and results. No deviation from baseline.

### Voice and Register
**FIELD DEFAULT** — Articles sampled use first-person plural ("we") and active voice consistent with field baseline. Formality, precision, and economical prose observed match ABS 4*/4 norms. No deviation from baseline.

### Statistical Reporting
**FIELD DEFAULT** — Results sections integrate reporting and interpretation; tables are primary vehicle; quantitative specificity observed (e.g., "affects lending by X percent"). Consistent with field baseline.

### Abstract Style
**DELTA - GUIDELINE ONLY:** 100-word abstract is markedly shorter than field baseline's typical practice. Unstructured format (no mandatory headings) differs from contemporary empirical economics norms, which increasingly require structured abstracts (Background, Methods, Results, Conclusion). However, observed abstracts in sampled articles (e.g., Pillay & Makrelov 2025; Sibande et al. 2025) do contain these elements logically organized, suggesting practice exceeds guideline minimalism.

### Tables and Figures
**DELTA - ENFORCED:** Explicit constraint to keep tables, equations, and diagrams "to a minimum" is more restrictive than field baseline, which treats tables as "primary vehicle for quantitative results." Observed articles (e.g., bank-lending studies) include multiple tables, suggesting this constraint is interpreted flexibly or that journals at lower ABS tiers allow fewer results tables than top venues. No quantitative maximum stated in guidelines; practice suggests 5–8 tables typical.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Assuming no word limit means unlimited length:** The journal's silence on word limits does not permit 15,000+ word manuscripts. Submission guidelines emphasize "final form" and peer review overhead; observe comparable recent articles (typically 8,000–12,000 words). Err toward 10,000 words maximum for empirical pieces.

2. **Overloading with tables and figures:** The explicit constraint to keep tables "to a minimum" is a real norm at this lower tier. Consolidate results; use appendix liberally. Observed articles prioritize a few high-impact tables over comprehensive results display.

3. **Using American English or colloquial hedging:** British English is mandatory; American spellings, contractions, and vague hedging ("arguably," "it could be argued") are discouraged. Write in clear, direct claims defended by evidence, not linguistic softening.

4. **Omitting JEL codes or keywords:** These are explicit requirements; their absence may trigger desk rejection or delay review.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Schmalenbach Journal of Business Research

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN
- **Publisher:** Springer Nature (Springer Gabler)
- **Scope:** International English-language journal publishing original high-quality research covering traditional and emerging business administration areas including accounting, finance, marketing, strategic management, organization, human resource management, production and logistics management, taxation, digital business, entrepreneurship, and business ethics/CSR.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://link.springer.com/journal/41471/submission-guidelines (author guidelines behind Springer paywall; accessible to authors via submission portal)
- **Field baseline:** mgmt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 5 (examined titles, abstracts, and metadata from 2024-2026 issues; full-text access limited by Springer paywall)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows standard IMRaD structure (Introduction–Methods/Research Design–Results/Findings–Discussion) for empirical articles. The journal accepts multiple article types: quantitative empirical, qualitative empirical, theoretical/conceptual, and integrative reviews. Section naming follows the article type; no prescriptive section labels enforced, but empirical articles consistently employ Methods/Research Design and Results/Findings sections. Acknowledgments, Declarations of Competing Interest, and Data Availability statements appear at article end.

### Length
Springer Nature does not impose strict manuscript length limits; journal tolerates 8,000–12,000 words typical for business research journals. No explicit word limit stated in publicly available guidelines.

### Abstract
Unstructured or minimally structured. Springer Nature standard for affiliated journals: 150–250 words. No citations in abstract. Abstract should state research question/gap, approach/method, main findings, and contribution. Specific structured abstract format not enforced by journal guidelines reviewed.

### Citations
APA in-text format (Author, Year) or Author (Year) when author is part of sentence. Springer Nature standard: et al. for three or more authors. Multiple citations ordered alphabetically or chronologically. Reference lists typically 40–80 references for empirical articles (consistent with FIELD_SKILL). Double-blind review requires author anonymization; avoid self-citations that reveal identity.

### Headings
Decimal numbering system with no more than three levels (e.g., 1, 1.1, 1.1.1). Consistent formatting required; no skipping levels. Run-in headings are the only exception to level-skipping rule. Bold formatting for run-in headings only.

### Tables and Figures
No explicit guidelines extracted from publicly available sources. Springer Nature standard: figures and tables should have captions and be numbered sequentially; tables report data/findings; figures illustrate concepts or patterns. Footnotes in tables use superscript lowercase letters or asterisks for significance.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Simultaneous submission to multiple journals prohibited
- Manuscript must not have been published elsewhere in any form or language, in full or in part, unless explicitly new work expanding prior research
- Author responsibility to anonymize manuscript for double-blind review
- File formats required: .docx or .doc (Word 2007+) or LaTeX for mathematical content. All editable source files must be provided at submission and revision.
- Footnotes to text should not consist solely of citations or include bibliographic details
- Declarations of interest (conflicts within 3 years of beginning work) must be disclosed

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Structural Deviations
**RELAXATION:** This journal accepts a broader range of article types than the FIELD_SKILL baseline implies. FIELD_SKILL emphasizes quantitative deductive, qualitative inductive, theoretical/conceptual, and integrative review templates. Schmalenbach Journal of Business Research similarly accommodates all four types, but does not mandate section names (e.g., "Theory" section is not strictly required; "Theoretical Background" or "Conceptual Framework" are acceptable). Empirical articles apply rigorous methodologies (analytical, archival, experimental, field, case research) but section labeling is flexible to fit research question and article type.

### Anonymous Submission Requirement
**ENFORCED, not mentioned in FIELD_SKILL:** Double-blind review is mandatory. Authors must anonymize all text and accompanying materials, removing names, affiliations, and identifying self-citations. A separate Title Page with full author information is required and submitted independently. This is a hard constraint absent from the FIELD_SKILL baseline.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- **Voice and Person:** FIELD DEFAULT. First-person plural ("we") standard; active voice dominates; moderate hedging with modal verbs ("may," "might," "suggest").
- **Argumentation:** FIELD DEFAULT. Theory sections build logically toward hypotheses; propositions vs. hypotheses distinction maintained; named theoretical frameworks identify and scaffold argument.
- **Citation Density:** FIELD DEFAULT. Introduction and Theory sections citation-dense; Results/Findings sections cite sparsely; Discussion engages citations to interpret findings. Foundational works alongside recent publications.
- **Hedging and Epistemic Stance:** FIELD DEFAULT. "We find," "Our results show" for reported results; "We argue," "We propose," "We suggest" for theoretical positions; "Our findings suggest" for hedged interpretation.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Failure to anonymize:** Authors inadvertently reveal identity through self-citations, author notes, or file metadata. All identifying information must be removed from text; save submission as generic author/anonymous.
2. **Incomplete file submission:** Submitting only PDF or non-editable formats delays review. Provide all editable source files (.docx, .doc, LaTeX) at initial submission.
3. **Overly rigid section naming:** Authors forcing IMRAD labels when the research design (e.g., conceptual, case study, review) naturally calls for flexible section names. Journal tolerates and expects label flexibility; match section titles to research question, not template.
4. **Missing Data Availability statement:** Open Access journal increasingly expects explicit data statement (availability or reasons for restriction). Journals using this practice enforce it; verify current guidelines in submission portal.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

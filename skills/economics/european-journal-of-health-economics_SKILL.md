# Journal Writing Skill: European Journal of Health Economics

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Springer Nature
- **Scope:** Health economics research covering economic dimensions of healthcare systems globally, including insurance, care providers, prevention, health behaviors, equity, innovation, technology diffusion, and economic evaluation methodology.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://link.springer.com/journal/10198/submission-guidelines (paywall; public guidelines unavailable at retrieval time)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 5 full-text articles from PMC (2024–2025): health shocks and behavior; craniopharyngioma economics; Spain HTA reforms; FDA special designations; multi-attribute utility instruments systematic review

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

### Structure
**IMRAD enforcement:** All empirical and review articles follow Introduction–Methods–Results–Discussion–Conclusion order. No flexibility in section sequencing.

**Structured abstract required:** Abstract must use explicit labeled sections (Objective/Background, Methods, Results, Conclusions/Implications). Free-form prose abstracts not acceptable.

### Length
No specific word limit located in publicly available guidelines. Practice observation: articles typically 5,000–8,000 words (excluding tables/appendices).

### Abstract
- **Format:** Structured with labeled sections; flows as separate sentences under each heading
- **Scope:** 150–250 words typical; covers objective, methods, key findings, and policy/clinical implications
- **Keywords:** Mandatory; listed after abstract as indexed terms

### Citations
**Numbered in-bracket format required:** [1], [2, 3], [1–4] for multiple citations. Not author-year parenthetical.

**Reference list:** Alphabetical, with full journal titles and DOIs included. Follows standard biomedical citation conventions (similar to Vancouver style).

### Headings
Primary headings use standard unadorned style (not numbered); subheadings in outline hierarchy. "Methods," "Results," "Discussion" as standard section names (not "Empirical Strategy" or "Analysis").

### Tables and Figures
Tables and figures embedded in text near reference. Captions must be self-contained descriptive text. Figures often number 20+; extensive visual presentation is encouraged.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- No author-year in-text citations (violation of journal standard)
- No free-form abstracts (structured format is mandatory)
- Authorship changes after revision rarely permitted; order authors carefully at submission

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
**ENFORCED DELTA:** Passive voice dominates throughout. Specimen constructions: "was conducted," "were identified," "data were analyzed," "findings suggest." First-person plural "we" appears sparingly (chiefly in Methods: "we searched," "we extracted") but is NOT the default voice as in FIELD_SKILL. Authors frequently use "the study," "the analysis," "analyses" as impersonal subjects.

Contrast with FIELD_SKILL: "We show," "we find," "we develop" are near-universal in ABS 4*/4 economics. This journal's biomedical framing enforces depersonalization.

### Citation Density and Format
**ENFORCED DELTA:** Numbered in-bracket citations [#], not author-year. This is the journal's non-negotiable house style and overrides FIELD_SKILL's author-year norm. Introduction still carries high citation density per FIELD_SKILL, but format is reversed.

### Abstract Format
**ENFORCED DELTA:** Abstracts use explicit labeled sections (Objective, Methods, Results, Conclusions) with each section a self-contained paragraph or sentence cluster. FIELD_SKILL does not mandate this structure; leading economics journals permit flowing prose abstracts. This journal enforces biomedical IMRAD abstraction.

### Structural Rigidity
**ENFORCED DELTA:** Section ordering is fixed: Introduction–Methods–Results–Discussion–Conclusion. No flexibility for Background/Institutional Setting, Literature Review as standalone section, or rearrangement. FIELD_SKILL permits variable section ordering (e.g., Related Literature optional, Background conditional); this journal does not.

### Statistical Reporting and Results Presentation
**PRACTICE ONLY:** Results are heavily table-and-figure-driven, with 15–25+ figures common per article. Prose highlights quantitative findings with specificity ("254 out of 477 cases matched," "mean cost £X (SD Y)") but tables carry the analytic load. FIELD_SKILL permits this but does not enforce it; EJHE practice normalizes extensive visual reporting.

### Hedging Patterns
**PRACTICE ONLY:** Hedging is more frequent than FIELD_SKILL's economics baseline. Qualifiers appear routinely: "suggests," "appears," "do not contradict," "speak to the question of whether." This reflects the biomedical tradition of observational caution rather than identification-driven confidence. However, hedging is not excessive; clear claims are still present. This is a **mild relaxation** relative to FIELD_SKILL's preference for unhedged claims supported by identification.

### Discussion and Limitations
**PRACTICE ONLY:** Limitations receive prominent, transparent treatment (often subsection status in Discussion). Attrition, measurement error, and selection bias are named and candidly discussed. FIELD_SKILL expects acknowledgment; EJHE practice elevates transparency to a near-required ritual.

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Sentence and paragraph style:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Medium-to-long sentences with high technical density; paragraph density high (4–10 sentences per paragraph).
- **Argumentation and positioning relative to prior work:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Introduction states research question explicitly, previews results, identifies contributions. Positioning relative to similar work is required.
- **Methodological detail:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Identification strategy clearly stated; robustness checks expected; standard methods cited, novel methods fully described.
- **Tense conventions:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Past tense for data/estimation ("we constructed," "we estimated"), present tense for theoretical properties and identification logic.
- **Null results handling:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Null results reported directly as results; no suppression or excessive hedging of non-findings.
- **Conclusion length and content:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Conclusions are short (1–3 pages), restate findings, draw implications, acknowledge limitations, suggest future work. No new results in conclusion.

---

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Mixing citation styles:** Submitting with author-year in-text citations will trigger desk rejection or require complete reformatting. Use numbered brackets [#] exclusively from draft stage onward.

2. **Unstructured abstract:** Free-form abstracts (single paragraph without labeled sections) will be rejected in initial review. Structure as Objective–Methods–Results–Conclusions with line breaks or explicit labels.

3. **Flexible section ordering:** Attempting to defer Methods, combine Introduction and Literature, or reorder Results–Discussion will not pass initial editorial screening. Lock in Introduction–Methods–Results–Discussion–Conclusion order.

4. **Sparse visual presentation:** Submitting with fewer than 5–10 tables/figures is atypical and may signal underdeveloped analytic reporting. Health economics standards expect rich visual support; lean on figures and tables.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: International Journal of Learning and Intellectual Capital

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** HRM&EMP
- **Publisher:** Inderscience Publishers
- **Scope:** Covers organisational learning, knowledge management, intellectual capital measurement and reporting, with emphasis on understanding how knowledge-based resources and intangible assets drive competitive advantage. Interdisciplinary focus spanning HRM, strategy, learning, and organizational performance.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.inderscience.com/mobile/inauthors/index.php?pid=71
- **Field baseline:** hrm-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 8 articles across 2025, 2024, 2023 issues

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Standard article should follow IMRaD-type structure with: title, abstract, keywords, introduction with literature review or theoretical grounding, methods (where applicable), results/findings, discussion, conclusion. Acknowledgements and author biographical notes are required as back matter but author names must be removed from the submitted document itself. Conflicts of interest and informed consent declarations are mandatory components.

### Length
Original articles: **5,000-7,000 words** (excluding figures, tables, and references). High-quality articles exceeding 7,000 words are considered if justified. **ENFORCED**

### Abstract
**Less than 150 words** (approximately 100-150 range in practice). Must cover aim, methodologies, and conclusions. Avoid uncommon abbreviations; if essential, define at first mention. Avoid references unless essential; if unavoidable, cite author and year only. **ENFORCED**

### Citations
Harvard (author-date) system. Full reference list alphabetically ordered at end. All works cited in text must appear in references. References must include: all authors' names and initials, date, title, publication (italicized), volume and issue, page numbers, DOI if available. For websites: add access date and URL (shorten URLs over 90 characters to main domain only; do not use link shorteners). Footnotes discouraged but short succinct numbered notes permitted after reference list. **ENFORCED**

### Headings
Not specified in guidelines beyond standard heading hierarchy implied by IMRaD structure. Practice across articles shows numbered section headings (e.g., 1. Introduction, 2. Literature Review, 3. Methods) and subheadings within sections.

### Keywords
**10-15 words or phrases**. Important for online searching and discoverability. **ENFORCED**

### Tables and Figures
Illustrations (diagrams, photos, charts) referred to as Figures. Colour figures appear in colour online only; print version in black and white. Figures should be black and white unless colour is essential; if colour essential, provide high-quality colour image. All figures placed at end of article, not interspersed in text. Figure quality must accommodate potential 2-3x reduction in lettering size. **ENFORCED**

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Conference papers not accepted as-is; must be substantially revised, rewritten, and expanded with similarity index < 5% compared to original conference paper.
- Briefs and research notes not published in this journal.
- Author names must not appear in document file properties or body of submitted manuscript (removed during blind review).
- AI tools (e.g., ChatGPT) cannot be listed as named authors. If used for writing/editing, must be disclosed in Acknowledgements.

### Ethical Requirements
Conflicts of interest declaration mandatory for all articles. If authors declare no conflicts, must explicitly state "All authors declare that they have no conflicts of interest." Informed consent declaration required if research involves human participants, including clarity on written vs. verbal consent and any waivers. **ENFORCED**

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Scope
IJLIC accepts both empirical (quantitative and qualitative) and conceptual/theoretical papers with less emphasis on explicit hypothesis lists. Articles in the sample show flexible structure: some present hypotheses formally, others frame research questions or conceptual propositions. This aligns with but does not strengthen FIELD_SKILL's observation that qualitative papers use conceptual framing rather than hypothesis lists; IJLIC simply accommodates this variation without mandating either approach. **FIELD DEFAULT**

### Citation Density and Focus
Citation patterns in IJLIC articles follow FIELD_SKILL norms (high in intro/lit review, low in results, rising in discussion). Observed emphasis on citing intellectual capital, knowledge management, and organizational learning frameworks (e.g., references to IC theory, knowledge-based view of the firm, organizational learning literature) alongside HRM and strategic management citations. This represents topic concentration rather than a stylistic delta. **FIELD DEFAULT**

### Statistical Reporting
Articles using quantitative methods (regression, panel models, structural equation modelling, GMM estimation) report results in line with FIELD_SKILL norms: coefficient tables with significance levels, prose restating direction and magnitude without re-deriving statistics. No evidence of stricter econometric demands than FIELD_SKILL describes. One article using hierarchical regression shows robustness checks and model comparison tables; another employs quantile regression. These methods are methodologically sophisticated but consistent with FIELD_SKILL's observation that econometric rigor varies across HRM journals. **FIELD DEFAULT**

### Abstract Length Constraint
IJLIC specifies "less than 150 words" more explicitly and strictly than many ABS 4* journals, which simply require "concise" or "brief" abstracts. Spot-checked abstracts adhere closely to 100-150 word range. This represents a tighter numerical bound than FIELD_SKILL anticipates but not a fundamental reframing. **GUIDELINE ONLY (stated as hard constraint, consistently followed)**

### Human Participants and Ethical Disclosure
IJLIC requires explicit informed consent declarations for any research involving human participants, per COPE guidelines. FIELD_SKILL notes ethical approval statements are "near-universal" for primary data collection in top HRM journals, but IJLIC makes this a categorical requirement with explicit disclosure of consent type (written/verbal) and any ethical waivers. This is a strengthening of FIELD_SKILL's observation rather than a delta. **FIELD DEFAULT**

### Limitations of Public Information
IJLIC as an ABS 1 journal (lower tier than the ABS 4*/4 journals used to derive FIELD_SKILL) does not exhibit marked stylistic deviations. The journal encourages international perspective and clarity for a broad managerial and academic audience but does not impose novel voice, register, or argumentative conventions beyond FIELD_SKILL baseline. **FIELD DEFAULT**

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Voice and person:** FIELD DEFAULT. Formal impersonal register with first-person plural permitted, as per FIELD_SKILL.
- **Sentence and paragraph structure:** FIELD DEFAULT. Medium to long sentences with subordination for qualification and citation; paragraphs organized around claims or themes.
- **Argumentation and contribution:** FIELD DEFAULT. CARS-like introduction structure, explicit contribution statements, blend of theoretical and practical/policy framing.
- **Methods documentation:** FIELD DEFAULT. Quantitative papers detail sample, measures, estimation; qualitative papers detail sampling, access, analytic approach, reflexivity; ethical approval standard.
- **Results and findings reporting:** FIELD DEFAULT. Quantitative results reported via tables and prose; qualitative findings organized thematically with illustrative quotations.
- **Discussion and limitations:** FIELD DEFAULT. Discussion revisits contributions, distinguishes theoretical from practical implications, includes limitations and future directions subsection.
- **Hedging and caution:** FIELD DEFAULT. Moderate to high hedging, especially in discussion. Causal language qualified in observational designs.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Exceeding word count:** Submissions over 7,000 words are considered but may face resistance if not clearly justified; aim for the 5,000-7,000 range to avoid editor requests for compression.

2. **Abstract exceeding 150 words or including unnecessary citations:** IJLIC's abstract limit is stricter than some peer journals; authors familiar with 200-word limits elsewhere should trim aggressively. Avoid citing prior work in the abstract unless absolutely essential to contextualize the gap.

3. **Including author names or affiliations in the main document:** IJLIC operates under double-blind review. Author identifiers in document properties, headers, or body text will delay the review process or require resubmission.

4. **Insufficient disclosure of AI tool use:** If ChatGPT, Copilot, or similar was used for drafting, editing, or text generation, this must be declared in the Acknowledgements. Omission may constitute undisclosed use and ethical violation per COPE guidelines adopted by Inderscience.

5. **Inadequate informed consent or ethical clearance statements:** If human participants are involved, explicit statement of consent type and any waivers is mandatory. Omission will result in editor request for revision before review proceeds.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

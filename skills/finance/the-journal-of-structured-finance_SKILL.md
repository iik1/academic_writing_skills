# Journal Writing Skill: The Journal of Structured Finance

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** FINANCE
- **Publisher:** Portfolio Management Research (Euromoney Institutional Investor)
- **Scope:** The only international, peer-reviewed journal devoted to practical guidance on structured finance instruments, techniques, and strategies. Covers securitization, credit derivatives, MBS, ABS, CDOs, leasing, legal, regulatory, accounting, and tax issues in structured finance.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://pmr-journals.pm-research.com/index.php/JSF/about/submissions
- **Field baseline:** finance-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** Limited to 0 full texts (paywalled); guidelines and editorial practices reviewed.
- **Access limitations:** The journal maintains a paywall on published articles. Most recent content is not freely available. Spot-check of recent articles was not possible.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure

**First page:** Title, date, all author names with complete contact information and affiliations, corresponding author designation.

**Second page:** Title, date, abstract, keywords, and JEL classification codes.

**Third page onward:** Main text.

**Main text follows standard sections:** Introduction → Data/Institutional Background → Analysis/Results → Conclusion.

**Section headings:**
- **Level 1:** Bold, uppercase (e.g., INTRODUCTION)
- **Level 2:** Indented, bold (e.g., _Background_)
- **Level 3:** Bold, inline paragraph headings (e.g., _Category name:_ text continues...)

**Closing roadmap:** FIELD_SKILL standard applies; roadmap expected at end of introduction.

### Length

- **Target:** 4,000 words (excluding abstract, exhibits, references)
- **Acceptable range:** 2,500–7,500 words
- **Abstract word limit:** Approximately 160 words
- **Note:** Online supplements permitted for detailed results and proofs; these do not count toward word limit.

### Abstract

- **Length:** ~160 words
- **Style:** Non-technical, plain English, minimal jargon. Define all acronyms on first use.
- **No references within abstract.**
- **Purpose:** Communicate main points and encourage full-article reading.
- **Format:** Single paragraph (as per FIELD_SKILL).
- **Separate fields:** Keywords and JEL codes appear on second page as separate fields, not embedded in abstract body.

### Citations

**Format:** Author-date (Chicago Manual of Style, 17th ed., Chapter 15). Examples: "(Davis, 2020)" or "Davis (2020) shows that…"

**Footnotes vs. citations:** PMR journals use footnotes rather than endnotes. Footnotes are NOT used for references; they contain peripheral or tangential commentary unsuitable for the main text.

**Reference list:** Full bibliography placed after main text, before exhibits.

### Headings

Following guidelines:
- **Level 1:** Bold, all caps (e.g., DATA AND METHODOLOGY)
- **Level 2:** Indented, bold (regular case; e.g., _Sample Construction_)
- **Level 3:** Bold, inline (e.g., _Inclusion criteria:_ text continues...)

### Tables and Figures

**General:**
- Number consecutively using Arabic numerals (Table 1, Figure 2, etc.)
- Refer to each exhibit in text with clear explanation before or immediately after.
- Ensure legibility in both color and black-and-white printing.
- Place within text at appropriate points (not at end of manuscript).

**Tables:**
- Native Microsoft Word format preferred.
- Align numbers by decimal point.
- Use consistent decimal places and language across all numbers.
- Include source notes identifying data source and sample.
- Define all abbreviations in table footnotes.
- Identify bold or italicized entries in footnotes.
- Points-point table title (e.g., "Summary Statistics of Sample").

**Charts/Figures:**
- Prepare within Microsoft Word when possible.
- Paste as embedded objects (not as pictures) to preserve underlying data integrity.
- Maintain consistent fonts, capitalization, and abbreviations across all charts.
- Label all axes and data series clearly.
- Specify data source and sample in figure captions.

**Equations:**
- Display on separate lines with consistent paragraph indentation.
- Number consecutively using Arabic numerals; place numbering at right margin in parentheses.
- No punctuation follows equations.
- Use text boxes to explain notation or symbols if clarity is needed.
- Preferred submission: Microsoft Word with Equation Editor or MathType.

### Explicit Prohibitions

**Pre-publication distribution:** Authors must NOT post pre-publication versions to SSRN, ResearchGate, file-sharing sites, or distribute at conferences or in course packs before publication. Violations may result in damages assessed at PMR's then-applicable rate for unrestricted distribution rights.

**Post-acceptance removal:** Upon acceptance, authors must remove all pre-publication versions from public websites.

**Copyright transfer:** Authors assign copyright to PMR worldwide upon acceptance. Permitted post-publication uses are limited to: posting title, abstract, and link to published article; using charts/tables/figures in presentation slides only (not in papers, reports, or other publications).

### Language and Tone

**Emphasis:** Plain English, minimal jargon, high clarity. Written for investment professionals (investors, issuers, intermediaries, accountants, lawyers, rating analysts), not academic specialists.

**Required features:**
- Non-technical language in abstract and introduction.
- Practical orientation: real-world applicability and lessons.
- Define industry-specific terms on first use even if standard in finance.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice, Person, and Register

**RELAXATION:** While FIELD_SKILL prescribes confident, assertive language typical of ABS 4*/4 journals, The Journal of Structured Finance shows a **measured, practitioner-focused tone** that emphasizes clarity and accessibility over confidence assertion. The journal targets practitioners (not academics as primary audience), so:

- Confidence calibration is more conservative. Claims about findings are stated but with less emphasis on novelty assertion than FIELD_SKILL recommends.
- First-person plural ("we") is used, but the register is less formal and less technical than ABS 4*/4 norms.
- Practical language and industry terminology replace purely theoretical framing.

**Classification:** RELAXATION (ABS 1 journals typically soften the assertive register of higher tiers).

### Abstract and Introduction

**DELTA — Reduced technical density in abstract:** FIELD_SKILL notes high technical density throughout. JSF abstracts explicitly require plain English and non-technical language, with definitions of acronyms on first use. This is a **structural requirement that softens abstract register significantly.**

**DELTA — Introduction may be shorter:** FIELD_SKILL describes introductions as "the most important section" with extensive positioning. JSF's 4,000-word target (versus longer ABS 4* papers) means introduction proportions may differ. Introduction roadmap remains expected per FIELD_SKILL.

**Classification:** GUIDELINE ONLY (stated in submission guidelines; degree of adherence in published articles unknown due to paywall).

### Citation Density

**DELTA — Potentially lower in introduction:** Because JSF targets practitioners rather than academics, citation density may be **lower in opening paragraphs** compared to FIELD_SKILL baseline. Early heavy citation signals command of literature; practitioner journals emphasize problem statement and practical relevance first, literature positioning second.

**Classification:** PRACTICE ONLY (inferred from audience and scope; not explicitly stated in guidelines).

### Word and Page Limits

**MAJOR DELTA — Significantly shorter target:** FIELD_SKILL does not specify limits (deliberately excluded as journal-specific). JSF has a **4,000-word target** (range 2,500–7,500). This is shorter than typical ABS 4*/4 papers, which may force:
- Shorter introduction and methods sections.
- Results and mechanism discussion condensed or merged.
- Robustness and appendix material redistributed to online supplements.

**Classification:** ENFORCED (stated explicitly in guidelines).

### Footnote Use

**DELTA:** FIELD_SKILL is silent on footnotes (not specified for finance field). JSF **requires footnotes rather than endnotes** and explicitly states footnotes are for **peripheral or tangential commentary only, not for references.** This differs from some economics journals.

**Classification:** ENFORCED (stated in guidelines: "PMR journals use footnotes rather than endnotes, with footnotes generally not used for references").

### Results and Statistical Reporting

Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Tables are primary vehicle; prose guides the reader. Economic significance emphasized alongside statistical significance.

### Audience-Specific Adjustments

**DELTA:** FIELD_SKILL assumes academic specialist audience. JSF explicitly targets **investment professionals, lawyers, accountants, rating analysts, and practitioners** — not PhD economists. This shapes:
- Avoidance of econometric jargon without definition (parallel trends, identification, fixed effects, etc.).
- Emphasis on practical implications over methodological novelty.
- Plain-English abstract requirement (vs. technical abstracts in ABS 4*).

**Classification:** PRACTICE ONLY (audience-driven, not stated as a constraint but evident in scope and abstract requirements).

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Overestimating technical depth:** Submitting with econometric jargon and theoretical framing unsuitable for practitioner audience. JSF abstracts explicitly require plain English. Avoid "identification strategy," "fixed effects," "parallel trends" without clear definition or reframe in practitioner terms.

2. **Misplacing references in footnotes:** PMR journals prohibit reference citations in footnotes. Footnotes are for tangential discussion only. Use the reference list exclusively for citations.

3. **Pre-publication distribution without removal:** Posting to SSRN, ResearchGate, or conference sites during review and failing to remove after acceptance triggers copyright penalties. JSF's copyright transfer is strict.

4. **Ignoring word limit:** Exceeding 7,500 words without justification may result in desk rejection. Plan for condensed methods and potential online appendices.

5. **Weak practical contribution:** JSF values actionable findings for practitioners. Papers framed as purely theoretical contributions or with implications limited to academic debate may receive lower priority.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

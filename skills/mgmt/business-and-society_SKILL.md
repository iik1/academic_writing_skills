# Journal Writing Skill: Business and Society

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN
- **Publisher:** SAGE (in association with the International Association for Business and Society)
- **Scope:** Leading outlet for scholarship on the intersection of business and society — CSR, corporate sustainability, stakeholder management, business ethics, corporate political activity, business-government relations, and social entrepreneurship/innovation. ## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No mandated template; headings and section divisions must simply be "clear" and "clearly defined." The journal recognizes four distinct manuscript categories, each with its own length and process, which is a structural feature FIELD_SKILL does not address:
- **Full-length article**: standard research article.
- **Research note**: shorter empirical/theoretical contribution.
- **Commentary**: requires an invited pitch (submitted separately, reviewed by Commentary editors) before a full submission is accepted.
- **Perspectives**: concise, forward-looking viewpoint pieces.

### Length
- Full-length articles: 7,000–12,000 words at initial submission, including tables, figures, and references. Manuscripts outside this range are returned without review.
- Research notes: maximum 5,000 words.
- Commentaries: maximum 1,500 words, up to 5 references, invitation-only (preceded by a 1-page pitch: tentative title, 30–40 word summary, key points, and a statement of claimed novelty).
- Perspectives: 5,000–7,000 words.

### Abstract
Unstructured, exactly 150 words, placed between title and main body. Must concisely state purpose, major findings, and conclusions. Submissions that do not meet this word count "will not be considered" — stricter and more specific than FIELD_SKILL's general 150–250 word range.

### Citations
APA reference style. Every in-text citation must have an exact corresponding reference-list entry (matching spelling and year) and vice versa. Preprints should not be cited once a peer-reviewed version exists; citing preprints is otherwise discouraged.

### Headings
No fixed heading template — author discretion, provided heading levels are unambiguous.

### Tables and Figures
Figures numbered consecutively in order of in-text appearance (this order is preserved in publication). Figure resolution: 300dpi. Color figures are free online; print color incurs a cost. Permissions must be secured and documented for any previously published tables/figures/illustrations/lengthy quotations.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- No preprint submissions accepted (journal does not consider manuscripts posted to preprint servers).
- Manuscript must be original, not under review elsewhere, with all necessary third-party permissions in hand.
- AI chatbots (e.g., ChatGPT) must not be listed as authors; any generative-AI use must be disclosed per SAGE's AI-use policy.
- Third-party submissions (e.g., a communications company submitting on the authors' behalf) may be refused by the editor unless disclosed in the cover letter and Acknowledgments.
- Title page (with all identifying info) must be fully separated from the anonymized main manuscript — double-anonymized review is strictly enforced, including a "Statements and Declarations" block (Ethical considerations, Consent to participate, Consent for publication, Conflict of interest, Funding — even if "none") that appears with every submission, with "Not applicable" required where a heading does not apply.
- A funding statement is mandatory on every submission, even absent funding.

### Structural Deviations
PRACTICE ONLY / ENFORCED (guideline-level): the four-tier article-type system (full article / research note / commentary / perspective), each with its own word cap and, for Commentaries, a mandatory pre-submission pitch stage — is a journal-specific structural feature not present in FIELD_SKILL, which only distinguishes quantitative/qualitative/theoretical/review types by content, not by a formal editorial submission track. RELAXATION (ABS 3 note): the 7,000–12,000 word ceiling for full articles is materially shorter than FIELD_SKILL's typical 10,000–18,000 word range for empirical/qualitative management papers.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
ENFORCED: unusually granular front-matter requirements beyond FIELD_SKILL's coverage — mandatory "Statements and Declarations" section with explicit sub-headings (Ethical considerations, Consent to participate, Consent for publication, Conflict of interest, Funding), each required to be present (with "Not applicable" if not relevant), and a strict double-anonymization protocol for the title page separate from the manuscript body.

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting an abstract that is not exactly ~150 words and unstructured — guidelines state noncompliant submissions "will not be considered."
- Treating word limits loosely: full articles outside the strict 7,000–12,000 word band (inclusive of tables/figures/references) are returned before review.
- Omitting the "Statements and Declarations" block or any of its required sub-headings, including a funding statement when there was no funding.
- Submitting a preprint-posted manuscript, which this journal does not accept.
- Pitching a Commentary as a full manuscript instead of following the required pre-submission pitch process.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

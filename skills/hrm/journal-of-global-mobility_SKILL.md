# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Global Mobility

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** HRM&EMP
- **Publisher:** Emerald Publishing Limited
- **Scope:** Research on global mobility and all types of global employees, including corporate expatriates, self-initiated expatriates, inpatriates, international business travellers, short-term assignees, and international commuters. Welcomes empirical (quantitative and/or qualitative), rigorous theoretical, and focused literature review submissions.
- **Guidelines URL:** Author guidelines not directly accessible without login; based on Emerald Publishing standard practices and published article analysis.
- **Field baseline:** hrm-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 4 recent articles from 2023-2026 (title verification and structural analysis from published PDF and metadata; full-text access limited by paywall)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows standard IMRaD-with-theory structure. Empirical and qualitative articles typically include:
- Title page with journal name, manuscript ID (assigned at submission), manuscript type (Research Paper, Conceptual, Literature Review, etc.), and keywords
- Structured abstract
- Keywords (separate line)
- Introduction
- Literature review and/or theoretical background
- Methodology section (for empirical work; optional for conceptual/literature review)
- Findings/Results section
- Discussion
- Conclusion
- References

Qualitative papers may organize findings thematically rather than by hypothesis. Acknowledgements, ethics statements, and author contribution statements are treated as standard back matter.

### Length
Typical articles appear to range 8,000–12,000 words; no explicit maximum stated in accessible guidelines. Emerald standard for journal articles is 5,000–8,000 words, but Journal of Global Mobility articles observed exceed this, suggesting higher limits may apply. **Verify with journal submission page before drafting.**

### Abstract
Structured abstract (required) with 4–7 subsections:
- **Purpose:** Research aim and contribution
- **Design/methodology/approach:** Methods employed (quantitative, qualitative, ethnographic, interviews, etc.)
- **Findings:** Key results
- **Originality/value:** Novelty and contribution to field
- **Research limitation(s):** Stated limitations of scope or method (near-universal in observed articles)
- Optional: **Practical/social implications** (observed in some articles)

Maximum 250 words total (including abstract and keywords). No separate keywords subsection; keywords listed immediately after abstract as a paragraph or line.

### Citation Style
Harvard referencing (author-date). Examples:
- Paraphrase: (Author, Year)
- Direct quotation: (Author, Year: page number)
- Multiple authors: Use "and" in running text; use "&" in parentheses. For 3+ authors at first citation, cite all; at subsequent citations, use "et al."

### Headings
Hierarchical heading system:
- **Level 1:** Bold, full-width (introduction, methods, discussion, etc.)
- **Level 2:** Bold, left-justified (subsections within each main section)
- Level 3 (if needed): Italicized, left-justified

No numbered headings observed.

### Tables and Figures
- Tables and figures embedded within text at point of reference
- Captions below tables, above figures
- Tables use simple grid format; avoid excessive shading
- Figures should be clear, high-resolution; diagrams acceptable
- All tables and figures must have descriptive captions and be referenced in text

### Explicit Prohibitions
None stated beyond standard academic integrity (avoid plagiarism, disclose funding, obtain ethics approval for primary data collection).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Methodology Reporting – Qualitative Work
**PRACTICE ONLY** (observed, not stated): Qualitative articles explicitly report ethical approval, sampling rationale, data collection sites/activities, and reflexivity. One observed article (ethnography) included 86 pages of fieldwork diary notes, detailed observation locations (shopping malls, cafes, gyms, private homes), and named coding software (MAXQDA). Validity checking with colleagues is mentioned. This is consistent with FIELD_SKILL but may be slightly more detailed/rigorous in execution than field baseline.

### Participant/Sample Presentation
**PRACTICE ONLY**: Sample characteristics table (demographics, expatriate status, origin, interview language) is standard in qualitative work. Not a stated requirement but observed in all qualitative articles reviewed.

### Manuscript Type Declaration
**ENFORCED**: Manuscript must be labeled as Research Paper, Conceptual Paper, Literature Review, or Editorial/Guest Editorial at submission. This is metadata, not part of the text, but required for tracking and peer-review assignment.

### Thematic Organization in Findings (Qualitative)
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm: findings organized around research questions or emergent themes rather than linear reporting. One observed article organized findings around four overarching themes: Dubai at first sight, Beyond first sight, Cultural context, Expatriate bubbles. No deviation from field baseline.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Unstructured abstract:** Abstract must have explicit subsections (Purpose, Design/methodology/approach, Findings, Originality/value). Single-paragraph abstracts will be desk-rejected or returned for revision.

2. **Missing ethics statement in qualitative/primary-data research:** Articles collecting primary data (interviews, ethnography, surveys) must include a statement of ethics approval or explicit note that ethics approval was not required. Absence is treated as a red flag.

3. **Weak originality claim:** Journal emphasizes empirical and theoretical contribution. Failure to explicitly state what is novel (e.g., "first ethnographic study of X from Y perspective," "extends Z theory to new context") will result in desk desk-reject or request for major revision. See Originality subsection of structured abstract and discussion/conclusion.

4. **Nationality/passport status as research variable without acknowledging power dynamics:** Given the journal's focus on global mobility and expatriate hierarchies, studies that mention country of origin or education background without reflexive discussion of privilege, positionality, or racialization may face critical review. Even quantitative work should acknowledge these dimensions in limitations.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

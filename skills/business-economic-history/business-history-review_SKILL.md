# Journal Writing Skill: Business History Review

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 4
- **Field:** BUS HIST & ECON HIST
- **Publisher:** Cambridge University Press (editorial office at Harvard Business School)
- **Scope:** Seeks articles from rigorous primary research addressing major debates and
  offering comparative perspectives on entrepreneurs, firms, and business systems, and
  on innovation, globalization, regulation, and the relation of business to political
  regimes and the environment.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-file-manager/file/5d3ad9ce7192a5cf08569a6d/BHR-ifc.pdf (Instructions for Contributors, last updated 22 March 2019); https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/business-history-review/information/author-instructions
- **Field baseline:** business-economic-history-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 9 (Nissen 2025 "From Oil Nation to Wind Power Nation?"; Hansen 2025 "Making Sense of the 1931 Financial Crisis and the Great Depression"; Matringe 2025/2026 "The Art of Distrust"; Colpan & Üsdiken 2025 "Continuity and Change in Turkey's Largest Industrial Enterprises, 1970–2010"; Field 2025 "The World War II US Rubber Famine"; Michney 2025 "Black Life Insurance Companies, Mortgages, and African American Homeownership before 1964"; Toivanen & Jensen-Eriksen 2025 "Pioneering the Circular Economy"; an unattributed full-text article "The Peculiar, Diverse, and Vital Business of Personal Mortgages, 1890s–1950s"; plus title-level review of the full Vol. 99 Issues 3–4 table of contents)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Subheads must divide the manuscript into three or four sections (or more,
depending on length). No fixed named-section template beyond this.

### Length
Articles must not exceed 10,000 words, including footnotes.

### Abstract
Unstructured abstract of no more than 100 words, outlining the paper's
main point(s) and placing the article in context. A separate short author
bio (3–4 sentences, stating affiliation and recent publications) is
required at submission.

### Citations
Chicago Manual of Style, 17th edition (2017), footnotes. No loc. cit.,
op. cit., idem., or ibid. — use shortened citation form instead (e.g.,
"McCraw, Prophet of Innovation, 205–21." then "McCraw, 205–210."). Do not
include the publisher in book citations. Dates in citations use
day-month-year format (e.g., "11 February 2007"). Double quotation marks
for journal article titles and direct quotation; single quotation marks
for quotes within quotes. Authors must not identify themselves in the
manuscript body — self-citations are third person, without possessive
pronouns (not "See my...").

### Headings
No specific heading-label conventions beyond the three-or-four-section
minimum; spelling/hyphenation follows Merriam-Webster's Collegiate
Dictionary.

### Tables and Figures
All illustrative material is labeled "Figure" (not "Chart," "Diagram,"
etc.), numbered consecutively in Arabic numerals. Each table and figure
must carry a complete source note. Authors must supply data files with
figures and are responsible for obtaining reproduction permissions and
writing captions. Tables submitted in Word format for in-house editing.
Color figures publish in color online but black-and-white in print.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Manuscripts under concurrent consideration elsewhere, or previously
published material (substance or form), are prohibited. Generative
AI cannot be listed as an author, and undeclared AI-generated text or
imagery is treated as a plagiarism violation. Gender-neutral prose is
required wherever not anachronistic (no male nouns/pronouns for people of
both sexes). Book reviews (separate track): 800–1,000 words, review
essays 1,500–2,000 words, no footnotes, parenthetical page citations only.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
The impersonal/third-person register FIELD_SKILL flags as common in
single-authored archival business history also dominates in this
journal's multi-authored and quantitative economic-history pieces (e.g.,
Michney; Colpan & Üsdiken), rather than shifting toward first-person
plural as FIELD_SKILL's general pattern would suggest. PRACTICE ONLY —
sample skews toward impersonal framing across article types, but the
guidelines are silent on voice, so this is not an enforced rule.

### Abstract Format
FIELD_SKILL notes abstracts are compact, non-technical summaries; this
journal enforces a hard 100-word ceiling, tighter than typical for the
field. ENFORCED (stated in guidelines).

### Structural Deviations
FIELD_SKILL describes flexible section organization (chronological or
thematic) without prescribing a minimum count. This journal requires at
least three or four labeled subsections regardless of article length.
ENFORCED. Observed articles (e.g., the personal-mortgages piece, with six
named sections) comply.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Self-anonymization requirement (no first-person self-citation, no "See
my...") is specific to this journal's guidelines and not addressed in
FIELD_SKILL. ENFORCED.

## Common Pitfalls
- Exceeding the 100-word abstract limit (guidelines are strict and explicit on this, unlike most economics journals).
- Self-identifying via first-person possessive self-citation ("as I show in my earlier work") — this journal requires third-person self-reference for anonymized review.
- Using "Ibid." or op. cit./loc. cit. — the journal has explicitly moved to shortened-citation form instead.
- Treating a book review submission like a research article: reviews have their own word limits (800–1,000, or 1,500–2,000 for essays) and use parenthetical page citations with no footnotes at all.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

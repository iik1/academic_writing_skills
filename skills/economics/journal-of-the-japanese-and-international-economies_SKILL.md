# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of the Japanese and International Economies

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Elsevier
- **Scope:** Original reports of research devoted to academic analyses of the Japanese economy and its interdependence on other national economies, featuring theoretical, empirical, and comparative analyses with policy implications.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/journal-of-the-japanese-and-international-economies/publish/guide-for-authors (paywalled; content extracted from search results)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 11 (2024–2025 publications; access via arXiv, IDEAS/RePEc, and search snippets)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Manuscripts must include: Abstract, Keywords, Introduction, Materials and Methods (or Empirical Strategy/Identification for empirical papers), Results, Conclusions. Acknowledgements appear in a separate section directly before the reference list. An appendix is standard for robustness checks, supplementary analyses, and proofs.

### Length
No explicit word or page limits stated in accessible guidelines.

### Abstract
- **Format:** Single paragraph
- **Length:** Observed abstracts run approximately 150–160 words (no explicit limit found in guidelines)
- **Content:** Typically opens with data source and methodology, progresses to findings, concludes with implications or elasticity results

### Keywords
- **Requirement:** 1 to 7 keywords (ENFORCED - stated in guidelines)
- **Format:** Avoid multi-word phrases using "and" or "of"; use single-word or tightly-coupled terms (GUIDELINE ONLY - stated but not universally enforced in articles)
- **Abbreviations:** Avoid unless firmly established in the field (GUIDELINE ONLY)

### Citations
- **Style:** Author-date format, parenthetical or in-text (ENFORCED - confirmed in guidelines and all articles)
- **References list:** Arranged first alphabetically, then chronologically for same-author, same-year entries; use letters "a", "b", "c" to distinguish (ENFORCED)
- **Journal names:** Follow Journal of Economic Literature Abbreviations (ENFORCED)

### Headings
- **Numbering:** Numbered sections (Arabic or Roman numerals) standard; observed articles use Arabic numerals (PRACTICE ONLY)
- **Capitalization:** Sentence case observed in subsection headings (PRACTICE ONLY)
- **Example observed:** Section 1. Introduction; 1.1 Literature Review; 5. Results; 5.1 Matching Efficiency and Elasticity

### Tables and Figures
Standard academic practice. No special or unusual conventions noted. Follow FIELD_SKILL baseline.

### JEL Classification
- **Requirement:** Include JEL codes (ENFORCED - appears in all examined articles, though not explicitly stated in accessible guidelines; standard Elsevier economics practice)
- **Range:** Typically 3–5 codes per article (PRACTICE ONLY)

### Explicit Prohibitions
None identified beyond standard research ethics (originality, disclosure of conflicts of interest, ethical approval where required).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Voice and Register in Results Sections
**Delta (PRACTICE ONLY):** While FIELD_SKILL baseline emphasizes "we" as near-universal in economics, this journal shows *higher prevalence of passive voice and third-person construction in results sections* compared to top-tier outlets. Example observed: "matching efficiency on the private platform is analyzed" rather than "we analyze matching efficiency." The "we" construction appears more frequently in methodology sections and is retained in interpretive passages, but results reporting shifts more toward passive/impersonal frames. This is consistent with ABS 2 tier relaxation of ABS 4*/4 norms.

### Hedging in Results
**Delta (PRACTICE ONLY):** Moderate hedging language ("suggests," "may indicate," "potentially," "appears") is more common in results interpretation than FIELD_SKILL baseline implies. FIELD_SKILL states "clear claims are strongly preferred" and hedging is used narrowly for external validity, causal interpretation limits, and data limitations. Articles in this journal show hedging applied more broadly to interpretations of mechanisms and relationships, though not to an extent that obscures findings. This is likely a tier-related relaxation, not a formal requirement.

### Citation Density in Results
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (low to medium in results sections, with citations appearing when comparing magnitudes with prior estimates).

- **Sentence and Paragraph Style:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (medium to long sentences, high paragraph density, high technical vocabulary density)
- **Argumentation and Rhetorical Moves:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (introduction announces results, identifies gap, states contributions, includes roadmap)
- **Methods Conventions:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (mixed tense logic, sufficient procedural detail, identification strategy explicitly stated, robustness checks standard)
- **Statistical Reporting:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (quantitative specificity, economic significance expected alongside statistical significance, null results reported)
- **Discussion and Conclusion:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (genuine synthesis, policy/theoretical implications, limitations acknowledged, future research suggested)

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Keyword format**: Submitting multi-word keywords joined by "and" or "of" (e.g., "labor market dynamics and wage differentials") when single-word or minimal-phrase terms are preferred (e.g., "labor markets," "wage premiums"). This is not a hard rejection but increases likelihood of editorial requests.

2. **Missing JEL codes**: While not explicitly stated in the most accessible guidelines, all recent articles include JEL classification codes. Failure to include them is a submission oversight that may trigger a revision request.

3. **Over-hedging results**: Using excessive qualifiers ("may possibly suggest that the evidence could indicate") on core findings weakens the paper's impact. Hedge external validity and mechanism interpretation, but state quantitative results directly.

4. **Unclear identification strategy** (for empirical papers): Reviewers expect a dedicated Identification or Empirical Strategy subsection clearly stating assumptions. Embedding it within a Methods section risks reviewer confusion.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

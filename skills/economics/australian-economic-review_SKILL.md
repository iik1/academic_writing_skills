# Journal Writing Skill: Australian Economic Review

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Wiley-Blackwell (official journal of the Melbourne Institute of Applied Economic and Social Research, University of Melbourne)
- **Scope:** Applied economics journal with a strong policy orientation; publishes economic analysis of macro and micro topics relevant to Australian economic and social policy, with results explained for the generalist economist while retaining enough technical detail for specialist referees.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/page/journal/14678462/homepage/forauthors.html (current Wiley "Free Format" submission page, thin on detail); supplemented by the journal's own detailed "Guide to Authors" PDF (legacy but appears to remain the in-house style reference): https://www.blackwellpublishing.com/pdf/AERE_author_guide_21july2010.pdf
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 11 (abstracts/front matter via RePEc/IDEAS, Wiley abstract pages, and search-engine-extracted snippets — full text paywalled). Sample: Ballantyne & Cusbert "The NAIRU Under Anchored Inflation Expectations" (2025); Tsiaplias "Introduction to the Policy Forum on the NAIRU" (2025); Murad & Rahman "Governance Quality Under Economic Uncertainty" (2025); Zeng & Fox "Productivity Measurement With Big Data" (2025); Garnaut & Vines "Monetary Policy Mistakes and Remedies" (2023, labelled "Special Article"); Gerrans, Kristoffersen & Earl "Great (Retirement) Expectations" (2026); Duong "Inflation Propagation in Production" (2026); Duong "The Time-Varying Anchoring of Inflation Expectations in Australia" (2026); Borland "Decline to Boom to Slowdown: Australia's Labour Market in the COVID-19 Era" (2026); Greenwood-Nimmo et al. "The Australian Economy in 2025-26" (2026, annual-review type); Lim et al. "The Australian Economy in 2024-2025" (2025, annual-review type).

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Required sections: abstract, introduction, body sections (methods/data/results as applicable), conclusion — all sections must be titled, including "1. Introduction" itself. Consecutive Arabic numeral section/subsection numbering (e.g., 1., 1.1, 1.1.1). Appendices numbered separately (Appendix 1, Appendix 2...) with continuous A-prefixed numbering for tables/equations/figures across appendices. Distinct recurring article types beyond standard research articles: **Policy Forum** pieces (themed clusters with a short editor's "Introduction to the Policy Forum on X"), an annual **"The Australian Economy in [year]"** survey/outlook piece, and occasional **"Special Article"**-labelled pieces (e.g., RBA-review commentary) that are more essay-like and policy-facing than a standard empirical paper.

### Length
Papers should not normally exceed 30 A4 pages, double-spaced, single-sided. Very long papers will not be considered. No separate limit is stated per article type in accessible sources.

### Abstract
Unstructured abstract of **up to 100 words** (per the journal's own Guide to Authors) summarising the central argument or findings — notably shorter than is typical elsewhere in ECON. Also requires a separate short description (fewer than 30 words) for the contents page. Note: a generic Wiley template page surfaced in search results claims a 200-word *structured* abstract (Research Question/Findings/Policy Implications/Methods headings) — this appears to be boilerplate from a different Wiley journal's guidelines template, not confirmed by the journal's own Guide to Authors or by observed abstracts, which are unstructured. Treat the 100-word unstructured form as authoritative; flag the structured-abstract claim as unconfirmed.

### Citations
Harvard author-year in text (surname + year, page ref if needed; no initials in-text). Full alphabetical reference list at end of article, after endnotes. Legislation and legal authorities are cited in-text/footnotes in full legal-citation form and explicitly excluded from the reference list. Journal offers "Free Format" submission — authors need not format references to house style at submission, but accepted papers are typeset to it.

### Headings
Arabic numerals for all section/subsection levels, headings in sentence-style upper/lower case (not all-caps or title-case-every-word), every section titled including the introduction.

### Tables and Figures
Placed at the end of the manuscript (not embedded in text) with captions in upper/lower case (e.g., "Table 1 Growth, Wages and Unions"). All diagrams termed "Figures." Tabbed (not space-separated) table entries; Excel files requested where possible. Source line required at the base of each table/figure. Table notes use consecutive lowercase Roman letters (a, b, c) rather than numerals, to avoid confusion with in-table numbers.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- No papers already published or under review elsewhere.
- No papers that are "mainly descriptive," minor variations of existing studies, or of limited interest — explicitly stated as grounds for non-consideration.
- Replication requirement: data must be documented and available to researchers for replication; sufficient computational detail must be supplied.
- Endnotes must not be used to list references.
- Avoid ellipses in quotations; quote extracts must preserve original punctuation/spelling exactly.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
Explicit stated audience constraint not found in FIELD_SKILL: results must be "explained in a way that can be understood by the generalist economist," with technical/specialist detail relegated to appendices or supplementary material for referees. This is a real accessibility requirement, not merely practice — confirmed by observed abstracts (e.g., Murad & Rahman, Ballantyne & Cusbert) which foreground plain-language framing of findings before any technical description. ENFORCED.

### Statistical Reporting
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm on integrating results and interpretation; no delta observed in the sample.

### Citation Density
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm; abstracts/front matter show no unusual density pattern.

### Structural Deviations
The "Australian Economy in [year]" annual outlook piece and "Special Article" / "Policy Forum" formats are recurring, named article types distinct from the standard empirical/theory paper structure in FIELD_SKILL — these are shorter, more discursive, policy-commentary pieces without a full Data/Empirical Strategy/Results architecture. PRACTICE/ENFORCED (named explicitly in the journal's table of contents and forum introductions).

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Australian/British spelling is mandatory (Oxford Spelling Dictionary as guide; "-ise" and "-our" endings, e.g. "summarise," "labour") — not addressed in FIELD_SKILL, which is US-spelling-agnostic. ENFORCED per the Guide to Authors.

Abstract length (100 words) is markedly shorter than typical ABS 4*/4 practice implied by FIELD_SKILL's silence on the matter; treat as a RELAXATION appropriate to this journal's shorter, more accessible article format rather than a new field norm.

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting an abstract in the 150-250 word range common at ABS 4/4* journals — this journal's own guide caps it at 100 words.
- Using US spelling ("summarize," "labor") instead of the required Australian/British forms.
- Writing for a specialist-only audience without a generalist-accessible framing of results, which the journal explicitly requires.
- Embedding tables/figures inline instead of placing them at the end of the manuscript with source lines.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

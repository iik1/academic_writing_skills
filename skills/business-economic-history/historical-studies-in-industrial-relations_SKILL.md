# Journal Writing Skill: Historical Studies in Industrial Relations

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** BUS HIST & ECON HIST
- **Publisher:** Liverpool University Press
- **Scope:** Journal established in 1996 by the Centre for Industrial Relations, Keele University. Publishes historical work in industrial relations, covering the employment relationship, labour markets, union and employer policies, labour law, and gender and ethnicity in industrial relations contexts. Welcomes articles with explicit political dimensions and historical work on labour law.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.liverpooluniversitypress.co.uk/journal/hsir (full guidelines not publicly accessible)
- **Field baseline:** business-economic-history-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 5-8 (limited access; sampled via publisher abstracts and article listings for volumes 45-46)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict. Note: The journal's full author guidelines are not publicly accessible. Information below is derived from publisher house policies and observed article structures.

### Structure
The journal accepts three article types: Research Articles, Essays, and Documents. Research articles and essays follow standard scholarly structure with introduction, substantive sections, and conclusion. No rigid section-heading requirements observed; structure follows FIELD_SKILL narrative-analytical mode.

### Length
Not publicly specified. No explicit word limits found in available guidelines; typical articles in recent volumes (45–46) appear to range 6,000–10,000 words.

### Abstract
Not explicitly specified. Articles observed include abstracts; format appears to be compact, non-structured narrative summary (consistent with FIELD_SKILL).

### Citations
Liverpool University Press house style uses author–date or author–short title referencing. No ibid., op. cit., or art. cit. Used. Full reference on first appearance; shortened form thereafter. British English spelling and Oxford punctuation conventions (en dashes with spaces: "Monday–Friday").

### Headings
No explicit guidelines found. Observed articles use numbered or unnumbered section headings in title case, consistent with FIELD_SKILL practice.

### Tables and Figures
No explicit guidelines found. Articles include tables and figures with captions; sourced and interpreted in prose (FIELD_SKILL standard).

### Explicit Prohibitions
Journal adheres to COPE Core Practices. Double-anonymous peer review. Do not use ibid., op. cit., or art. cit. in citations. British English required (Oxford style).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### British English and House Style
RELAXATION: Liverpool University Press enforces British English spelling and punctuation (Oxford style) throughout all journals. Use British forms ("labour" not "labor"; "-ise" not "-ize"; "organisation" not "organization"). Use en dashes with spaces before and after ("Monday–Friday") and three points for omitted text ("..."). This is a publisher-level requirement, not a journal innovation, but marks a stylistic divergence from some North American field practice.

### Citation Style (Author-Date or Author-Short Title)
ENFORCED (Publisher house style): Liverpool University Press journals use either author–date (e.g., Smith 2020) or author–short title (e.g., Smith, "Title," 2020) referencing. Do NOT use footnote or endnote citations for bibliography references. Footnotes may be used for substantive comments but not for citation. This contrasts with FIELD_SKILL's note-heavy historiographical practice and represents a shift toward author-date style more typical of social science.

### Article Types and Formal Sections
PRACTICE ONLY: The journal explicitly recognizes and publishes three article types — Research Articles, Essays, and Documents — each with potentially different expectations. Essays may be more interpretive and less structured than Research Articles. Documents (archival materials, interviews, transcribed proceedings) have their own section. FIELD_SKILL does not distinguish article types formally, but this journal does.

### Scope and Political Framing
PRACTICE ONLY: Articles with "explicit political dimension" are explicitly encouraged, particularly those recognising "divisions within the working class and within workers' organisations." This is slightly stronger than FIELD_SKILL's general openness to revisionist framing and may encourage more overt political positioning than some top-tier journals in the field.

### "Documents" as Published Content
PRACTICE ONLY: The journal publishes Documents (archival materials, interviews, oral history transcripts) alongside research articles and essays. This is not a standard FIELD_SKILL category and reflects the journal's role as a venue for primary-source publication, not only interpretation.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Mixing citation styles**: Do not use both footnotes for citation and an author-date bibliography. Pick one style (author-date or author-short title) and use it consistently. Liverpool University Press house style prohibits ibid., op. cit., and similar shortened forms across subsequent citations.

2. **American English spelling**: This journal is published by a UK press and enforces British English. Check for -ise/-ize, -our/-or, and labour/labor consistency. Use British spellings throughout.

3. **Submitting to the wrong editor**: Papers should be sent to the Lead Editor, Paul Smith (Keele University), via the Liverpool University Press submission portal. Verify the current submission email or online submission system before sending.

4. **Unclear political or labour-history positioning**: This journal explicitly welcomes articles with "explicit political dimension" and values work that recognises class divisions and labour movement contestation. Do not assume neutrality is valued; position your argument clearly within labour history and IR scholarship debates.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

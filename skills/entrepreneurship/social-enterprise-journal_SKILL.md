# Journal Writing Skill: Social Enterprise Journal

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ENT-SBM
- **Publisher:** Emerald Publishing
- **Scope:** Interdisciplinary, critical studies of social enterprise and social entrepreneurship; the first journal dedicated specifically to social enterprise research.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.emerald.com/sej (author guidelines behind Emerald's ScholarOne portal)
- **Field baseline:** entrepreneurship-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 11 (2021–2026; mix of empirical, conceptual, and case study formats)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Introduction–Methodology–Results–Discussion–Conclusion is the standard spine for empirical work. Sections may be presented with first-level headings in bold and subheadings in italics. Literature review may be integrated into Introduction or presented as a separate section. No fixed requirement for a standalone "Contributions" section; contributions are often woven into Discussion and Conclusion.

### Length
Manuscript word count is not strictly specified in publicly accessible guidelines; Emerald's general journal standard is 6,000–10,000 words including tables, figures, and all text. Emerald encourages concision but does not enforce a rigid limit per journal.

### Abstract
**Mandatory structured format:** 250 words maximum total. Four mandatory sections (Purpose, Design/Methodology/Approach, Findings, Originality/Value). Three optional sections (Research Limitations/Implications, Practical Implications, Social Implications) may be omitted if not applicable. Each section should be concise and focused.

### Citations
Harvard referencing style. Two-author citations cite both names at every occurrence. Three to five authors: cite all at first mention, then surname of first author + "et al." thereafter. Six or more authors: first author surname + "et al." from first mention onward. Article titles in quotation marks; journal names emphasized.

### Headings
First-level headings in bold. Subheadings in medium italics. No prescribed limit on heading depth, but hierarchy should support clarity. Headings should be short and descriptive.

### Tables and Figures
Tables numbered with Roman numerals. Figures in high-resolution formats (.ai, .eps, .jpeg, .bmp, .tif). Captions should be descriptive and self-contained. No explicit restrictions on table/figure density observed in sampled articles.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Author names and institutional affiliations must be removed from the main manuscript for double-blind peer review; place in separate file.
- No submission by email or third-party agents; ScholarOne submission only.
- Any acknowledgments should be in a separate file to maintain anonymity during review.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
**RELAXATION:** First-person plural voice is common but not universal. Passive constructions appear more frequently than in ABS 4*/4 journals, particularly in the Methods section and in reporting of results. When describing research procedures, passive voice (e.g., "data were collected," "participants were recruited") is standard. First-person active voice ("we interviewed," "we conducted") appears selectively, particularly when describing researcher choices or analytical decisions. Overall register is formal but less uniformly rhetorical than the field baseline; explicit signposting of contributions is less mandatory. **Classification: RELAXATION** — the journal tolerates greater voice variation and passive voice use than the FIELD_SKILL baseline prescribes.

### Statistical Reporting
**PRACTICE ONLY:** Effect sizes are reported less consistently than in the FIELD_SKILL baseline. Correlation tables and descriptive statistics are standard, but reporting of Cohen's f-squared or R-squared change is variable; significance tests and point estimates dominate. Standard errors are sometimes omitted in favor of confidence intervals or simple p-value reporting. **Classification: PRACTICE ONLY** — observed variation suggests no enforced guideline, but statistical reporting is less rigorous than top-tier journals.

### Hedging and Epistemic Caution
**FIELD DEFAULT with minor variation:** Moderate hedging with epistemic markers ("may," "could," "suggests," "appears to") is present throughout, though perhaps slightly elevated in Discussion sections. Causal claims are softened and limitations explicitly discussed, consistent with the field norm. Forward-looking hedges ("future research should examine") close limitations sections. No delta from FIELD_SKILL on this dimension.

### Citation Density and Engagement
**RELAXATION:** Citation density in Introduction and Theory sections is notably lower than in ABS 4*/4 journals. Supporting citations per major claim are fewer (often single or dual citations rather than multiple). Disagreement in the literature is handled descriptively and neutrally ("some research finds X, other work suggests Y"), consistent with the field baseline. Paraphrase dominates; direct quotation is rare. Self-citation and engagement with foundational theorists is present but less visibly foregrounded than in the FIELD_SKILL baseline. **Classification: RELAXATION** — the journal permits leaner citation footprints, particularly in Introduction and Literature sections.

### Structural Deviations
**PRACTICE ONLY:** Some empirical articles combine Results and Discussion into a single section rather than separating them. Practical/Managerial Implications subsections appear inconsistently; some articles address practitioner relevance within Discussion rather than as a standalone subsection. Limitations and Future Research may be collapsed into one brief section rather than treated as extensive separate subsections. The "Contributions" section is not always a distinct heading; contributions are often embedded in Discussion or Conclusion. **Classification: PRACTICE ONLY** — observed variation, not stated requirement; FIELD_SKILL's expectation of distinct, labeled subsections is not enforced uniformly.

### Citation and Reference Standards
Follows Emerald Harvard style (see Hard Constraints). **FIELD DEFAULT.**

### Tone and Hedging Placement
Hedging concentrated in Discussion and Limitations sections, consistent with FIELD_SKILL. Results section reports findings in direct language ("The study found," "Hypothesis 1 was supported"). **FIELD DEFAULT.**

## Dimensions With No Delta
- **Sentence and Paragraph Style:** Medium-to-long sentences with embedded citations, dense paragraphs in Introduction and Theory sections — FIELD DEFAULT
- **Argumentation and CARS Pattern:** Clear Create-a-Research-Space framing; explicit positioning of research gaps; positioning studies as extending or contextualizing prior work — FIELD DEFAULT
- **Methods Conventions:** Procedural detail on sampling, screening, and data quality safeguards; construct measurement with operational detail (item wording, scales, Cronbach's alpha); robustness demonstrations through supplementary studies or sensitivity checks — FIELD DEFAULT (though somewhat less extensive than ABS 4*/4)
- **Keywords:** Up to 6 keywords, consistent with field baseline (Emerald standard: 6 recommended) — FIELD DEFAULT

---

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Overuse of passive voice:** This journal tolerates more passive construction than ABS 4*/4, but do not abandon active voice entirely, particularly in describing researcher decisions and methodology choices.
2. **Insufficient structured abstract specificity:** The four mandatory sections must each be substantive; cramming all content into Purpose or Findings weakens the abstract's value to reviewers.
3. **Weak Practical Implications:** While less mandatory than in top-tier journals, Practical Implications are still expected; failure to address practitioner relevance or social/organizational context may be read as overlooking the journal's applied orientation toward social enterprise practitioners.
4. **Anonymity breach:** Removing author names from the main text is critical for blind review; any identifying information (including self-citations phrased as "our prior work") must be edited or moved to the separate author file.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

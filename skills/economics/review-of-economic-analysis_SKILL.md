# Journal Writing Skill: Review of Economic Analysis

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** International Centre for Economic Analysis (nonprofit, chapters in Canada, Poland, Italy, Ukraine); hosted on University of Waterloo Open Journal Systems
- **Scope:** Open-access general economics journal publishing applied empirical, theoretical, and policy-oriented papers across micro, macro, finance, development, and related social science topics; no narrow disciplinary focus.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://openjournals.uwaterloo.ca/index.php/rofea/about/submissions
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 11 (abstract/keyword/metadata level; full text not reliably extractable via automated fetch) — Erkişi (2026, v18i2.7028); Köse & Süt (2026, v18i2.6657); Hamrouni, Yao & Tonde (2026, v18i2.6199); Ben Mbarek (2026, v18i2.6504); Gans (2026, v18i1.6854); Matsue (2026, v18i1.6194); Do Thi Mong et al. ## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Manuscript order: text, appendix, references. First page must contain title, author name(s) with full affiliations, abstract, corresponding author contact details, JEL classification codes, up to 10 keywords, and an acknowledgements footnote.

### Length
No explicit word or page limit stated.

### Abstract
Unstructured, maximum 200 words.

### Citations
Author-date in text: "Friedman (1956)" or "(Johnson, 2004)"; three-plus authors use "et al." References alphabetical by first author, then chronological; same-author-year works disambiguated with letters (2006a, 2006b). Reference list double-spaced.

### Headings
Sections numbered with Arabic numerals (not Roman); subsections numbered consecutively (2.1, 2.2).

### Tables and Figures
Numbered consecutively in order of first reference, each with a brief descriptive title, embedded in the text at point of first reference (not collected at the end).

### Explicit Prohibitions
No previously published work or concurrent submission elsewhere without disclosure. Excessive footnotes discouraged.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
GUIDELINE ONLY / not enforced: guidelines require JEL classification codes on the first page, but none of the 11 spot-checked articles display JEL codes in their published metadata. Do not treat JEL codes as a de facto requirement despite the guideline text.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
PRACTICE ONLY: the visible published sample skews toward applied cross-country or single-country panel/time-series papers (VECM, FMOLS, quantile regression, threshold/SVAR models) with policy-oriented framing, rather than structural or theory papers — though at least one purely theoretical paper (Gans 2026) and one applied theory/model paper (Matsue 2026) appear, so this is a tendency in the mix rather than an exclusive pattern. No delta from FIELD_SKILL section architecture or voice is discernible at abstract/metadata level; full-text access was not available to confirm introduction-level rhetorical moves, hedging, or citation density in body text.

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting without JEL codes is common in practice despite the guideline requiring them; including them anyway is safer and costs little, but their absence has not visibly caused rejection or correction in the published record.
- Guidelines explicitly flag "excessive footnotes" as something reviewers watch for — keep footnotes to genuine asides, not citation overflow.
- Because REA accepts a broad range of applied and theoretical work with no narrow scope, do not over-tailor framing to a single subfield; the "gap" and "contribution" framing from FIELD_SKILL still applies but competitive positioning language can be more modest given the journal's ABS 1 tier and broad scope.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

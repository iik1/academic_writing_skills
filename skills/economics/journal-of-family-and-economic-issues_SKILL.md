# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Family and Economic Issues

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Springer Nature
- **Scope:** Peer-reviewed research at the intersection of family dynamics and economic environments, including household finance, work-family balance, financial socialization, household economics, and material well-being.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://link.springer.com/journal/10834/submission-guidelines
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 (editorial articles and research papers from 2020-2025; full-text paywall limited accessibility; analysis based on abstracts, PMC editorials, and structure from published metadata)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

### Structure
Manuscripts follow standard academic structure with an Introduction, body sections (Methods/Data where applicable), Results/Findings, Discussion, and Conclusion. Exact sectioning varies by paper type (empirical vs. qualitative vs. review). No mandated section-naming convention beyond industry norms.

### Length
No explicit word or page limit stated in accessible guidelines. Journal publishes articles ranging from empirical research to literature reviews; typical length inferred as 6,000-10,000 words based on published content.

### Abstract
**Required:** 150–250 words. Unstructured format (single paragraph) is the norm. Should concisely state research question, method, key findings, and implications.

### Citations
**Style:** Author-date (parenthetical), e.g., (Author Year) or (Author and Author Year) for two authors, (Author et al. Year) for 3+.
**Format:** Springer SPBASIC citation style. References list at end of manuscript in alphabetical order by author surname.
**Density:** Follows FIELD_SKILL norms: high in Introduction (literature positioning), medium in Methods, low to medium in Results, low in Conclusion.

### Headings
No more than three levels of displayed headings. No explicit mandated heading format (e.g., Roman vs. Arabic numerals) stated in guidelines; practice is flexible and should match Springer house style conventions.

### Tables and Figures
Standard academic conventions. Figures should be formatted to fit Springer column widths (84 mm for double-column layouts, 174 mm for single-column). Tables with clear titles and notes explaining abbreviations, data sources, and statistical notation.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Double-blind review requires: author names, affiliations, and identifying information must be removed from manuscript text and accompanying files. Separate title page submitted via portal with author information. Author contribution and competing interest statements submitted via submission interface (not in manuscript).

### File Format and Submission
Manuscript text in .docx or LaTeX. All editable source files required at submission and revision. Supplementary material in PDF, .csv, or .xlsx formats only.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
**Delta: Accessibility increased; formality moderated.** While the journal publishes in the ECON field, its interdisciplinary scope (family studies, social work, psychology alongside economics) yields prose slightly less technical and more accessible than ABS 4*/4 pure econ outlets. First-person plural ("we") remains standard, but hedging is more explicit than FIELD_SKILL baseline—"may," "appear to," and "suggest" are more frequent, reflecting the mixed-method and qualitative research that coexist alongside quantitative work. Authors are more cautious about causal claims when data or design permits only correlational inference.

**Classification:** PRACTICE OBSERVED across editorials and article abstracts; consistent with journal's applied family science positioning.

### Methodological Diversity
**Delta: Qualitative and mixed-method research explicitly valued.** Unlike ABS 4*/4 economics journals which privilege causal identification and quantitative methods, JFEI explicitly calls for and publishes qualitative research, case studies, and mixed-method designs. Articles frequently combine survey data with qualitative interviews or focus groups. No expectation of instrumental variables or natural experiments; descriptive and correlational designs are acceptable and common.

**Classification:** ENFORCED (stated in editorials and special issues; confirmed across recent article titles: "Understanding Family Financial Socialization," interviews with practitioners, etc.).

### Mechanism and Mediation Focus
**Delta: Mechanisms explicitly addressed; indirect effects emphasized.** Articles routinely test mediating pathways (e.g., how parental financial socialization affects youth outcomes *through* financial confidence or behavior). Mediation analysis is a common statistical approach. This reflects FIELD_SKILL, but the emphasis on *understanding the process* (not just the treatment effect) is stronger.

**Classification:** PRACTICE OBSERVED; not mandated but standard in recent publications.

### Citation to Interdisciplinary Literature
**Delta: Psychology, sociology, and social work cited alongside economics.** FIELD_SKILL baseline assumes economics-primary citation culture. JFEI integrates non-economics literatures routinely. Introduction and Discussion sections cite family systems theory, developmental psychology, and sociological frameworks on gender and household labor without treating them as "outside" the field. This is structural, not a stylistic quirk.

**Classification:** ENFORCED (evident in editorial "decade review" which explicitly celebrates disciplinary diversity and recommends theoretical frameworks from psychology and systems theory).

### Limitation Acknowledgment
**Delta: Limitations section more explicit; "research gaps" language more formal.** While FIELD_SKILL baseline includes limitations discussion, JFEI papers more frequently devote explicit subsections or paragraphs to limitations (sample characteristics, measurement error, reverse causality). The language is direct: "A limitation of this study is…" or "The generalizability of these findings is constrained by…"

**Classification:** PRACTICE OBSERVED; consistent across empirical articles and editorials.

### Special Issues and Thematic Foci
**Delta: Special issues with multiple short reviews common; editorial/introduction pieces longer than typical conclusion.** The journal regularly publishes special issues comprising 15–20 short review articles (4–6 pages each) organized around a theme, with a unifying editorial introduction. These pieces are conference-like in tone, more conversational than the FIELD_SKILL baseline, and explicitly invite future directions and research gaps.

**Classification:** PRACTICE ONLY (not a requirement for regular submissions, but reflects journal's editorial strategy).

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Sentence and paragraph style:** FIELD DEFAULT (medium to long sentences, dense paragraph structure, high technical vocabulary within discipline norms—equivalent to FIELD_SKILL).
- **Tense conventions:** FIELD DEFAULT (past tense for methods and data; present tense for model properties, theoretical claims, and findings).
- **Argumentation structure:** FIELD DEFAULT (Introduction with research question, preview of results, roadmap; explicit contribution statements; conclusion restates findings and draws implications).
- **Statistical reporting:** FIELD DEFAULT (coefficients with confidence intervals or standard errors; magnitude and economic/practical significance expected alongside statistical significance; null results reported directly).
- **Robustness and checks:** FIELD DEFAULT (sensitivity analyses and alternative specifications common, though not as formalized as top-tier econ journals).

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Over-claiming causality with correlational designs.** JFEI accepts correlational research, but authors must carefully distinguish between "associated with" and "caused." Hedging language is expected and valued. Avoid statements like "X causes Y" when the data is observational; use "X is associated with Y" or "X predicts Y."

2. **Neglecting mechanism.** Even in descriptive or correlational studies, readers expect some discussion of *how* or *why* the pattern might exist. A finding without mechanistic reasoning feels incomplete to this audience. Address plausible pathways, even if not formally tested.

3. **Excluding non-economist perspectives.** The journal explicitly values interdisciplinary input. Positioning your work *only* in economics literature (e.g., labor economics, household production models) without acknowledging psychological, sociological, or family studies perspectives may be seen as parochial. Cite foundational work in family systems, financial socialization, and developmental theory where relevant.

4. **Underestimating methodological diversity.** Submitting a purely theoretical paper or a qualitative interview study is appropriate and welcome. Conversely, do not assume all papers are quantitative empirical work. Vary your structure and evidence to match your research question and design.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

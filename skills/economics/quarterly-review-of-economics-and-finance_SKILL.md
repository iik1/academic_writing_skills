# Journal Writing Skill: Quarterly Review of Economics and Finance

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Elsevier
- **Scope:** Economics, financial economics, and finance. Empirical, theoretical, and policy-related research with emphasis on quality, originality, clear arguments, persuasive evidence, intelligent analysis, and clear writing.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/the-quarterly-review-of-economics-and-finance/publish/guide-for-authors (publisher guidelines; full content behind paywall; key requirements extracted from Elsevier's standard author guidelines)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 10 (2024-2025): Elyasiani & Movaghari (Money demand), Moura et al. (Brazilian banks), Abdelaziz et al. (CSP and stock returns), Feng et al. (AI and innovation), He et al. (FinTech and bank performance), Natural gas volatility GARCH-MIDAS, Bask et al. (Media sentiment), Fernandes et al. (Equity premium disconnect), Tomita (Bond markets), Tug of war with noise traders)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows standard ECON empirical article structure: Introduction, Methods/Data, Results, Conclusion. Optional Background/Institutional Setting and Robustness sections permitted. Appendices standard for supplementary analyses and proofs.

### Length
No explicit page/word limit stated in publicly available guidelines. Abstracts capped at 250 words maximum.

### Abstract
- **Maximum length:** 250 words
- **Format:** Unstructured (single continuous paragraph; no structured fields)
- **Required content:** Objectives, methodology, principal results, major conclusions
- **Restrictions:** Must stand alone; avoid references where possible; define non-standard abbreviations at first mention; avoid references but if essential cite author and year

### Keywords
- **Quantity:** 1 to 6 keywords required for indexing
- **Language:** English
- **Format:** Comma-separated list following abstract

### Citations
- **Style:** Author-date (parenthetical or in-text citations)
- **Format examples:**
  - Single author: (Author, Year)
  - Multiple authors: (Author1, Year; Author2, Year)
  - Three+ authors: (Author et al., Year)
  - Use "&" before final author in citations
  - Italicize journal titles and book titles
  - Include volume and issue numbers for articles
- **Bibliography:** Full reference list at end; author surname first
- **Notes:** Footnotes carry supplementary material and qualifications, not primary citations

### Headings
No specific mandatory naming conventions stated. Roman or Arabic numerals accepted (per FIELD_SKILL). Observed practice: simple, descriptive headings using standard labels (Introduction, Data, Methods, Results, Conclusion, Robustness).

### Tables and Figures
Standard ECON conventions apply. Tables are primary vehicle for results. Table notes explain variable definitions, standard error clustering, sample restrictions. Figures illustrate outcome variation, event-study designs, distributional results. Both common in published articles.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Submissions must not have been previously published except in limited forms
- Must not be under consideration elsewhere
- Must have approval from all authors and relevant authorities
- If accepted, cannot be republished in same form without written consent
- Must follow Elsevier Publishing Ethics Policy

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Voice and Register:** FIELD DEFAULT (first-person plural "we" dominant; active voice preferred; formal, precise, economical register)
- **Sentence and Paragraph Style:** FIELD DEFAULT (medium-to-long sentences in methods/theory; high paragraph density; high technical vocabulary)
- **Argumentation and Rhetorical Moves:** FIELD DEFAULT (results announced in introduction with quantitative preview; contributions stated explicitly; gap framing concrete and specific; conclusion is genuine synthesis)
- **Methods Conventions:** FIELD DEFAULT (mixed tense following principled logic; sufficient procedural detail; identification strategy required for empirical papers; robustness standard)
- **Results and Reporting Norms:** FIELD DEFAULT (results and interpretation integrated; tables primary vehicle; quantitative specificity expected; null results reported directly)
- **Citation Density by Section:** FIELD DEFAULT (high in Introduction, medium in Methods, low-to-medium in Results, low in Conclusion)
- **Paraphrase vs. Direct Quotation:** FIELD DEFAULT (paraphrase strongly preferred; direct quotation extremely rare)
- **Handling Disagreement:** FIELD DEFAULT (precise, non-hostile framing; acknowledgment of prior work with explanation of differences)
- **Hedging and Epistemic Caution:** FIELD DEFAULT (low overall hedging; clear claims preferred; hedging applied to external validity, causal interpretation beyond identified parameter, data limitations, and mechanism vs. reduced-form only)
- **Discussion and Conclusion Norms:** FIELD DEFAULT (conclusion restates findings, draws implications, acknowledges limitations, suggests future research; no new results in conclusion; shorter than introduction)

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Exceeding abstract word limit:** Keep abstracts under 250 words. Observed abstracts that reach or exceed this boundary often struggle with clarity; aim for 180–240 words for maximum clarity.

2. **Missing keywords:** Ensure you include 1–6 keywords immediately after the abstract for indexing. These should reflect the paper's core contribution.

3. **Inadequate identification strategy:** For empirical papers, state the identification strategy explicitly and defend assumptions. This journal's ABS 2 rating does not relax this requirement from the field baseline.

4. **Insufficient quantitative specificity in results:** Do not report directional findings without magnitudes. "We find an increase" is insufficient; "We find a 3.2 percentage point increase" is required.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

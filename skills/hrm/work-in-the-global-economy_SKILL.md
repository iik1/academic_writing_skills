# Journal Writing Skill: Work in the Global Economy

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** HRM&EMP (Human Resource Management & Employment Relations)
- **Publisher:** Bristol University Press
- **Scope:** Interdisciplinary peer-reviewed journal rooted in Labour Process Conference traditions. Promotes understanding of work in all forms and dimensions: labour processes, labour markets, labour organising, labour reproduction. Welcomes qualitative, quantitative, and theoretical contributions equally.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://bristoluniversitypress.co.uk/journals/work-in-the-global-economy/instructions-for-authors (paywalled; guidelines extracted via WebSearch)
- **Field baseline:** hrm-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 8 ([Chauhan 2025 "Uneven deskilling"; Kenny & Webster labour process theory; platform economy studies; editorial introductions 2021, 2025])

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows standard IMRaD with theory integration. Required front matter: title (10-15 words), abstract, keywords. Back matter includes references and optionally acknowledgements. No explicit prescriptive section headings enforced, but empirical articles typically include: Introduction, Literature/Conceptual Framing, Methods (or Approach), Findings/Results, Discussion, Conclusion.

### Length
**Strict word limit:** 7,000–9,500 words **including all text** (abstract, title page, keywords, acknowledgements, references). Manuscripts exceeding this limit may be desk-rejected before review. Research articles explicitly noted as "up to 8,000 words."
**Delta from FIELD_SKILL:** This is SIGNIFICANTLY STRICTER than typical ABS 4* HRM journals (which allow 12–15k words). Demands discipline in scope and argument density. [ENFORCED]

### Abstract
Maximum 250 words. Should outline central question, approach/method, findings, and take-home message. Must include main keywords. Unstructured format; no mandatory subdivisions specified. [ENFORCED]

### Citations
Harvard system (author-date, in-text). Author surname + year in brackets. Two authors: both surnames. Three+ authors: first surname + "et al." + year. Multiple works same author/year: a, b, c, d distinction. References (not cited in guidelines but standard practice) appear at end. Paraphrase strongly preferred over direct quotation (inherited from FIELD_SKILL); direct quotation reserved for precise definitions or evidence extracts.

### Headings
No explicit convention stated. Observed practice in articles: numbered and unnumbered primary headings (Introduction, Methods, Findings, etc.), with secondary headings (e.g., "Data Collection" under Methods). Informal conventions; hierarchy indicated by formatting, not rigid numbering.

### Tables and Figures
Not explicitly specified in guidelines. Assumed to follow Bristol University Press house style: figures and tables numbered, captioned, and referenced in text. No specific format requirements found.

### Language and Tone
**British English spelling and punctuation required.** Non-discriminatory language is mandatory. Beyond this, follows FIELD_SKILL norms: formal, impersonal baseline with first-person plural ("we") permitted in methods and discussion; narrative and interpretive prose tolerated more than in economics but less than in pure sociology. No colloquialisms. [ENFORCED]

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Discriminatory language (enforced; sensitivity guidance in appendix C of submission materials, not seen in this research but referenced)
- Manuscripts exceeding word limit subject to desk rejection

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Manuscript Length and Scope
**RELAXATION:** Work in the Global Economy imposes stricter word limits (7–9.5k) than the ABS 4* baseline implied by FIELD_SKILL (12–15k typical). This **forces tighter argumentation and prioritization**. Introductions and literature sections must be more concise; methods more streamlined; discussion must foreground only the most critical implications. Not a prohibition of IMRaD structure, but a compression of each section. Writers familiar with FIELD_SKILL norms should expect 30–40% reduction in word budget. [ENFORCED]

### Theoretical Grounding and Labour Process Specificity
The journal is **rooted explicitly in Labour Process Conference traditions** and emphasizes labour process theory (LPT) as a core analytical frame. While FIELD_SKILL acknowledges theoretical diversity (AMO, social exchange, institutional theory), Work in the Global Economy shows a distinct preference for LPT-informed analysis: examination of control, consent, deskilling, labour mobility, and capital circuits. Papers ignoring LPT or positioning it as one of many equivalent frameworks risk reviewer feedback that they have underutilized the journal's distinctive intellectual tradition. [PRACTICE]

Articles frequently forefront concepts like: control and consent regimes, labour process and capital accumulation, race/skill intersections, transnational labour mobility, platform economy through labour-process lens. [PRACTICE]

### Empirical–Theoretical Balance
FIELD_SKILL notes that "empirical (quantitative and qualitative), conceptual, and review-type articles" are welcome in ABS 4* journals. Work in the Global Economy **explicitly states that theoretical pieces and empirical analyses are equally welcomed,** and emphasizes papers that "draw upon empirical findings to support and develop theory." This is less a delta than a **reassurance to theory-focused writers** that they need not apologize for limited original data so long as their work brings conceptual innovation. [GUIDELINE ONLY]

### Interdisciplinary Scope
Explicitly welcomes sociology of work, employment relations, HRM, organisational studies, political economy, labour geography, labour history, development studies. This is broader than the FIELD_SKILL baseline, which foregrounds HRM and organisational psychology framing. Writers from sociology, geography, or development studies should feel confident that their disciplinary vocabulary and traditions (e.g., institutional analysis, comparative political economy, ethnographic immersion) are explicitly encouraged. [GUIDELINE ONLY]

### Double Blind Peer Review and Replication
Guidelines mention "double anonymous peer review by at least two referees," consistent with FIELD_SKILL. No special delta observed.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Overrunning the word limit.** The 7–9.5k cap is absolute. Reviewers and editors are instructed to desk-reject overruns. Many writers trained on ABS 4* journals (12–15k) unconsciously write too long. Plan to sacrifice depth in one section (e.g., compress lit review or limit methodology detail) to stay within bounds. [ENFORCED / HIGH PREVALENCE]

2. **Underutilizing labour process theory.** Submissions that frame work issues through HRM best-practice, organisational behaviour, or generic management theory risk feedback that they have missed the journal's intellectual centre. Even non-LPT papers benefit from explicit dialogue with labour process concepts (control, consent, commodification, skill, labour mobility). [PRACTICE / MEDIUM PREVALENCE]

3. **Insufficient acknowledgement of scholarly tradition.** The journal is rooted in ILPC (International Labour Process Conference) intellectual lineage. Submissions that ignore this context or treat LPT as incidental may read as poorly matched. Cite foundational ILPC scholars (e.g., Braverman, Burawoy, Smith, Thompson) where relevant. [PRACTICE / LOW–MEDIUM PREVALENCE]

4. **Assuming universality of findings without international/global framing.** The journal title and scope emphasize "global economy." Even geographically localized studies (single country, single firm) benefit from explicit positioning within global capital dynamics, labour mobility, or transnational value chains. Purely parochial framing risks criticism for failing to engage the journal's global agenda. [PRACTICE / MEDIUM PREVALENCE]

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

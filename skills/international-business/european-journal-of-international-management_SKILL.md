# Journal Writing Skill: European Journal of International Management

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** IB&AREA
- **Publisher:** Inderscience Enterprises Ltd.
- **Scope:** Leading journal with European and global perspectives, devoted to advancing international management research, practice and policy. Publishes original papers, case studies, literature reviews, commentaries, and research notes on regional, international, or comparative issues in management. Seeks conceptual, theoretical, methodological, empirical, qualitative and review papers advancing the field of international business and management.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.inderscience.com/jhome.php?jcode=ejim
- **Field baseline:** international-business-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 3 (abstracts and metadata from 2026 Vol. 28)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Manuscripts must include (in order): title, abstract, body text, conflicts of interest statement, references, and tables/figures with captions. Keywords section follows abstract. Author biographical information should not appear in the document file itself (authors are added during editorial processing).

For empirical research: follow standard introduction, methods, results, discussion, conclusion structure. Conceptual and review papers follow their own logical progression; no single mandatory structure is prescribed for non-empirical submissions.

### Length
- **Main text:** 5,000–7,000 words (excluding figures, tables, and references)
- High-quality articles exceeding 7,000 words will be considered
- Research notes: approximately 3,000 words

### Abstract
- **Word limit:** Less than 150 words (ENFORCED—significantly tighter than FIELD_SKILL's 150–250 norm)
- Must include research aims, methodologies, and conclusions
- Avoid citations where possible; if essential, use author-year format only (no full reference)
- Define any uncommon abbreviations upon first use

### Keywords
Provide 10–15 words or phrases to enhance discoverability.

### Citations
**Harvard system (author-date, name-date format):** Citations appear parenthetically in text as (Author Year) or as narrative citations "Author (Year) argues...". Full reference list appears alphabetically at end of article. Include DOI when available. Two-author works list both names; 3+ authors use et al. format.

### Headings
Inderscience guidelines do not specify heading hierarchy. Follow a logical hierarchy (e.g., level 1 for main sections, level 2 for subsections) and maintain consistency. Do not number sections unless submitting a research note.

### Tables and Figures
- Figures should preferably be in black and white
- Place all figures and tables at the end of the manuscript, numbered sequentially
- Include descriptive captions for all figures and tables
- Obtain and provide permission documentation for any reproduced materials from copyrighted sources

### Language and Tone
**All articles must be written in English, using UK spelling and terminology** (ENFORCED—explicit requirement not stated in FIELD_SKILL). Submit in single or double spacing, preferably Times New Roman 12-point font. Authors should ensure text quality, ideally with native English speaker review if authors are non-native speakers.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Do not include author names in the document file properties
- Do not submit papers previously published or currently under review elsewhere (conference papers accepted only if substantially rewritten with proper permissions)
- Research notes must include "Research note:" in the title

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Abstract Length
FIELD_SKILL endorses 150–250 word abstracts at the top tier. EJIM caps abstracts at **under 150 words** (ENFORCED). This is a material tightening: authors must compress research aims, methods, and conclusions into 1/3 fewer words than FIELD_SKILL baseline. This requires ruthless elimination of context-setting and framing; focus on essentials only.

### Language Specification
FIELD_SKILL does not mandate language variety. EJIM explicitly requires **UK English and UK spelling/terminology** (ENFORCED). This affects spelling (colour vs. color, organisation vs. organization, -ise endings vs. -ize), vocabulary choices (e.g., "graduate student" vs. "postgraduate"), and date formatting (DD/MM/YYYY convention preferred in British academic writing).

### Article Word Ceiling
FIELD_SKILL does not specify total word count. EJIM enforces a **5,000–7,000 word range for main text** (ENFORCED), which is below the typical 8,000–12,000 word ceiling at higher-tier IB journals. This constraint forces tighter argumentation and limits space for extended literature reviews or methodological detail. Trade-offs are common: authors often abbreviate literature grounding, reduce robustness checks, or relegate secondary analyses to appendices.

### First-Person Usage
FIELD_SKILL strongly endorses first-person plural ("we argue," "we find") for authors' own claims. Inderscience guidelines do not explicitly encourage or prescribe first person. Passive voice remains fully acceptable throughout. In practice, EJIM articles observed employ both registers (first person for key claims, passive for procedural description), consistent with FIELD_SKILL, but without the field baseline's *presumptive* in favor of first person. **No explicit preference stated; both registers are acceptable** (PRACTICE ONLY—no delta from baseline, but field norm not assumed by publisher guidelines).

### Structural Deviations
FIELD_SKILL flags variation without prescribing a single structure. EJIM follows similarly: empirical papers use IMRaD-like sections; conceptual and review papers are flexible. No major deviation from FIELD_SKILL here.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- **Voice and person:** First-person plural acceptable, passive voice acceptable; follows FIELD_SKILL norm.
- **Citation density:** Harvard format is standard across Inderscience journals; high citation density in introduction/theory/discussion expected per FIELD_SKILL. No deviation.
- **Hedging and epistemic caution:** Moderate hedging expected as per FIELD_SKILL; no explicit guidance in Inderscience guidelines contradicts this.
- **Results presentation:** Tables and prose narrative expected per FIELD_SKILL; no departure.
- **Discussion/conclusion style:** Loop back to research question, state limitations, future research—FIELD_SKILL norms; EJIM guidelines silent but observed practice aligns.
- **Sentence and paragraph style:** Medium-to-long sentences, topic-sentence-led paragraphs per FIELD_SKILL; no evidence of tighter constraints in EJIM.
- **Argument structure (CARS introduction, contributions, limitations):** FIELD_SKILL baseline observed in EJIM articles; no deviation.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Oversized abstracts:** Authors accustomed to 200+ word abstracts often submit drafts with 180–200 word abstracts. EJIM will desk-reject or require reduction. Count every word; ruthlessly cut context-setting and restate findings as 2–3 crisp sentences.

2. **Exceeding word count:** 5,000–7,000 words is a hard ceiling for most submissions. Methods and results sections often exceed space; consider relegating supplementary robustness checks to online appendices or removing lower-priority literature from the introduction.

3. **American English spelling and terminology:** Non-UK-based authors frequently use American English. Use a UK English dictionary (e.g., Oxford) and flag instances of -ize/-ise, -or/-our, and terminology (e.g., "organisation" not "organization"). Automated tools can help, but human review is essential.

4. **Missing or incomplete author declarations:** Inderscience requires explicit conflicts of interest statements. Omitting this statement, or including author biographical information in the document itself, will trigger desk rejection.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Man and the Economy

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** De Gruyter (De Gruyter Brill)
- **Scope:** Founded by Ronald Coase as the official journal of The Coase Society. Positions itself explicitly as an alternative to mathematics- and theory-driven mainstream economics, favoring economics as "a study of man as he is and the economy as it actually exists in the real world." Interdisciplinary and international in intent, aimed at scholars, policymakers, and business/legal professionals as well as academic economists.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Guidelines page not directly accessible; inferred from published table of contents. The journal publishes a mix of article types rather than a single uniform research-article template:
- **Original Articles** — standard full-length research papers
- **Research Notes** — shorter, less-developed ideas or findings
- **Voices from the Field** — practitioner contributions (business/policy)
- **Marketplace for Ideas** — interviews with scholars/game-changers
- **Wisdom of the Past** — essays recovering forgotten insights
- **Letters from Readers**
This multi-format structure is a delta from FIELD_SKILL, which assumes a single empirical/theory research-article template.

### Length
Not confirmed from a guidelines document. Observed page ranges vary enormously: some Original Articles run 20–45 pages (e.g., 21–44, 61–92, 93–127), while other content pieces run 1–12 pages (e.g., pp. 1–2, 11–12, 37–43, 59–60). Treat short pieces as a distinct, non-standard-length article type rather than evidence of a short length norm for full research papers.

### Abstract
Abstracts observed are short, single-paragraph, unstructured (no labeled subsections) — consistent with FIELD_SKILL default. No explicit word-limit could be confirmed from a guidelines page.

### Citations
No journal-specific citation style could be confirmed from public guideline pages. No delta observed in the sampled abstracts/metadata.

### Headings
Not independently confirmable from abstract-level access. No delta observed.

### Tables and Figures

### Explicit Prohibitions
A "Template for Ethical and Legal Declarations" must be completed and uploaded as a separate file at submission (per De Gruyter general submission-checklist language associated with this journal). These are standard De Gruyter-wide requirements, not distinguishing content, but are noted since they are stated explicitly.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
PRACTICE ONLY / low confidence (abstract-level evidence only): several sampled pieces (Sheng Hong's essays on the Coase theorem and on filial piety, the Cheung/tenancy extension) read as discursive, argument-driven essays rather than tightly quantified empirical papers, with claims stated in narrative/interpretive prose rather than "we find X increases Y by Z percent" form. This is consistent with the journal's explicit anti-formalist editorial mission, but cannot be confirmed as a binding norm from front-matter alone — flagged as PRACTICE ONLY, not a stated requirement.

### Statistical Reporting
PRACTICE ONLY, low prevalence: JEL classification codes are inconsistently present — absent entirely in at least two of the eight sampled articles (Chen & Liao 2024; Sheng Hong 2023 filial-piety essay), present in others. FIELD_SKILL treats standard reporting apparatus (data, identification, robustness) as near-universal for empirical papers; this journal's applied/quantitative papers (e.g., the remittances and globalisation papers) do follow FIELD_SKILL norms (multiple estimators, EKC/quantile methods, clear JEL codes), but the journal also publishes qualitative/conceptual pieces to which these norms simply do not apply. Net effect: RELAXATION for a meaningful share of the journal's content, not a redefinition of the norm for its empirical papers.

### Citation Density
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm for the empirical/applied papers sampled (remittances, EKC/globalisation, tenancy). Insufficient full-text access to assess the essay-type pieces.

### Structural Deviations
ENFORCED (evident from the published record itself, not just guidelines): the journal maintains a formal taxonomy of non-standard article types shorter than a full research article (Research Notes, Voices from the Field, Marketplace for Ideas, Wisdom of the Past, Letters from Readers), several of which appear in the sampled tables of contents as 1–12 page items. This is a genuine structural delta from FIELD_SKILL, which assumes the empirical/theory research-article template is close to universal. When writing for this journal, confirm which article type is intended before applying the full IMRAD-style structure.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
PRACTICE ONLY: the journal appears unusually receptive to institutional-economics, legal-economics, and history-of-economic-thought topics with policy or philosophical argumentation as the primary contribution, alongside conventional applied econometric papers. This breadth is wider than is typical at ABS 4/4* general-interest outlets and reflects the journal's explicit founding mission (Coase Society) rather than a formatting rule.

## Common Pitfalls
- Assuming every submission must follow the full empirical-paper IMRAD template — the journal explicitly accepts shorter, non-standard formats (Research Notes, Voices from the Field, Wisdom of the Past); confirm the intended article type first.
- Omitting JEL codes is tolerated in practice for essay-type submissions but should not be assumed acceptable for a standard empirical Original Article.
- Treating the journal as demanding the same formalism/quantification density as ABS 4/4* outlets — its founding editorial stance explicitly favors real-world, less formalized argumentation for at least a subset of its content.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Urban Studies

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** REGIONAL STUDIES, PLANNING AND ENVIRONMENT
- **Publisher:** SAGE (on behalf of Urban Studies Journal Limited)
- **Scope:** Multidisciplinary journal covering urban and regional development, planning, governance, and socio-spatial change, with explicit interest in engaging broad international debates in urban theory.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/author-instructions/usj ; formatting supplement: https://www.urbanstudiesonline.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Formatting_Guidelines.pdf
- **Field baseline:** regional-studies-planning-environment-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 11 (abstracts/front matter via search-engine extraction and secondary summaries, full text paywalled in this environment): Bathla (2025, "Authoritarian urbanism beyond the city"); Lee (2025, "Seeing like AI?"); Kurt-Özman & Tasan-Kok (2025, "Community politics in urban regeneration"); Connolly & Kythreotis (2026, "Building back better through urban blue and green space?"); Angst, Rosen, Painter & De Gregorio (2025, "Harassment or neglect?"); Song & Chapple (2024, "Does gentrification constrain housing markets?"); Croese & Wood (2024, "African Urban Studies: Contributions and Challenges", editorial); Guibrunet et al. ## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No mandated template or fixed section list. Authors choose their own analytical structure, but guidelines explicitly stress that a strong introduction (signposting the argument) and a strong conclusion (inter-relating findings and drawing out their general analytical significance) are the key structural anchors expected by reviewers. Authors are explicitly encouraged, in the cover letter and in the paper itself, to engage with and cite prior Urban Studies articles relevant to their argument.

### Length
Regular articles: 8,500 words. Critical commentaries: 6,000 words. Debates papers: 10,000 words. Word count includes abstract, references, notes, appendices, tables and figures; each table/figure counts as ~250 words (~half a page) regardless of actual word content within it.

### Abstract
Unstructured (no subheadings/fields), 250 words, placed in the main text file (not on a separate title page). Required for all article types except book reviews. Minimum 5 keywords listed after the abstract. Translated abstracts are included by the journal.

### Citations
SAGE Harvard style specifically (not a generic author-date style). Website references require an "accessed" date; book references require publisher location. Working papers, conference papers, and dissertations should include a URL where possible.

### Headings
No numbering convention specified; headings/subheadings should be set in bold or italics (not a numbered outline format).

### Tables and Figures
Uploaded as separate files from the main manuscript, referenced via text placeholders (e.g. "Insert Figure X here"). No automated numbering for tables/figures. Main text file must be an editable format (Word preferred); PDF uploads are not accepted for the main text.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Double-blind review is strictly enforced: authors must avoid identifying language, including self-citation phrasing such as "in my/our previous study." Self-citation of the author's own published work is permitted only via "Author, year" with the reference omitted from the bibliography; unpublished/forthcoming self-citations use "Author, forthcoming" and are likewise omitted from the bibliography. Author names, affiliations, and acknowledgements/funding statements must go on a separate title page, never in the main text file.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Structural Deviations
RELAXATION (ABS 3): unlike the more prescriptive structures common at the ABS 4 level, Urban Studies explicitly declines to mandate a fixed section sequence (no required "Literature review," "Methods," "Discussion" labels), leaving structure to authorial judgment so long as introduction and conclusion carry the signposting/synthesis load. This is looser than FIELD_SKILL's description of near-universal literature-review sections, though the spot-checked articles still functionally include equivalent framing sections under varied headings — so in practice this is a labelling relaxation, not a content one.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
PRACTICE ONLY: spot-checked articles show unusually broad topical and methodological range even by field standards — spanning ethnographic/qualitative political-economy pieces (Bathla; Kurt-Özman & Tasan-Kok), conceptual/theoretical interventions (Lee), quantitative survey-based causal analysis (Angst et al.), and longitudinal quantitative mobility analysis (Song & Chapple) — consistent with FIELD_SKILL's "no single consensus method" but at the high end of methodological heterogeneity within a single outlet.

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting a PDF as the main text file, or embedding tables/figures inline instead of as separate uploads with placeholders — both are explicit formatting rejections risks per the guidelines.
- Using generic author-date citation instead of the specific SAGE Harvard variant (missing "accessed" dates or publisher locations).
- Breaking double-blind anonymity through self-citation phrasing like "in our previous work" instead of the required "Author, year" convention.
- Miscalculating the word count by excluding tables/figures/appendices, which the journal counts explicitly (each table/figure = ~250 words) and enforces against the 8,500-word regular-article ceiling.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

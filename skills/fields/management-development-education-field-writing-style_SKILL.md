# Field Writing Style: MDEV&EDU

## Scope and Intended Use
This baseline describes the writing conventions expected at the top of the
Management Development and Education (MDEV&EDU) field, as defined by the
ABS 2024 Academic Journal Guide. The field has only one ABS 4*/4 journal
(no ABS 4 journals exist in this category), so this baseline is derived
from a single flagship outlet rather than a cross-journal convergence
exercise. Because the field sits at the intersection of management
scholarship and education/pedagogy research, its conventions borrow
substantially from general management writing norms while accommodating
a distinctly wider range of article types — including conceptual essays,
dialogues, and practice-oriented scholarship — than is typical in
mainstream management journals. Journal-specific skills in this field
should treat this document as the default and record only points of
departure.

## Typical Article Structure
Leading journals in this field publish several structurally distinct
article types rather than a single template, and authors are expected to
select the template appropriate to their contribution type:

- **Empirical research articles** (quantitative or qualitative) follow a
  structure close to mainstream management journals: Abstract →
  Introduction → Theoretical Background/Literature Review → Methods →
  Results/Findings → Discussion → Conclusion (implications, limitations,
  future research). A distinct feature at this level is that the
  Discussion is expected to translate findings explicitly into
  implications for management teaching, curriculum design, or pedagogical
  practice, not only for theory.
- **Conceptual/theoretical essays** are a fully legitimate and common
  article type at the top of this field, not a lesser alternative to
  empirical work. These follow Abstract → Introduction (framing a
  provocation or gap in prevailing assumptions) → sequential argumentative
  sections (named by the substantive claim, not generic labels) →
  Implications for Practice/Education → Conclusion. There is no separate
  Methods section.
- **Review/synthesis articles** integrating literature on a pedagogical or
  developmental topic are also standard, following Abstract → Introduction
  → Review Method/Search Strategy → Thematic Synthesis → Future Research
  Agenda → Conclusion.
- A Discussion or Implications section connecting the work back to
  management education, teaching practice, or learner/developmental
  outcomes is a near-universal expectation, regardless of article type.
  This is the clearest structural marker distinguishing this field from
  general management research: every article type is expected to close by
  answering "what does this mean for how managers are taught or
  developed," not only "what does this mean for theory."
- Abstracts are unstructured, running approximately one paragraph, and
  are expected to state the problem, the contribution, and (for empirical
  work) the core method and finding.

## Voice, Person, and Register
- First-person plural ("we") is standard and normatively expected when
  describing authorial choices, arguments, and interpretive moves,
  including in conceptual essays, where an assertive authorial voice is
  part of the genre.
- Passive constructions are used for describing established procedures or
  prior findings, but active voice dominates when authors state their own
  claims, interpretations, or contributions.
- Register is formal but rhetorically more direct and occasionally more
  provocative than mainstream management journals — essays in this field
  are expected to stake out a clear, sometimes deliberately challenging
  position on prevailing assumptions in management education, rather than
  hedge toward a neutral synthesis.
- Register remains scholarly throughout; provocation is disciplined by
  argumentative rigor and engagement with literature, not informality.

## Sentence and Paragraph Style
- Sentences are typically medium to long, reflecting a preference for
  fully qualified, logically connected claims over terse assertions.
- Paragraphs are structured around a single claim or theoretical move,
  opened with a topic sentence and developed with supporting reasoning
  and citations.
- Conceptual/essay contributions favor a more overtly argumentative
  paragraph structure (claim, counter-consideration, resolution) than the
  descriptive-then-analytic paragraphing typical of empirical results
  sections.
- Section and subsection headings are used to signal the logical
  architecture of the argument, especially in conceptual essays where
  headings substitute for the methods/results scaffolding available in
  empirical papers.

## Argumentation and Rhetorical Moves
- The field follows a CARS-like (Create-a-Research-Space) framing:
  establish the territory (why management learning/education/development
  matters), identify a gap or a flawed assumption in current practice or
  theory, and stake the article's contribution as resolving or
  reframing it.
- A distinctive rhetorical move at this level is direct engagement with
  taken-for-granted assumptions or institutionalized practices in
  business education — articles frequently open by naming and
  problematizing a widely held belief or practice before offering a
  reframing.
- Contributions are framed dually: as advances to theory/knowledge about
  learning and development, and as actionable implications for
  educators, program designers, or organizations. Papers that only argue
  the former are considered incomplete at this level.
- Disagreement with prior work is expressed through direct, reasoned
  critique rather than indirect gap-filling language; authors are
  expected to state clearly what prior assumptions or findings they are
  challenging.

## Methods Conventions
- Empirical work reports methods in the past tense with procedural
  detail sufficient for replication, following general social science
  conventions (sampling, instrumentation, coding/analytic procedure).
- Both qualitative and quantitative methods are well represented at this
  level; qualitative, mixed-methods, and design-based/intervention
  studies are treated as equally rigorous options, reflecting the
  field's applied, practice-oriented subject matter.
- Studies of pedagogical interventions or curricular innovations are
  expected to include a description of the educational context and
  intervention design detailed enough to allow adaptation by other
  educators, in addition to standard methodological reporting.
- Robustness reporting follows general management/social science norms:
  sensitivity checks, alternative explanations, and limitations tied to
  design choices are addressed, typically in the discussion or a
  dedicated limitations subsection.

## Results and Reporting Norms
- Quantitative results are reported with precision (test statistics,
  effect sizes, confidence intervals or significance levels) in tables,
  with prose reserved for narrating the pattern and its interpretive
  significance rather than restating every value.
- Qualitative findings are reported thematically, supported by
  illustrative evidence (quotations, vignettes, or case detail), with
  clear separation between what was observed and what it is argued to
  mean.
- Interpretation is more freely integrated into the results narrative in
  this field than in more positivist management outlets — authors
  regularly connect findings to educational implications within the
  results/findings section itself, not only in a separate discussion.

## Discussion and Conclusion Norms
- Discussion sections are expected to move outward in two directions
  simultaneously: contribution to theory/knowledge on learning and
  development, and contribution to practice (teaching, curriculum,
  organizational development, policy for management education).
- Limitations are stated directly and specifically, tied to the study's
  design or scope, followed by concrete future research directions.
- Conclusions in conceptual essays often end with a call to action
  directed at educators, institutions, or the scholarly community,
  reflecting the field's applied and reformist orientation.

## Citation and Literature Engagement
- Citation density is moderate to high in introduction and
  theory/literature sections and lighter in results/findings sections,
  consistent with general management-field practice.
- Paraphrase dominates; direct quotation is reserved for qualitative
  evidence (interview or document excerpts) or when precise wording of a
  prior claim is the object of critique.
- Disagreement with prior literature is voiced explicitly and by name
  (naming the assumption, framework, or stream of work being
  challenged), rather than through vague hedged gap statements.
- Interdisciplinary citation — drawing on education, psychology,
  sociology, and organizational behavior alongside core management
  literature — is normal and expected, reflecting the field's
  boundary-spanning subject matter.

## Hedging and Epistemic Caution
- Empirical claims are hedged in proportion to evidentiary strength,
  following standard social-science practice (qualifying causal language
  where design does not support causal claims).
- Conceptual/essay contributions carry less hedging on the central thesis
  itself — the genre rewards a clearly stated, well-argued position — but
  still hedge appropriately around boundary conditions, scope, and
  generalizability of the argument.
- Practical recommendations for educators or institutions are typically
  qualified with attention to context-dependence (e.g., institutional
  type, learner population, cultural setting), acknowledging that
  practices proven effective in one setting may not transfer directly.

## What This Baseline Does NOT Specify
This baseline does not specify word or page limits, formatting or
citation-style mechanics, submission or peer-review procedures, or any
other publisher-specific requirement. Because this field has only one
ABS 4*/4 journal, this baseline reflects the norms of a single flagship
outlet rather than a convergence pattern validated across multiple peer
journals; where a journal-specific skill in this field provides more
detailed or divergent guidance, defer to it. Practice varies across the
field's broader (non-4*/4) journal population, particularly regarding
the balance between empirical and conceptual contributions and the
degree of practitioner-facing framing expected; no consensus norm beyond
what is stated above should be assumed for journals outside the ABS
4*/4 tier.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Accounting and Management Information Systems

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ACCOUNT
- **Publisher:** Faculty of Accounting and Management Information Systems, Bucharest University of Economic Studies, Romania
- **Scope:** Accounting, financial analysis, management information systems, and law. Publishes empirical, experimental, qualitative, and theoretical research across these domains.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://jamis.ase.ro/index.php/author-guidelines/
- **Field baseline:** account-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 15 recent articles from December 2024 to March 2026 across multiple issues

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
**Required sections (in order):** Introduction → Literature Review → Research Questions and Methodology → Results → Discussion → Conclusions

This structure differs slightly from field baseline's "Hypothesis Development / Theory" label, using instead explicit "Research Questions" and combined "Methodology" section placement. The explicit methodology integration reflects this journal's emphasis on research design transparency.

### Length
**Word limit: 5,000–10,000 words.** This is a RELAXATION of field baseline norms. This is a lower-tier journal (ABS 1) with tighter length constraints than the ABS 4*/4 baseline, which permits longer papers without stated limits. Authors should be more concise in literature review and results narration.

### Abstract
**Maximum 250 words, structured format.** Must include research objectives and contributions to field. No specific internal structure (e.g., Background/Methods/Results/Conclusions subsections) is mandated, but the abstract must address both components. This RELAXATION simplifies abstract structure relative to field baseline's implicit expectation of clear delineation across all components.

### Citations
**Harvard system (author-date, in-text).** Single author: "(Author, Year)." Multiple authors (3+): "(Author et al., Year)." DOI codes required if available. Follows FIELD_SKILL norm without deviation. ENFORCED.

### Headings
**Numbered with clear hierarchy:** 1, 2, 1.1, 1.2, etc. Abstract is excluded from numbering. This is more explicit than field baseline and represents ENFORCED standardization. Field baseline notes that practice varies (Roman vs. Arabic) across journals; this journal mandates Arabic numerals with decimal subsection numbering.

### Tables and Figures
**Maximum 10 combined tables and figures.** All tables must be in editable MS Word format. Figures must be in black, white, and grey (no color). Large items may be placed in appendices. This ENFORCED limit differs from field baseline, which does not specify a maximum. The grayscale requirement is explicit and strict.

### Language and Formatting
**English only.** Titles must be concise and informative; abbreviations should be avoided where possible. Manuscript must be grammatically correct with no spelling errors. These are standards aligned with FIELD_SKILL with no significant deviation.

### Submission and Review
**Blind review required.** Manuscript must not contain information allowing reviewer identification of authors. Submission via email in MS Word format with three components: cover page (authors, affiliations, funding sources), blind manuscript, originality statement, and AI assistance disclosure. These are administrative constraints not covered by FIELD_SKILL.

### Keywords and JEL Codes
**4–6 keywords required,** reflecting paper specificity. Avoid general/plural terms and multiple concepts. Up to 6 JEL classification codes required. These are standard field conventions aligned with FIELD_SKILL with no deviation beyond explicit quantity requirements.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
**Section naming and order:** FIELD_SKILL describes "Hypothesis Development / Theory / Background" as the second major section; this journal requires explicit "Literature Review" followed by separate "Research Questions and Methodology." The explicit separation of methods from theory/hypothesis framing reflects this journal's lower tier (ABS 1) and its emphasis on accessible methodological description. Robustness and additional analysis may appear as subsections within Results or as a standalone section; no explicit guidelines mandate their placement.

**Length constraint impact:** The 5,000–10,000 word limit forces tighter integration of literature, methodology, and results than ABS 4*/4 papers typically employ. Authors should expect to reduce literature review breadth relative to field baseline's expectation of 15–40 citations in the introduction. Hypothesis enumeration in the introduction may be briefer; results narration must be denser (fewer replications of table content in prose).

### Voice and Register
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Active voice dominates; first-person plural ("we") is standard. Formal register throughout. No deviations observed in spot-checked articles.

### Citation Density
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. High in introduction and hypothesis development, medium in methods, low to medium in results, medium in conclusion. No explicit deviations observed. Paraphrase is universal; quotation from prior scholarship is absent (except in qualitative/interpretive papers where interview transcripts are primary evidence).

### Statistical Reporting
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Coefficient estimates, significance levels (1%, 5%, 10%), and t-statistics are reported in prose alongside table references. Economic significance is increasingly reported. No deviations from field baseline observed.

### Hedging and Epistemic Caution
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Medium level of hedging overall. Well-identified results warrant direct language ("we find," "we document"); mechanistic interpretations use softened language ("suggest," "indicate," "consistent with"). Limitations are acknowledged explicitly.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- **Sentence and paragraph style:** Medium to long sentences; dense paragraphs in introduction and results sections. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Argumentation patterns:** Introduction follows CARS-like structure (establish significance → survey gap → describe study → enumerate results → state contributions). FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Methods presentation (tense, procedural detail, identification strategy, robustness):** Past tense, high detail, explicit identification discussion, expectation of 3–5 robustness checks. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Results and reporting norms (tables vs. prose, null results, reporting structure):** Tables as primary vehicle, prose interpretation, organization by hypothesis/question, null results reported transparently. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Discussion and conclusion norms (merged vs. separate, content, tone):** Merged or separate sections both acceptable; restatement of question, summary of findings, explicit contributions, explicit limitations paragraph, confident but not overreaching tone. FIELD DEFAULT.

## Common Pitfalls Specific to This Journal

1. **Exceeding word limits:** Authors from ABS 4*/4 traditions may draft papers at 12,000–15,000 words. This journal's 5,000–10,000 limit requires aggressive editing. Cut literature review breadth, streamline results narration, and prioritize robustness checks over multiple mechanism tests.

2. **Unstructured or overly long abstracts:** The 250-word limit is strict and must be enforced. Abstracts exceeding this word count are grounds for desk rejection. Ensure abstract explicitly states research objective and contribution; vague objectives are not acceptable even if under the word limit.

3. **Color in figures:** Figures submitted in color will be rejected or must be resubmitted. Ensure all figures are prepared in grayscale from the outset (black, white, grey); charts should use patterns or shading, not color, for distinction.

4. **Over-ambitious analysis:** The length constraint and lower journal tier mean papers attempting both main tests and 5–7 robustness checks may exceed limits. Prioritize: main tests + 3 key robustness checks. Relegate additional robustness to appendices if length permits, or accept that some sensitivity tests will not be reported.

5. **Ignoring blind review requirements:** Cover pages, headers, or author-identifying information in the manuscript body will trigger desk rejection. Ensure the blind version is genuinely anonymous before submission.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

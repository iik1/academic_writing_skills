# Journal Writing Skill: CASE Journal

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN
- **Publisher:** Emerald Publishing (journal of The CASE Association)
- **Scope:** Publishes factual teaching cases and case exercises spanning the full spectrum of business and management disciplines, each accompanied by a Teaching Note/Instructor's Manual. Also accepts articles on case teaching, case writing, and case reviewing (conceptual or original-research pieces).

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
CASE Journal does NOT use the empirical/theoretical research-article template in FIELD_SKILL. It publishes two stand-alone documents per submission:
1. **Teaching Case (narrative body)**: Opening Hook (short scene-setting paragraph designed to generate reader interest — explicitly NOT a synopsis) → Synopsis (≤250 words) → Industry description (size, profit drivers, structural factors) → case narrative body → Closing Hook (returns to opening scenario, restates the issue) → Exhibits (tables/charts/diagrams/photos, labeled Exhibit 1, Exhibit 2…) → References.
2. **Teaching Note / Instructor's Manual (separate document)**: target audience/intended course, Learning Objectives, relevant theoretical concepts or models, research methodology used to develop the case, Discussion Questions with suggested responses, teaching strategies.
Cases must be factual (with permissions/releases where required); fictional cases, traditional research-based cases, and teaching cases submitted without an accompanying Teaching Note are explicitly not published.

### Length
- Standard Teaching Case: up to 1,500 words (per current Compact/standard case guidance) for the case narrative body — much shorter than any FIELD_SKILL article-type length band (8,000–25,000 words).
- Compact Case: 500–1,000 words, a distinct short-form submission type with fewer Learning Objectives (1–2) and fewer Discussion Questions than a standard case.
- No conventional "word count for research article" applies; length is governed by case-writing conventions, not empirical-paper norms.

### Abstract
Replaced by two elements, neither structured in FIELD_SKILL's sense: an unstructured Opening Hook (narrative teaser, not a summary) and a Synopsis (≤250 words, summarizes the case for instructors deciding whether to adopt it). No citations in either.

### Citations
APA (most recent edition), consistent with FIELD_SKILL. Stand-alone "References" section required at the end of both the case narrative and the Teaching Note.

### Headings
No hypothesis/proposition numbering (H1, P1…) applies — cases are narrative, not deductive theory-testing. Sections instead follow the fixed case-writing labels (Opening Hook, Synopsis, Industry, Exhibits, Closing Hook in the case; Learning Objectives, Discussion Questions in the Teaching Note).

### Tables and Figures
Called "Exhibits," not "Tables"/"Figures," and numbered sequentially as a single series (Exhibit 1, Exhibit 2…) rather than separate Table/Figure sequences.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- No fictional cases.
- No traditional research-based (empirical/hypothesis-testing) cases.
- No teaching case without an accompanying Teaching Note.
- No prior publication or simultaneous submission of the case in substantially similar form elsewhere.
- Permissions must be cleared for any non-author-created content before submission.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
Case narratives are written in expository/narrative third person describing a real company or protagonist's situation, not in the first-person-plural "we argue/we find" register FIELD_SKILL describes for empirical papers. The Teaching Note reverts to a more conventional instructional register (addressing the instructor directly). PRACTICE ONLY / genre default, not comparable to FIELD_SKILL's voice guidance, which assumes a research-article author.

### Statistical Reporting
Not applicable — cases are descriptive/narrative; no hypothesis testing, coefficients, or FIELD_SKILL-style results reporting occurs in the case body. Financial/operational figures may appear in Exhibits but are not reported with FIELD_SKILL's regression-table conventions.

### Citation Density
Cases themselves are citation-light in the narrative (facts are sourced to company disclosures, interviews, news, or public data rather than a dense academic literature); the Teaching Note carries the academic citations linking the case to theoretical concepts/models. This is a lighter, differently-distributed citation pattern than FIELD_SKILL's dense Introduction/Theory sections. RELAXATION appropriate to genre, not a stated numeric norm — PRACTICE ONLY.

### Structural Deviations
Entire macro-structure differs from all four FIELD_SKILL article-type templates (see Hard Constraints > Structure). This is the dominant delta for this journal and is ENFORCED (stated in guidelines, and consistent with the titles/format observed in the spot-check sample).

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
None observed beyond the case/Teaching-Note format itself.

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting a case without a Teaching Note — an explicit, stated prohibition.
- Writing the Opening Hook as a summary/synopsis rather than an interest-generating narrative teaser; the two are distinct required elements.
- Applying FIELD_SKILL's empirical-paper structure (Theory/Hypotheses, Methods, Results) instead of the case + Teaching Note format — this journal does not publish traditional research-based cases.
- Exceeding the ~1,500-word case-body limit (or 500–1,000 for a Compact Case) by writing at typical FIELD_SKILL article length.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

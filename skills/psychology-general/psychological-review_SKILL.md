# Journal Writing Skill: Psychological Review

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 4
- **Field:** PSYCH (GENERAL)
- **Publisher:** American Psychological Association
- **Scope:** Publishes theoretical contributions to any area of scientific
  psychology, including systematic evaluation/comparison of alternative
  theories. Also publishes shorter "Theoretical Notes" (commentaries,
  critiques of prior theoretical models, meta-theoretical discourse).
  Does NOT accept primary empirical research as such.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/rev (official APA
  page; detailed instructions-to-authors accessed via secondary sources,
  see Sources table)
- **Field baseline:** psychology-general-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 3 full/near-full manuscripts or detailed
  records, plus title/abstract-level review of ~10 additional recent
  titles (2024–2026) via search indices (ResearchGate, PsycNet, arXiv
  mirror). Full text: Tsang, Barrentine, Chadha, Oishi, & Wood (2024/2025),
  "Social Exploration: How and Why People Seek New Connections,"
  https://dx.doi.org/10.1037/rev0000499. Title/abstract only: Frost et al.
  (2025) "Statistical learning subserves a higher purpose..." (10.1037/rev0000547);
  Goudeau et al. (2024) "What Causes Social Class Disparities in
  Education?"; Livingston et al. (2024) "The Double Empathy Problem: A
  Derivation Chain Analysis and Cautionary Note" (Theoretical Note);
  Gluth, Deakin, & Rieskamp (2026) "A Theory of Multiattribute Search and
  Choice." Access chain exhausted for full text beyond one open
  Chorus-hosted manuscript; remaining evidence is front-matter/title-level,
  as permitted when full text is paywalled.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No fixed IMRaD requirement. This journal is the field's primary
theory-only outlet: manuscripts must be theoretical contributions, not
primarily empirical reports. Illustrative empirical material may be
included but cannot be the primary content. A separate, shorter
"Theoretical Note" format exists for commentaries, critiques of prior
theoretical models, and meta-theoretical discourse — this is a distinct
submission type from a full theoretical article, with no equivalent in
most other PSYCH (GENERAL) journals in the field baseline.

### Length
No fixed word limit stated by the journal itself; manuscripts exceeding
approximately 15,000 words require the author to justify the length.
This is looser than the typical empirical-report length norms assumed in
FIELD_SKILL.

### Abstract
Unstructured narrative abstract (not divided into Background/Method/
Results/Conclusion subheadings), consistent with APA 7th-edition
convention for non-empirical articles. Approximately 200–250 words.
Confirmed in the sampled full-text article: a single unstructured
paragraph followed by a "Keywords" line.

### Citations
Standard APA style (7th edition), per general APA journal policy. No
journal-specific deviation observed.

### Headings
Thematic, researcher-defined headings organized around theoretical
constructs and logical structure (e.g., "The explore-exploit tradeoff,"
"Social exploration-exploitation," "Person-level factors," "State-level
factors," "Situational factors," "Summary and future directions") rather
than IMRaD labels. This matches the review/theoretical-journal variant
already described in FIELD_SKILL; no journal-specific deviation from
that variant.

### Tables and Figures
No journal-specific figure/table formatting rules beyond standard APA
style found. Note as a structural pattern (see below): a table
consolidating the paper's full set of generated hypotheses/predictions
appears as a distinctive closing device in the sampled article, though
this is PRACTICE ONLY, not a stated requirement.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Manuscripts that are primarily empirical (i.e., structured as a
standalone empirical report rather than a theoretical contribution with
illustrative data) are explicitly out of scope and are returned/redirected
to empirical journals rather than reviewed.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
This is the sharpest and most consequential delta from FIELD_SKILL.
FIELD_SKILL treats "review and theoretical journals" (including this one)
as a minority structural variant within a largely empirical field
baseline. For Psychological Review specifically, the theoretical/
integrative-review structure is not a variant — it is the only accepted
structure. Authors should not default to IMRaD, Method/Results sections,
or hypothesis-testing framing unless illustrating a point with existing
data. ENFORCED (stated in scope guidelines and confirmed in the sampled
article, which has no Method or Results section at all).

### Citation Density
Citation density is markedly higher and more uniform across the entire
paper than FIELD_SKILL's empirical-report norm (dense in Intro/
Discussion, sparse in Method/Results). Because there is no Method/Results
section, near-every paragraph — including person-level and situational
mechanism claims — carries multiple citations synthesizing prior work.
This matches FIELD_SKILL's note that review articles "sustain much higher
citation density throughout," but the degree is worth flagging explicitly
for this journal: citation density approaches that of a systematic
literature synthesis rather than a typical discussion section. ENFORCED
(confirmed pervasively in the sampled article).

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
- Closing tables that consolidate the paper's generated hypotheses/
  predictions as a numbered list (e.g., "Table 1. List of testable
  hypotheses...") appear as a distinctive closing device for integrative
  theoretical papers. PRACTICE ONLY — observed in the sampled article, not
  stated in guidelines, and likely to vary by paper.
- A distinct "Theoretical Note" submission type (shorter commentaries on
  existing theory, critiques, or meta-theoretical discourse) exists
  alongside full theoretical articles. GUIDELINE-STATED structural
  category with no equivalent noted in FIELD_SKILL.
- Explicit invited-revision/associate-editor attribution lines and
  funding disclosures appear on the manuscript title page as part of
  APA's standard author-note requirements; this is a general APA
  publishing-policy matter (conflict-of-interest and prior-dissemination
  disclosure), not unique prose style, so treated as FIELD DEFAULT/APA
  policy rather than a style delta.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- Voice and person: FIELD DEFAULT (first-person plural for authors' own
  moves, e.g., "we argue," "we propose," consistent with FIELD_SKILL)
- Sentence and paragraph style: FIELD DEFAULT (plain declarative prose,
  clear topic sentences, explicit signposting such as "First,... Second,...")
- Argumentation/rhetorical moves: FIELD DEFAULT (CARS-style funneling from
  broad phenomenon to specific gap, framed constructively)
- Hedging and epistemic caution: FIELD DEFAULT (explicit qualification of
  predictions as hypotheses/predictions rather than established fact,
  e.g., "we predict," "we expect," "future research should test")
- Citation style/formatting minutiae: FIELD DEFAULT (standard APA 7th
  edition)

---

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting a primarily empirical paper (even with strong theoretical
  framing) — this journal will redirect it rather than review it; the
  theoretical contribution must be the primary content, with any data
  used only illustratively.
- Defaulting to a Method/Results/Discussion structure out of habit; the
  accepted structure is thematic and construct-organized throughout, not
  merely in the Introduction/Discussion as in most other PSYCH (GENERAL)
  journals.
- Underciting mechanism/synthesis claims relative to what this journal
  expects — citation density should remain high in every section, not
  taper off outside the literature review, since the entire paper
  functions as a literature synthesis and theory-building exercise.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

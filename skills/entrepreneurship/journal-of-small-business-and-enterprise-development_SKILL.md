# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Small Business and Enterprise Development

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ENT-SBM
- **Publisher:** Emerald Publishing Limited
- **Scope:** International peer-reviewed journal publishing empirical and conceptual research on entrepreneurship, small business management, and enterprise development in diverse economic contexts (developed and emerging economies).
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.emeraldgrouppublishing.com/journal/jsbed (submission portal via ScholarOne)
- **Field baseline:** entrepreneurship-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 10 (Vol 32 Issue 1, 2025; Vol 31, 2024)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines & Observed Practice)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Empirical articles follow a consistent macro structure with seven labeled sections:
1. **Purpose** (replaces "Introduction"—states research question and gap directly)
2. **Design/Methodology/Approach** (replaces "Methods"—includes design type, sample/data, analytical approach)
3. **Findings** (presents results; integration of interpretation varies by methodology—qualitative case studies blend findings with thematic synthesis, quantitative studies separate coefficients from discussion)
4. **Research Limitations/Implications** (explicit section addressing validity threats and forward-looking research agenda)
5. **Practical Implications** (explicit, standalone section for practitioner/policy relevance—matches field baseline emphasis)
6. **Originality/Value** (explicit statement of contribution and novelty—not standard in ABS 4*/4 baseline)
7. **Keywords** (required; indexed terms provided separately from abstract)

Conceptual and systematic review papers follow the same section ordering but substitute "Design/Methodology/Approach" with a transparent literature methodology (e.g., "systematic analysis of existing published definitions" for framework papers; "systematic review protocol" for SLRs including search strategy, inclusion/exclusion criteria, data extraction).

### Length
- Word limits not stated in accessible guidelines; observed practice in Vol 32 Issue 1 articles ranges from 8,000–15,000 words, suggesting no strict enforced cap but expectation of substantial manuscripts (FIELD DEFAULT).

### Abstract
- Structured format with implicit Purpose-Design-Findings-Originality hierarchy (not explicitly stated as "structured abstract" in guidelines, but universal in articles).
- Appears to be under 250 words (FIELD DEFAULT).

### Citations
- Citation density and style: Follows FIELD DEFAULT (paraphrase-heavy, high density in intro/theory, lower in methods/results, moderate in discussion). No journal-specific deviations observed. (FIELD DEFAULT)

### Headings
- Section headings use title case, bold or numbered. All observed articles use heading hierarchy (section > subsection > sub-subsection) consistently. (FIELD DEFAULT)

### Tables and Figures
- Integral to results narrative, especially in quantitative studies (descriptive statistics, correlation tables, regression tables, path diagrams). Qualitative studies use figures less frequently. Practice matches FIELD_SKILL expectation of tables and figures as integral. (FIELD DEFAULT)

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Submissions must be original (stated in guidelines).
- No noted prohibitions on methodology or rhetorical style beyond standard journal standards.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
**Delta: Section labeling and terminology (ENFORCED)**
- This journal uses "Purpose" as the opening research-framing section and "Design/Methodology/Approach" as the methods label. These replace the "Introduction" and "Methods" terminology expected in the ABS 4*/4 baseline.
- All 10 spot-checked articles use this exact nomenclature, with "Purpose" always introduced in the first section and "Design/Methodology/Approach" always used for the second major section.
- "Research Limitations/Implications" is labeled as a distinct section, not embedded in Discussion (ENFORCED practice across all articles).

**Delta: Explicit "Originality/Value" section (ENFORCED)**
- A standalone, often paragraph-length section explicitly articulating the paper's contribution to theory, practice, or methodology appears consistently after "Practical Implications" in all observed articles.
- This is more explicit and structured than the "Contributions" subsection in ABS 4*/4 baseline, which is typically embedded within Discussion.
- Language in this section is evaluative and claims-oriented: "This study extends/challenges/bridges/synthesizes existing literature by..." or "The originality of this work lies in..."

### Results and Discussion Integration
**Delta: Findings presentation in qualitative studies (PRACTICE ONLY)**
- Qualitative case studies and interview-based research show a looser boundary between Findings and Discussion compared to ABS 4*/4 baseline norm.
- Example: The government venture capital article weaves thematic findings ("monitoring, mentoring, and nurturing create value") directly with interpretive claims ("building capacity through skills acquisition") within the same Findings section, rather than strict separation of reporting from interpretation.
- Quantitative studies (e.g., structural equation modeling, probit regression) maintain the sharper Findings-Discussion boundary expected in FIELD_SKILL.
- **Classification:** PRACTICE ONLY—observed in approximately 4 of 10 qualitative/mixed-method articles, not stated as explicit guideline. Quantitative articles adhere to FIELD_SKILL norm of separation.

### Keywords
**Delta: Indexing separate from abstract (ENFORCED)**
- Keywords appear as a separate line-item after the abstract, typically 5–10 terms, always in English.
- This is standard Emerald practice and not deviations from FIELD_SKILL, but differs from some ABS 4* journals that embed keywords within abstract or abstract metadata. (ENFORCED/FIELD DEFAULT)

- Voice and person: FIELD DEFAULT (first-person plural, active voice throughout)

- Argumentation and rhetorical moves: FIELD DEFAULT (CARS-style, explicit gap-identification, enumerated contributions)
- Methods conventions: FIELD DEFAULT (past-tense, procedural transparency, construct measurement detail, robustness checks, common-method bias discussion in appropriate location)
- Citation and literature engagement: FIELD DEFAULT (high density in intro/theory, paraphrase dominates, self-citation of canonical theorists)
- Hedging and epistemic caution: FIELD DEFAULT (causal claims softened in Discussion/Limitations; results reported in direct language; forward-looking hedges as convention for converting limitations into research agenda)

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Misusing "Introduction" instead of "Purpose"**: This journal has standardized on "Purpose" as the opening section label. Submitting with "Introduction" may trigger a request to reformat.
2. **Embedding Originality claims within Discussion rather than as a standalone section**: The "Originality/Value" section is not optional—it must be a separate, explicitly labeled section. Do not fold it into Discussion or Conclusion.
3. **Separating Findings sharply from interpretation in qualitative work**: While quantitative studies benefit from strict Results-Discussion separation (per FIELD_SKILL), qualitative case studies and interview-based research in this journal often integrate findings and thematic interpretation fluidly. Forcing artificial separation may make qualitative narratives rigid. Check your methodology type.
4. **Omitting explicit "Research Limitations/Implications" section**: Unlike some journals that treat limitations as a subsection within Discussion, this journal expects it as a labeled, standalone section following Findings.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

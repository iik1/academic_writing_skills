# Journal Writing Skill: Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** PSYCH (GENERAL)
- **Publisher:** SAGE (journal of the Experimental Psychology Society; formerly published by Taylor & Francis/Psychology Press)
- **Scope:** General experimental psychology — attention, perception, learning, memory, language, and reasoning. Accepts single-experiment manuscripts as well as multi-experiment papers, provided the data and conclusions are of interest. Also runs a two-stage Registered Reports track.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/author-instructions/qjp (direct fetch blocked; content triangulated via search-engine snippets and cached PDFs)
- **Field baseline:** psychology-general-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 read in detail (full text or near-full text), several more at abstract/metadata level: Lador-Weizman & Deutsch (2024, QJEP 77(11), full text via cached publisher PDF); Mercer (2025, "Familiarity influences on proactive interference in verbal memory," PMC12335633); Bains, Spaulding, Ricketts & Krishnan (2024, "Providing choice enhances reading motivation," PMC12982546); Schimke, Copland, Gomersall & Angwin (2023, "To sleep or not to sleep?," PMC10960315); plus abstract-level checks on additional 2023–2025 articles (attentional boost effect, working memory prioritisation, animacy/memory) via search snippets.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Standard IMRaD (Introduction, Method, Results, Discussion, References). Multi-experiment papers use "Experiment 1," "Experiment 2," etc. (not "Study 1/Study 2") as the section label, each with its own Method/Results/Discussion, closing with a General Discussion — consistent with FIELD_SKILL's multi-study variant. Registered Reports follow a two-stage format: Stage 1 (Introduction, Method, Proposed Analyses) submitted and reviewed before data collection; Stage 2 (adds Results and Discussion) submitted after data collection, with publication guaranteed if the approved Stage 1 protocol was followed.

### Length
No word limit for Original Articles; the editors "encourage concise submissions" but do not enforce a cap. This relaxes FIELD_SKILL's implicit expectation of proportionate length — long or short manuscripts are both acceptable on their merits. [RELAXATION — ABS 3 may relax but not redefine baseline.]

### Abstract
Single, unstructured paragraph (no labeled Background/Method/Results/Conclusion subsections), consistent with FIELD_SKILL norms for empirical psychology journals. A minimum of 4–5 keywords is required immediately after the abstract.

### Citations
APA author–date style, consistent with FIELD_SKILL. (Not numbered/bracketed, despite what some automated extraction tools suggested — confirmed directly from full-text PDF inspection.)

### Headings
Follows FIELD_SKILL conventions; no distinctive heading requirements beyond the "Experiment N" convention noted above.

### Tables and Figures
No journal-specific deviation found; integrated near first reference, consistent with FIELD_SKILL.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Ethics statement is a hard requirement for any study involving human participants: full name and institution of the approving Ethics Committee/IRB plus the approval number must be stated (or an explicit waiver noted). This is stated in guidelines and consistently observed in sampled articles (approval numbers cited in Methods/Procedure). Submissions that don't conform to formatting requirements may be returned before peer review or desk-rejected if out of scope.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
Articles carry a distinctive **"Transparency and openness statement"** or **"Data availability statement"** subsection, typically placed at the start of Results or in the closing matter, giving a direct repository link (OSF or similar) or explicitly stating why data cannot be shared. This is more consistently a named, standalone subsection here than FIELD_SKILL's general "near-universal requirement... placed after the abstract or in the Method section" — QJEP articles place it flexibly (start of Results, or end-of-article Data Availability Statement) rather than fixed to Method. [ENFORCED — stated in guidelines, confirmed in all sampled articles.]

Author contributions are reported using the standardized **CRediT taxonomy** (e.g., "Conceptualisation, supervision, resources... "), a SAGE house convention not mentioned in FIELD_SKILL. [PRACTICE ONLY, but consistent across sample — likely SAGE-wide house style.]

Discursive asides and secondary technical details (e.g., orthographic details, model-fitting caveats) are frequently pushed into **numbered endnotes/footnotes** rather than parenthetical text or appendices, more heavily than typical for this field. [PRACTICE ONLY, low-to-moderate prevalence.]

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
British/Commonwealth spelling conventions are used throughout (e.g., "generalisation," "randomisation," "organised"), reflecting the journal's EPS/UK editorial base. FIELD_SKILL does not specify a spelling variant. [PRACTICE ONLY — near-universal in sample, worth following for UK-based submissions.]

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting a structured (labeled-subsection) abstract — QJEP abstracts are single unstructured paragraphs.
- Omitting a standalone data availability/transparency statement — treat it as close to mandatory even though it is not formally listed as a required section header in the guidelines summary.
- Using US spelling in a UK-edited submission context — sampled articles consistently use British spelling.
- Labeling multi-experiment sections "Study 1/Study 2" instead of the journal's conventional "Experiment 1/Experiment 2."

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: International Journal of Management Practice

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN
- **Publisher:** Inderscience Publishers
- **Scope:** Aims to "turn management theory into management practice" — publishes papers that translate existing theory into practitioner-usable frameworks or that document leading-edge managerial practices, across HRM, marketing, strategy, finance, entrepreneurship, and sustainability topics. Explicitly practitioner-facing rather than theory-building.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.inderscience.com/jhome.php?jcode=ijmp (journal home); manuscript-prep rules are the Inderscience-wide standard at https://www.inderscience.com/mobile/inauthors/
- **Field baseline:** mgmt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 13 (titles/abstracts across 2021–2026 issues; full text not accessible — paywalled). Reviewed: "Authentic leadership, social support and employee voice" (v18(6) 2025); "Role of organisational culture in promoting workplace spirituality" (v19(3) 2026); "A systematic literature review and bibliometric evaluation of the use of analytics in organisations" (v18(3) 2025); "An exploration of government policies for supporting informal entrepreneurship in Zimbabwe" (v17(1) 2024); "Strategic receptivity of maintenance quality function deployment across heterogeneous organisational cultures" (v16(5) 2023); plus 8 additional titles surveyed from the 2020–2026 table of contents for scope/topic pattern confirmation.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict. Note: these are Inderscience's publisher-wide standard rules (no IJMP-specific supplement found), so they apply uniformly across all Inderscience management titles, not uniquely to this journal.

### Structure
No fixed section template is mandated beyond standard IMRaD; briefs and research notes are explicitly not accepted — only full research articles, case studies, technical reports, and expanded conference papers.

### Length
5,000–7,000 words excluding figures, tables, and references (shorter than FIELD_SKILL's 10,000–18,000 word range for top-tier MGMT outlets). Papers exceeding 7,000 words are accepted if quality warrants it.

### Abstract
Unstructured, factual and precise, under 150 words (tighter than FIELD_SKILL's 150–250 word range). No citations unless essential. Followed by 10–15 keywords (larger than typical field norms).

### Citations
Harvard (author-date) style, references alphabetized at the end of the article — consistent with FIELD_SKILL's APA-style author-date convention; no material difference.

### Headings
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.

### Tables and Figures
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (no journal-specific rule found beyond standard placement/numbering).

### Explicit Prohibitions
Previously published or under-review-elsewhere work rejected; conference papers must be substantially rewritten with copyright clearance; briefs/research notes not accepted; UK spelling required.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates.

### Sample Size and Rigor
RELAXATION. Empirical samples are markedly smaller than FIELD_SKILL norms — e.g., 15 managers, 36 interview participants, 72 bibliometric records, 251 survey respondents — versus FIELD_SKILL's "dozens to tens of thousands." Robustness checks and endogeneity treatment (IV, DiD, PSM) described in FIELD_SKILL as routine are largely absent; single cross-sectional surveys analyzed via PLS-SEM, SEM, or simple regression are the norm.

### Theoretical Framing
RELAXATION. Papers rarely develop or extend named theoretical frameworks as scaffolding in the FIELD_SKILL sense. Many use an existing theory (e.g., stakeholder theory, TPM/QFD) instrumentally to organize a practitioner problem rather than to test or refine the theory itself. Formal numbered Hypotheses (H1, H2…) or Propositions (P1, P2…) appear inconsistently across the sample — several articles proceed directly from research questions/objectives to empirical findings without a distinct hypothesis-development section.

### Contribution Framing
PRACTICE ONLY. Contributions are framed as managerial/practical recommendations ("policymakers should…," "organisations can apply this scorecard to…") rather than the FIELD_SKILL pattern of "We extend Theory X by…" Practical implications are often the primary stated contribution, not a secondary section after theoretical contribution.

### Methods Mix
PRACTICE ONLY. High prevalence of systematic-literature-review/bibliometric papers (PRISMA, Biblioshiny, VOSviewer-style clustering) and small-sample qualitative/case studies alongside survey-based quantitative work — a broader methodological mix than FIELD_SKILL's quantitative-deductive/qualitative-inductive split, with review-type papers appearing far more often than at top-tier outlets.

### Empirical Setting
PRACTICE ONLY. Strong concentration of samples from India, Zimbabwe, South Africa, and other emerging/developing-economy contexts, and from single-organization or single-sector convenience samples — not a stated journal norm but a consistent pattern across the sample checked.

## Common Pitfalls
- Do not over-invest in a FIELD_SKILL-style extended Theory-and-Hypotheses section with multiple numbered hypotheses and boundary-condition reasoning; a shorter research-question-driven framing tied to a practical problem is more consistent with accepted articles here.
- Do not assume large-sample archival data or panel methods are expected; small convenience or single-organization samples are standard and accepted.
- Lead the contribution statement with practical/managerial application, not theoretical extension — this is the journal's stated raison d'être.
- Keep the abstract under 150 words (not the 150–250 word range typical of top-tier MGMT journals).

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: British Actuarial Journal

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** FINANCE
- **Publisher:** Cambridge University Press (for the Institute and Faculty of Actuaries, IFoA)
- **Scope:** Publishes sessional research papers presented to the IFoA's sessional programme, along with transcripts of the discussions/debates that follow, plus presidential addresses and practitioner-facing memoirs. Covers life/non-life insurance, pensions, health, risk management, and emerging actuarial applications (e.g. quantum computing, machine learning, biodiversity/ESG risk).
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-actuarial-journal/information/instructions-contributors and https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-actuarial-journal/information/author-instructions/preparing-your-materials
- **Field baseline:** finance-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 full abstract/structure reads (Operational resilience in the UK financial sector: practical guidance, 2025; Quantum internal models for Solvency II and quantitative risk management, 2025; Biodiversity & justice, n.d.; On contemporary mortality models for actuarial use I: practice, 2025; plus corroborating title/abstract-level search snippets for ~5 additional 2024-2026 articles including Validating operational risk models, Inequality – actuarial perspectives, and Reinventing Pareto). Full text is Gold Open Access but the WebFetch tool could only reliably extract abstract/structure-level content, not full body text — consistent with the ABS 1 good-faith sampling allowance.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Required sequence: Title → Author name(s) (corresponding author marked with asterisk) → date/place of presentation (if a sessional paper) → Abstract → Keywords → correspondence details → Introduction (numbered §1) → main numbered sections/subsections → Acknowledgements (optional) → References → Appendices. Many published items are not standalone research articles but sessional papers followed by a published transcript of the discussion/debate held at the sessional meeting — a publication type with no equivalent in FIELD_SKILL.

### Length
No explicit word or page limit stated in the guidelines.

### Abstract
Unstructured, single field, **100–200 words**, one long or two-to-three short paragraphs. Must state main objectives and conclusions and "encourage readers to continue." Must NOT contain references. Followed by up to 5 keywords/short phrases, each with an initial capital.

### Citations
Author-year, matching FIELD_SKILL, but with explicit house formatting: in-text "Smith & White (1990)" or "(see Jones et al., 1975)" (ampersand, not "and"). Reference-list entries: surname first, all authors' initials given, "&" before the last author, year in brackets followed by a full stop, e.g. "Smith, A.B. & White, C.D. (1990). The Quick Brown Fox. British Actuarial Journal, 10, 71–139."

### Headings
Title uses initial-capital-only (sentence case), not title case. Numbered sections/subsections (1., 2., 2.1., 2.2.). All paragraphs are numbered for cross-reference purposes — a formatting convention not present in FIELD_SKILL journals. Acronyms spelled out in full on first use, with the abbreviation given in brackets immediately after.

### Tables and Figures
Numbered consecutively with captions; referenced explicitly in text (e.g., "see table 4"), never via positional words "above"/"below". Supplied as separate files, not embedded in text. Photographs: TIFF or high-res JPEG, minimum 300 dpi; line illustrations 800–1200 dpi. No tinted panels or borders. No PowerPoint files, Word-generated images, or web-sourced GIFs.

### Explicit Prohibitions
No simultaneous submission ("should not have been copyrighted or submitted for possible publication elsewhere"). No embedded figures. No "above"/"below" cross-references. No PowerPoint/Word-image/GIF figure files.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
BAJ's core publication unit is frequently a **sessional paper + published discussion transcript**, not a self-contained research article. This dual format (paper followed by a verbatim record of the sessional debate) is unique among sampled articles and has no counterpart in FIELD_SKILL. PRACTICE ONLY / ENFORCED by journal convention — confirmed via guidelines description and the "Validating operational risk models" discussion transcript.

### Voice and Register
Sampled abstracts and section structures read as more directly practitioner/policy-oriented than the FIELD_SKILL empirical-identification register: papers foreground "practical guidance," "implementation issues," and "actuarial skills" sections rather than identification strategy or econometric framing. This reflects the journal's practitioner-facing scope rather than a stated stylistic rule. PRACTICE ONLY.

### Structural Deviations (headings)
Sampled papers commonly end with an actuary-facing implications/application section (e.g., "Actuarial Skills and Their Role in Operational Resilience," "Implications for Actuaries," "Opportunities for Actuaries") before or instead of a traditional academic Conclusion — a section type absent from FIELD_SKILL's canonical structure. PRACTICE ONLY.

### Citation Density
Not assessable from abstract-level content in this sample; no delta identified. FIELD DEFAULT assumed.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Numbered-paragraph convention (every paragraph numbered for cross-referencing) is a distinctive house style not found in FIELD_SKILL journals. ENFORCED (stated in guidelines).

## Common Pitfalls
- Treating BAJ as a standard empirical-finance journal and omitting the practitioner-facing "implications for actuaries" closing material that many published papers include.
- Using "above"/"below" for table/figure cross-references — explicitly prohibited; use explicit numbers ("see table 4").
- Embedding figures/tables in the manuscript text instead of supplying them as separate files.
- Writing a structured or over-length abstract; BAJ wants a short (100–200 word), unstructured, reference-free abstract.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Housing Economics

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Elsevier
- **Scope:** Economic research related to housing markets, including transaction outcomes, mortgage finance, policy, and international studies. Encourages careful analytical technique on housing-related questions.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/journal-of-housing-economics/publish/guide-for-authors (paywalled; public information extracted from search results)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 2 accessible preprints ([arXiv:2602.18592](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.18592) — Hungary housing price-at-risk; [arXiv:2512.01139](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.01139) — Australian housing market)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
**Flexible:** No strict formatting requirements. Journal states "manuscripts must contain the essential elements needed to convey your manuscript." Standard elements are Abstract, Keywords, Introduction, Materials and Methods, Results, Conclusions, Artwork, and Tables with Captions. Sections must be "clearly defined" but the number, order, and naming are not prescribed.

**Heading format:** Headings use numbered Arabic numerals in hierarchical format (1 Introduction, 2.1 Data, etc.), as confirmed in recent articles. Either numbered or unnumbered is acceptable per guidelines, but practice favors numbered.

### Length
No explicit word or page limit stated in publicly available guidelines. Field baseline applies (introduction longer than conclusion, body sections medium to long).

### Abstract
- **Maximum 250 words**
- Concise and factual
- Avoid non-standard abbreviations; define at first mention if essential
- No structured format mandated (unstructured prose acceptable)

### Keywords
- **Required: 1 to 7 keywords**
- English only
- Prefer single-word keywords; avoid multi-word phrases with "and" or "of"

### Article Highlights
- **Required** at submission
- Short bulleted list (collection of bullet points)
- Capture novel results and new methods
- Purpose: enhance discoverability via search engines

### Graphical Abstract
- **Encouraged** (not required)
- Optional at submission
- Pictorial, professional summary designed for interdisciplinary appeal

### Citations
- **Citation style: elsarticle-num** (numbered, author-year available as alternative)
- Use editable formats (Word or LaTeX)
- Standard author-year or numbered format; follow submission checklist

### Tables and Figures
- Require captions
- Both tables and figures are common; no prohibition on either
- Include in main text or appendices as appropriate

### Explicit Prohibitions
- None stated in public guidelines
- Standard Elsevier ethical requirements apply (originality, no prior publication except as preprint/thesis/abstract)

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Heading Format and Numbering
**Delta: ENFORCED** — Field baseline states "Roman or Arabic numerals; either is acceptable" with no strong preference. Journal practice (confirmed in both accessible articles) consistently uses numbered Arabic numerals (1, 2, 2.1, etc.) in a strict hierarchical format. This is not stated as mandatory in guidelines but is universal in practice.

### Article Highlights Requirement
**Delta: ENFORCED** — Field baseline does not mention article highlights. Journal explicitly requires authors to submit "Article highlights" (short collection of bullet points capturing novel results and methods). This is a journal-specific requirement absent from ABS 4*/4 practice.

### Structural Flexibility (Relaxation)
**Delta: RELAXATION** — Field baseline for ABS 4*/4 journals prescribes near-universal structures: (1) Introduction, Data, Methods, Results, Conclusion for empirical; (2) Introduction, Literature, Model, Analysis, Results, Conclusion for theory. This journal states explicitly "no strict formatting requirements," and guidelines do not mandate section order or naming beyond requiring "clearly defined sections." Authors have more latitude than at top-tier journals. This is a **relaxation of field norm**, not a tightening.

### Graphical Abstract
**Delta: GUIDELINE ONLY** — Field baseline does not mention graphical abstracts. Journal encourages (but does not require) a graphical abstract at submission. Observed in spot-check articles but optional; not a hard constraint.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Forgetting Article Highlights:** Journal explicitly requires bullet-point article highlights at submission. This is absent from ABS 4*/4 practice and easy to overlook. Missing highlights will trigger a revision request.
2. **Exceeding 250-word abstract:** Concise abstracts are expected. Verbose abstracts will require cutting without substantial content loss.
3. **Multi-word keywords:** Guidelines discourage keywords like "housing market" or "land value." Use single words where possible (Housing, Market, Hedonic, etc.).
4. **Submission fee:** Journal charges $100 submission fee ($25 student rate). Failure to pay during submission portal interaction blocks review. Not a writing issue, but a critical logistics step.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

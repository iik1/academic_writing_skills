# Journal Writing Skill: Revista de Historia Economica - Journal of Iberian and Latin American Economic History

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** BUS HIST & ECON HIST
- **Publisher:** Cambridge University Press
- **Scope:** Original research papers on economic history, economic thought, and long-term economic analysis of Iberian and Latin American regions
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/revista-de-historia-economica-journal-of-iberian-and-latin-american-economic-history/information (detailed author instructions embedded in submission system, full guideline PDF inaccessible during research)
- **Field baseline:** business-economic-history-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 7 articles from Volume 42-44 (2024-2026): Poggio "French Merchants"; Cermeño & Santiago-Caballero "Von Thünen"; García Heras "Argentina's Creditworthiness"; Samà "Tuberculosis Mortality"; Lamikiz "Fiscal Transformation"; Sánchez-Picón et al. ## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

> Detailed author guidelines are accessible only through the journal's manuscript submission system (Manuscript Central). The following constraints are inferred from Cambridge University Press standards and observed in published articles.

### Language
Articles must be submitted in **English or Spanish**. Published articles display bilingual abstracts (English and Spanish resumen).

### Abstract
- **Format:** Bilingual (English abstract followed by Spanish "Resumen")
- **Length (inferred):** Approximately 100-150 words per language, though word count not explicitly stated in accessible guidelines
- **Style:** Unstructured; summarizes the historical problem, argument, and contribution

### Citation Style
- **System:** Author-date (Harvard style)
- **Format:** (Author Year, p. page) for parenthetical citations

### Structure (Observed Pattern)
Most articles follow: Introduction (with embedded literature review) → Substantive sections (organized chronologically or thematically) → Conclusions → References. Some quantitative articles add formal Methodology and Results sections. No required section labeling or numbering observed.

### Explicit Constraints (from observation)
- No explicit word limit found in accessible guidelines (articles vary: 20–30 pages typical length)
- Footnotes are common and appear to be permitted
- Tables and figures are expected to have descriptive captions and full source attribution

**Delta: ENFORCED**

All articles examined feature bilingual abstracts (English and Spanish). The journal explicitly publishes in English and Spanish, and every article observed includes both versions. This is a distinguishing feature from the field baseline, which does not address language or multilingual publication. Authors writing for this journal must plan for abstract translation or bilingual composition from the outset.

### Structural Heterogeneity
**Delta: PRACTICE ONLY / High Variation**

While the field baseline describes a flexible structure (introduction with embedded literature review, substantive sections, conclusion), this journal exhibits notably *higher* structural variation than other ABS 4-level journals in the field. Quantitative articles (Cermeño & Santiago-Caballero; Samà) use explicit Methodology and Results sections, resembling applied economics structure more closely than narrative business history. Qualitative articles (Poggio; Lamikiz; García Heras) follow the narrative-analytical mode described in FIELD_SKILL. This variation is not a deviation but rather a feature of the journal's willingness to accommodate both traditions without enforcing uniformity. Authors should match their structure to their evidence type (archival/qualitative vs. econometric/quantitative) rather than imposing a single template.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Forgetting bilingual abstract translation.** Authors accustomed to monolingual publication may submit with only an English abstract. This journal requires both English and Spanish versions. Budget time for translation or composition in both languages from the outset.

2. **Unclear archival source attribution.** While footnotes are permitted, observed articles include detailed source notes and archival citations. Underdocumented sources weaken historical credibility; provide full provenance, including repository, collection, and file/document identifiers.

3. **Inconsistent structure across quantitative vs. qualitative work.** Do not force a single organizational template if your evidence is primarily archival or narrative. The journal accommodates both methodological approaches; match your structure to your data and argument.

4. **Insufficient engagement with Spanish-language historiography.** Given the journal's focus on Iberian and Latin American economic history, overreliance on English-language sources or Anglo-American historiography is a gap. Proportionate engagement with Spanish and Portuguese economic history scholarship strengthens submissions.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

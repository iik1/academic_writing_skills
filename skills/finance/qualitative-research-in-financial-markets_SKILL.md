# Journal Writing Skill: Qualitative Research in Financial Markets

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** FINANCE
- **Publisher:** Emerald Group Publishing Limited
- **Scope:** Publishes qualitative and mixed-methods research exploring contemporary issues in corporate finance and financial market behaviour. Welcomes empirical papers employing interviews, case studies, phenomenological approaches, and systematic literature reviews, as well as methodological debates about qualitative methods in finance.
- **Guidelines URL:** Emerald's general author guidelines (journal-specific guidelines behind publisher walls)
- **Field baseline:** finance-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 5 (PURPOSE abstracts from IDEAS/RePEc and published descriptions; 2024-2025 issues)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

### Abstract Format
**ENFORCED — Structured abstract required.** Emerald standard for this publisher:
- Maximum 250 words total (including keywords and classification)
- Seven labeled sub-sections (not all always used, but framework is mandatory):
  1. **Purpose** — Research aim or question
  2. **Design/Methodology/Approach** — Qualitative method(s) employed (interviews, case study, narrative inquiry, phenomenological approach, systematic review, etc.)
  3. **Findings** — Key results or conclusions
  4. **Research Limitations/Implications** — Scope constraints and methodological boundaries
  5. **Practical Implications** — How results apply to practitioners or policy
  6. **Social Implications** — Impact on society/stakeholders (if applicable)
  7. **Originality/Value** — Contribution to literature and gap addressed

- **Person and tense:** Third person throughout ("This paper investigates…," "The research reveals…"). NOT first-person plural ("we find") as in quantitative finance papers.

### Section Structure
No strict required structure stated, but observed practice across recent articles (2024-2025) follows this pattern:
1. Introduction (with literature context)
2. Methodology (detailed description of qualitative approach, sample/participants, data collection, analysis procedures)
3. Findings (results of qualitative analysis, often thematically organized)
4. Discussion
5. Conclusion
6. References
7. Appendix (if needed)

### Length
- No explicit word limit found for body text. Emerald typically accepts 5,000–8,000 words for journal articles, but this is not confirmed for QRFM specifically.

### Citation Style
Author-year parenthetical format (standard across finance field). Follows FIELD_SKILL.

### Tables and Figures
No deviation from FIELD_SKILL identified. Tables and figures are integrated or appear after references. Qualitative papers often include data displays for thematic matrices, code definitions, or interview participant summaries.

### Explicit Prohibitions
None found beyond standard Emerald publishing ethics (originality, disclosure of funding/conflicts).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice, Person, and Register — MAJOR DELTA
**Delta classification:** ENFORCED (structured abstracts in third person are a stated Emerald requirement; article-body voice observed consistently in spot-check).

The **field baseline expects first-person plural** ("we find," "we show") for multi-authored empirical papers and active, confident voice for quantitative analysis. **QRFM enforces third-person abstract writing** in structured format ("this research," "the study reveals," "the findings indicate"). Article bodies follow a more measured, exploratory tone appropriate to qualitative inquiry—less confident assertion of findings, more tentative/exploratory language reflecting the exploratory nature of qualitative work.

**Confidence calibration differs:** FIELD_SKILL calls for "strong declarative claims for results that are well-identified." Qualitative papers here instead use phrases like "suggests," "appears to indicate," "may provide evidence," reflecting the interpretive nature of qualitative analysis rather than causal identification.

### Hedging and Epistemic Caution — DELTA
**Delta classification:** PRACTICE (observed consistently, not stated in guidelines).

**Higher hedging level than field baseline.** FIELD_SKILL warns against over-hedging; QRFM papers systematically use measured language even for primary findings. Common patterns:
- "The findings suggest that…"
- "Participants' accounts indicate…"
- "The analysis provides evidence that [mechanism X] may operate…"
- "This appears consistent with the view that…"

This reflects the methodological boundaries of qualitative research (small samples, case studies, thick description) and is appropriate rather than a weakness.

### Statistical Reporting — MAJOR DELTA
**Delta classification:** FIELD DEFAULT (N/A for qualitative research).

The field baseline emphasizes "statistical precision," "exact coefficients," "standard errors," and "economic significance." **QRFM papers report qualitative findings through thematic categories, participant quotes, narrative accounts, and systematic codes rather than statistical tables.** Papers include tables displaying participant demographics, interview sample composition, or coding schemes, but NOT regression tables, coefficients, or statistical significance.

### Methodological Transparency — DELTA
**Delta classification:** PRACTICE (observed, not explicitly stated in guidelines).

**Stronger emphasis on methodological documentation than typical in quantitative finance.** Papers include:
- Explicit statement of qualitative approach (narrative inquiry, phenomenological, case study, systematic review, etc.)
- Sample/participant selection strategy and justification (purposive sampling, snowballing, etc.)
- Data collection procedures (number of interviews, duration, recording method)
- Data analysis method (thematic analysis, content analysis, constant comparison, coding framework)
- Researcher reflexivity or positionality statement (common in qualitative methods literature)
- Discussion of trustworthiness/validity (triangulation, member checking, saturation, transferability)

FIELD_SKILL requires high procedural detail, but QRFM extends this further because qualitative studies lack the formal identification assumptions (IV, DD, matching) that justify efficiency in quantitative papers. Qualitative rigor depends on transparent method.

### Argumentative Structure — RELAXATION
**Delta classification:** RELAXATION (appropriate for lower-tier journal and qualitative tradition).

The field baseline prescribes a strict introduction structure: "Establish significance → Identify gap → State question → Preview approach → Report quantitative magnitudes → Enumerate contributions → Review literature → Close with roadmap."

**QRFM papers follow a lighter version.** Introductions are often shorter, may not include quantitative scale-setting (financial magnitudes) because the study is not a large-N empirical test, and contribution enumeration is less formulaic. The gap/puzzle framing remains but is typically embedded narratively rather than formally enumerated. This reflects the different epistemology of qualitative research and is consistent with ABS 1 norms (lower than ABS 4*/4).

### Results and Interpretation Boundary — DELTA
**Delta classification:** PRACTICE (observed consistently).

FIELD_SKILL separates "reporting" (presenting coefficients) from "interpretation" (meaning and mechanisms). **QRFM papers interleave reporting and interpretation more closely.** Because findings are thematic/narrative rather than statistical, the act of describing a finding inherently includes interpretive framing. Example: "Participants repeatedly described a mental accounting strategy where they kept investment portfolios separate by perceived risk tier, which suggests a categorization heuristic" — this single sentence reports and interprets together.

### Citation Density and Literature Engagement — RELAXATION
**Delta classification:** RELAXATION (appropriate for ABS 1).

FIELD_SKILL documents "high" citation density in introductions and methods. QRFM papers are noticeably lighter, with more measured citation pacing. Introductions still ground the question in the literature, but opening paragraphs are less citation-dense, and the "heavy citation from the outset signals command of the literature" norm is softer. Related literature may be embedded in methods or discussion rather than concentrated in introduction. This aligns with ABS 1 expectations and the exploratory nature of qualitative inquiry.

### Discussion and Conclusion — DELTA
**Delta classification:** PRACTICE (observed, not stated).

FIELD_SKILL calls for short (1–4 paragraph) conclusions. **QRFM often has slightly longer conclusions or merged discussion/conclusion sections** to adequately address the contextuality and limitations inherent in qualitative work. Limitations are discussed more thoroughly (reflecting methodological humility about generalizability and sample boundaries) rather than briefly acknowledged.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Using first-person plural ("we") in the abstract.** Abstracts must be third-person. Article bodies may use "we" (single-authored papers use "I" or impersonal), but the structured abstract is a formal, objective summary.

2. **Over-claiming causation or broad generalizability.** Qualitative work is interpretive and context-bound. Avoid statements like "this study proves that [mechanism operates]." Use "suggests," "indicates," "provides evidence for" instead. Reviewers will flag overconfident claims as methodologically inappropriate.

3. **Insufficient methodological detail.** Do not assume readers know what "interviews" means. Specify: semi-structured vs. unstructured, number of interviews, duration, sampling method, analysis procedure (thematic analysis, coding scheme, etc.). Qualitative rigor depends on transparent method.

4. **Treating qualitative findings as statistical outcomes.** Do not present findings in the format of quantitative finance (e.g., "X increases by Y units when Z occurs"). Instead, organize by theme, participant perspective, or narrative logic. For sample demographics or coding summaries, use frequency tables (e.g., "12 of 18 participants reported X behavior"), not regression tables.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

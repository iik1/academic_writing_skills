# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Banking Regulation

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** FINANCE
- **Publisher:** Springer Nature (Palgrave Macmillan imprint)
- **Scope:** Banking regulation, supervision, and financial stability, spanning legal/regulatory analysis, policy commentary, and empirical banking research (capital, risk, governance, CBDC, fintech regulation).
- **Guidelines URL:** https://link.springer.com/journal/41261/submission-guidelines (page requires Springer login for direct fetch; content below extracted via search-engine indexing of the page).
- **Field baseline:** finance-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 9 full abstracts read directly, plus title-level review of ~30 additional articles spanning 2016–2026. DOIs/titles for full abstracts: 10.1057/s41261-022-00193-5 (e-CNY regulation); 10.1057/s41261-022-00207-2 (climate stress testing); 10.1057/s41261-021-00162-4 (ECB and CBDC constitutionality); 10.1057/s41261-022-00192-6 (BOFIA 2020 Nigeria); 10.1057/s41261-020-00127-z (blockchain/NPA India); 10.1057/s41261-020-00141-1 (bank executive recruitment, Israel); 10.1057/s41261-021-00177-x (IFRS 9 cost of funding); 10.1057/s41261-021-00152-6 (Hungary card payments policy); "Volcker rule, ring-fencing" v17i3 (structural reform comparison). Full-text of any article was not accessible (Springer paywall/login-gated); abstracts and keywords were retrieved via EconPapers/RePEc mirrors.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No prescribed section list beyond standard requirement of abstract + keywords; no evidence of a mandated section template distinct from FIELD_SKILL norms.

### Length
No explicit word/page limit found in accessible guideline text.

### Abstract
Short, unstructured abstract required ("outlining the paper's aims and subject matter"). Up to six keywords required. Papers "cannot be typeset without an abstract and keywords" — treated as a strict submission gate. Additionally requires a short (~100-word) author/institution bio accompanying the manuscript — not a FIELD_SKILL requirement.

### Citations
No citation style explicitly specified in accessible guideline text; default to Springer/Palgrave house style (author-date), consistent with FIELD_SKILL.

### Headings
No journal-specific heading convention stated.

### Tables and Figures
Authors are responsible for third-party permissions for any figures, tables, photos, illustrations, trade literature, or data reused from elsewhere; copyrighted material requires permission submitted with the final manuscript. This is a stricter, explicitly stated clearance requirement not detailed in FIELD_SKILL.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Guidelines explicitly instruct authors to avoid "language or slang which is not in keeping with the professional and academic style of the Journal," and require that references to named people/organizations be accurate and "not racist or sexist" and without libellous implications. This is a stated tone/legal-risk constraint beyond FIELD_SKILL's general formality norm.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
RELAXATION / PRACTICE ONLY. The sample shows substantially more impersonal and third-person-author framing than FIELD_SKILL's "we"-dominant norm ("This paper reviews...," "This contribution shows...," "The paper applies an in-depth qualitative study..."). First-person plural ("we find," "our results indicate") does appear in the more empirical/quantitative pieces (e.g., IFRS 9 cost-of-funding paper, Hungary card-payments paper) but is not dominant across the sample the way FIELD_SKILL describes. Many single-author legal/policy contributions avoid first person entirely.

### Statistical Reporting
RELAXATION. A substantial share of sampled abstracts are qualitative, legal, or policy-analytic rather than quantitative-empirical, and do not preview numeric magnitudes in the abstract (e.g., BOFIA 2020 review, ECB/CBDC constitutionality piece, structural-reform comparison, executive-recruitment case study). This is a lower-tier relaxation of FIELD_SKILL's expectation that findings be previewed with specific numbers — not a new norm, since the empirical papers in the sample (IFRS 9, Hungary cards) do report quantitative results and methods (modified CAPM, time-series analysis) in the abstract, consistent with FIELD_SKILL.

### Citation Density
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm in the empirical papers observed; not distinctly assessable from abstracts alone for the legal/policy pieces.

### Structural Deviations
PRACTICE ONLY. The journal publishes a substantial proportion of doctrinal/legal-commentary and policy-analysis articles (e.g., commentary on a Court of Justice judgment, constitutional analysis of ECB CBDC authority, structural-reform legislative comparison) alongside standard empirical finance papers. These legal/policy pieces do not follow the empirical-paper structure (Data, Empirical Analysis, Robustness) assumed as default in FIELD_SKILL; they instead resemble legal-analysis essays organized around statutory/regulatory provisions and case law. Authors writing for this journal should expect the "typical structure" in FIELD_SKILL to apply only to the empirical subset of submissions.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
None beyond the above.

## Common Pitfalls
- Assuming all submissions must follow the empirical-paper template (Data → Results → Robustness); a large share of accepted articles are legal/regulatory commentary or literature reviews with a different structure.
- Omitting the required ~100-word author/institution bio, which FIELD_SKILL does not mention but this journal requires for typesetting.
- Using first-person "we" reflexively for single-authored legal-analysis pieces, where the sample shows impersonal third-person framing is common and accepted.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

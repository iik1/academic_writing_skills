# Journal Writing Skill: Emerald Emerging Markets Case Studies

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN
- **Publisher:** Emerald Publishing
- **Scope:** Publishes discussion-based teaching case studies (with accompanying teaching notes) on business decision-making and management challenges in emerging markets and developing economies. Not a research-article journal — output is pedagogical case content for classroom use.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.emeraldgrouppublishing.com/journal/emcs (author-guideline PDFs at emeraldgrouppublishing.com/sites/default/files/2020-*/guide-writing-cases.pdf and Guide-writing-teaching-note.pdf returned 403 on direct fetch; content reconstructed from search-engine cache snippets and Emerald's general case-writing guidance pages)

(EEMCS-07-2024-0305); "Lemon Tree Hotels: decisions at the intersection of ownership and asset-light strategies" (EEMCS-02-2024-0051); "Double materiality and stakeholder engagement: JALA's sustainability journey" (EEMCS-05-2024-0216); "Surplus Indonesia: tackling food waste through app" (EEMCS-04-2024-0190); "Yuva Parivartan: the employee retention challenge" (EEMCS-07-2024-0306); "Navigating through turbulence: Jet Airways' acquisition failure and recapitalisation strategy" (EEMCS-10-2022-0371)

**Important note on genre mismatch:** FIELD_SKILL (mgmt-field-writing-style) describes conventions for empirical/theoretical research articles (hypotheses, theory sections, discussion of contribution to literature). This journal does not publish that genre at all — it publishes teaching cases (narrative decision-point scenarios for classroom use) plus separate teaching notes for instructors. Most of FIELD_SKILL's sections (Introduction/CARS structure, Theory and Hypotheses, Results, citation density in Discussion) simply do not apply. Where no direct FIELD_SKILL analogue exists, this is noted as "N/A — different genre" rather than a delta.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Two-part submission required: (1) the teaching case narrative itself, and (2) a separate instructor's teaching note. Also required: a title page and a signed consent-to-publish release form. The case narrative is a decision-focused business narrative (protagonist, dilemma, background, decision point) — not a hypothesis-driven empirical paper. The teaching note must include: relevant course/level, theoretical concepts or models applicable, research methodology (data sources — primary/secondary, disguising of information, ethics permissions where applicable), no more than 6 learning objectives (one Bloom's-taxonomy verb each), 3-5 discussion questions explicitly cross-referenced to learning objectives (notated "LO#"), a 90-minute multi-modal teaching plan, and supporting materials (worksheets, videos, reading lists) where relevant.

### Length
Case narrative: 5,000-7,000 words, excluding exhibits, references, and supplementary materials.

### Abstract
Structured abstract required, maximum 250 words including keywords. Visible structured fields include study level (e.g., postgraduate/MBA), subject area, case overview/synopsis, and expected learning outcomes.

### Citations
Emerald's standard house style is Harvard referencing (author-date parenthetical, e.g., "(Petit and Sieffermann, 2007)"), not the APA style stated in FIELD_SKILL.

### Headings
Not verifiable beyond structural sections noted above (title, case narrative, exhibits, references, teaching note sections); no additional heading-style constraints found.

### Tables and Figures
All exhibits are numbered and treated as figures/tables; case format uses 1" margins and single spacing per submission template guidance.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Cases must not have been previously published in the same or substantially similar form, and must not be under consideration elsewhere. No proofs are supplied before publication — the submitted manuscript is treated as final, so authors must ensure it is complete and error-free before submission (no post-acceptance correction window).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
N/A — different genre. Case narratives are written in third-person descriptive/narrative prose about a protagonist and organization, not in the "we argue/we find" authorial voice FIELD_SKILL describes for empirical papers. Teaching notes address the instructor directly (structured, prescriptive) rather than argue a scholarly contribution.

### Statistical Reporting
N/A — different genre. Cases may embed financial/operational exhibits (e.g., financial statements, KPI tables) but do not report regression coefficients, significance tests, or hypothesis-confirmation language as in FIELD_SKILL's Results section norms.

### Citation Density
RELAXATION. Cases are not literature-engagement documents; citations are sparse and largely limited to secondary-source data (news articles, company reports, industry data) rather than the theory-dense citation practice FIELD_SKILL describes for Introduction/Theory sections. Teaching notes reference underlying theoretical models/frameworks by name but do not build a citation-supported argument.

### Structural Deviations
ENFORCED. No Introduction/Theory/Hypotheses/Results/Discussion architecture at all. Instead: structured abstract (study level, subject area, case overview, learning outcomes) → case narrative (decision-point business story) → exhibits → references → separate teaching note (learning objectives with Bloom's verbs, discussion questions keyed to LOs, teaching plan, methodology/data-source disclosure, supporting materials). This entire structure is specific to the teaching-case genre and has no FIELD_SKILL counterpart.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
ENFORCED. Explicit emphasis on emerging-market/developing-economy setting as the defining editorial criterion — cases are selected in part for geographic/institutional context (protagonists and firms based in or closely tied to emerging markets), which is a acquisition criterion FIELD_SKILL does not address at all.

## Common Pitfalls
- Writing the submission as a research article (hypotheses, literature review, empirical results) instead of a decision-focused teaching narrative plus separate teaching note — this journal does not publish empirical/theoretical management research.
- Exceeding the 250-word structured abstract limit or omitting required structured fields (study level, subject area, case overview, learning outcomes).
- Submitting a teaching note with more than 6 learning objectives, multiple verbs per objective, or discussion questions not explicitly cross-referenced to learning objective numbers.
- Treating the submission as correctable after acceptance — Emerald does not issue proofs for EMCS cases, so errors in the final submitted version become permanent.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

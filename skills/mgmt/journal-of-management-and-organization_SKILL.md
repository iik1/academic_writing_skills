# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Management and Organization

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN
- **Publisher:** Cambridge University Press
- **Scope:** International, peer-reviewed journal publishing empirical and conceptual research across management discipline. Publishes quantitative, qualitative, mixed-methods, and theoretical work. Strong representation of organizational behavior, ethics, leadership, and entrepreneurship topics.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-management-and-organization/information/author-instructions
- **Field baseline:** mgmt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 (2025-2026 publications; quantitative, mixed-methods, qualitative, and conceptual articles)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Standard: Abstract → Introduction → [Content sections] → Discussion → Conclusion. For quantitative empirical work: Introduction → Theory/Literature → Methodology → Results → Discussion → Conclusion. For qualitative/mixed-methods: Introduction → Literature Review → Methodology → Results/Findings → Discussion → Conclusion. Conceptual/review pieces organize by argument theme rather than methodology.

### Length
No explicit word limit found in accessible guidelines. Observed article lengths: empirical quantitative (8,000–12,000 words), mixed-methods (10,000–15,000 words), theoretical/conceptual (3,000–6,000 words for editorials; longer for full conceptual pieces).

### Abstract
- Unstructured (single paragraph)
- Approximately 145–180 words (observed range)
- No citations in abstract
- States research purpose/question, methodology/approach, key findings, and theoretical/practical implications

### Citations
- Author-date system: (Author, Year) or Author (Year)
- Multiple citations ordered alphabetically or by sequence
- Et al. for three or more authors from first citation
- Reference list uses full author names, year, article/book title in italics, and publication details
- Citation density moderate in Introduction and Theory sections; lighter in Results; moderate-heavy in Discussion

### Headings
Follows hierarchical structure: primary headings (e.g., "Introduction"), secondary headings (e.g., "Remote work and leadership effectiveness"), tertiary headings where needed. Substantive naming standard for theory/conceptual sections (e.g., "Transformational entrepreneurship" rather than "Theory 1").

### Tables and Figures
Tables: Standard social science format with descriptive headers, means/SDs for quantitative data, correlations/fit indices clearly labeled. Figures: Used for visual models (path diagrams, process models, typologies, hierarchical frameworks). Captions below figures; table titles above. Both embedded in-text where appropriate.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Double-blind review requires separate title page (uploaded independently of main manuscript) with all author names, affiliations, and contact details
- No author identifying information in main manuscript body during review
- Competing interest declarations required for each author on title page
- Word count required on title page
- Submissions through ScholarOne Manuscripts platform only; no other submission method accepted

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Abstract Format
JMO abstracts are slightly shorter and less structured than typical MGMT journal norms. FIELD_SKILL observes 150–250 words; JMO observed range is 145–180 words, more consistently at the lower end. No structured subsections (Purpose/Methods/Results/Conclusions) observed; always unstructured narrative paragraph.

### Citation Density
Discussion sections show heavier citation engagement than FIELD_SKILL baseline suggests is typical. Authors frequently cite 4–6 sources per substantial paragraph in Discussion, weaving them throughout interpretation rather than clustering at topic transitions. This indicates more intensive literature synthesis alongside empirical interpretation.

### Conceptual/Theoretical Articles
JMO publishes shorter, more focused conceptual pieces than the typical MGMT outlet. Editorial and perspective articles are 3,000–6,000 words (significantly shorter than the 8,000–14,000-word range in FIELD_SKILL). These use fewer propositions (often 3–5 rather than P1–P7+), rely on direct quotations from foundational sources for definitional clarity, and emphasize forward-looking agenda setting over comprehensive framework development.

### Voice in Methodology
Greater use of passive voice and third-person reference in Methods sections than FIELD_SKILL baseline ("Expert participants...," "The analysis proceeded...") compared to "We selected...," "We conducted...". Reflects Cambridge house style for technical procedural clarity.

### Results/Findings Presentation in Mixed-Methods
Mixed-methods articles integrate quantitative and qualitative results more fluidly than the FIELD_SKILL pattern suggests. Rather than Results (quant) followed by Findings (qual), JMO articles often weave them: "Survey data showed X (n=150, p<.05); case study evidence illustrated this through [quote/vignette]." This reflects a more integrated synthesis approach.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Submitting via wrong channel or format:** JMO uses ScholarOne Manuscripts exclusively (mc.manuscriptcentral.com/jmo). Many authors accidentally submit via email or alternative platforms and face rejection without review. Verify the platform before submission.

2. **Failure to separate and anonymize title page:** Double-blind review requires uploading title page (with author names, affiliations, competing interests, word count) as a separate document from the main manuscript. Authors who include identifying information in the main text (e.g., "In our earlier work, Smith (2020)...") or omit competing interest declarations delay review and may trigger desk rejection.

3. **Exceeding implicit length norms without justification:** While no explicit page limit is stated, articles exceeding 15,000 words for empirical work or 8,000 words for conceptual pieces face scrutiny. Editors flag length; authors should justify extended scope in cover letter or manage content ruthlessly.

4. **Underdeveloping theoretical contribution:** As an ABS 2 journal, JMO expects clear theoretical advancement. Purely descriptive or confirmatory studies (e.g., testing well-known effects in a new context without new mechanisms or boundary conditions) are desk-rejected. Ensure your contribution extends theory or challenges existing frameworks.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Ekonomicheskaya Politika / Economic Policy

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Russian Presidential Academy of National Economy and Public Administration (RANEPA), founded by the Gaidar Institute for Economic Policy
- **Scope:** Broad-range bimonthly (six issues/year) Russian-language economics journal covering economic policy of contemporary Russia and global economic problems, spanning macro/monetary policy, fiscal federalism, regional economics, competition policy, and economic history. Published bilingually: Russian original with parallel English metadata/abstracts.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.ecpolicy.ru/jour/about/submissions?locale=en_US
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 2 full English abstracts read verbatim (Sizova 2025, DOI 10.18288/1994-5124-2025-4-142-169; Levashenko & Sinelnikov-Murylev, DOI visible via article ID 524), plus title/topic/JEL-code index of ~25 additional articles (2022-2026 issues) reviewed at abstract/metadata level via RePEc and journal archive. Full text is in Russian and not accessible to this review; English is limited to title, abstract, keywords, JEL codes, and author metadata for non-native readers. This is a good-faith sample appropriate for an ABS 1, non-English-primary journal.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Required components: author questionnaire/affiliation block (Russian and English), article title (max 8 words), abstract, keywords, JEL codes, article text with appendices, references, and a parallel English-language block (translated title, English abstract, transliterated references). No specific section-heading hierarchy is mandated beyond this front-matter; body structure otherwise follows standard economics practice per FIELD_SKILL.

### Length
Article body: 20,000-60,000 characters including spaces (roughly 10-25 manuscript pages) — a WIDE range with a much shorter floor than typical ABS 4/4* empirical papers. Formatting: 1.5 line spacing, Times New Roman 14pt.

### Abstract
English abstract: 100-250 words (some editorial guidance states "about 200-250 words"), original composition rather than a literal translation of the Russian version, and must explicitly cover: subject/theme/purpose, methodology, results, application area, and conclusions. Keywords: 3-10 words (guidance varies 3-6 vs. "no fewer than 3, no more than 10" across sources); JEL codes required.

### Citations
In-text citations use square brackets, not parenthetical author-year running prose: `[Author, Year]` or `[Author, Year. P. #]`. Reference list follows GOST 7.0.5-2008 (Russian bibliographic standard), not APA/Chicago. English-language reference list entries require Library-of-Congress-method transliteration (via translit.ru convention) with titles bracketed. This is a hard formatting departure from the parenthetical author-year in-text norm FIELD_SKILL describes as near-universal.

### Headings
Headers centered with spacing above/below; no enforced numbering scheme specified.

### Tables and Figures
Tables must be built with native Word table functionality (not tab/space-aligned text) and placed inline at point of relevance, not collected at document end. Figures must be submitted as editable source files (Excel/PowerPoint with underlying data, CorelDraw .cdr, or Adobe Illustrator .ai/.eps) — rasterized formats (.jpeg, .gif, .tif) are explicitly rejected.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- No underlining for emphasis (use italics instead), except for hyperlinks.
- No rasterized image formats for figures.
- Reference lists must exclude government regulations, statistics releases, official documents, and archival materials (these should be cited as footnotes/in-text, not in the bibliography).
- Author names/identifying information must be stripped from the manuscript file itself (double-blind review preparation).
- A plagiarism screening report (AntiPlagiat.ru) is required at submission.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
Mandatory bilingual front matter (Russian submission + English metadata block: translated title, English abstract, transliterated references) is a journal-specific requirement not found in FIELD_SKILL, which assumes a monolingual English-only publication pipeline. ENFORCED — required by guidelines and present in every article's metadata observed.

### Citation Density
The two full abstracts read (Sizova; Levashenko & Sinelnikov-Murylev) do not allow a direct check of in-body citation density, but the bracketed `[Author, Year]` in-text format itself (vs. FIELD_SKILL's parenthetical author-year prose) is ENFORCED at the formatting level per GOST 7.0.5-2008 — a RELAXATION/divergence in mechanical citation style, not a change in when or how densely sources are engaged.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Abstracts observed follow a compressed "theoretical section then empirical section" narrative arc within a single abstract paragraph, explicitly signposting internal article structure ("the theoretical section argues... the second section provides an empirical analysis...") — more explicit internal roadmapping within the abstract itself than FIELD_SKILL's baseline abstract norm assumes for ABS 4/4* journals, where abstracts are usually leaner and results-forward without narrating section order. PRACTICE ONLY (observed in the 2 full abstracts read; not stated in guidelines beyond the mandated subject/methodology/results/conclusions checklist).

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting APA/Chicago-style parenthetical citations and an alphabetical reference list instead of GOST 7.0.5-2008 formatting with bracketed in-text markers.
- Submitting figures as embedded raster images (screenshots, .png/.jpeg exports) rather than editable native source files.
- Writing an English abstract that is a literal machine/human translation of the Russian abstract rather than an independently composed English version — explicitly discouraged by the guidelines.
- Omitting the required English-language metadata block (transliterated references, translated title) that accompanies the Russian submission.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Asia Pacific Journal of Human Resources

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** HRM&EMP
- **Publisher:** Wiley-Blackwell
- **Scope:** Applied research on human resource management, employment relations, and workforce development in the Asia-Pacific region. Publishes original empirical research, reviews, and evidence-based practice guidance for HR professionals and scholars.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/page/journal/17447941/homepage/forauthors.html
- **Field baseline:** hrm-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 5+ recent articles from Volume 63 (2025) and Volume 62 (2024)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Original Articles must include: abstract, introduction, methods, results, and conclusions as required sections. Review Articles follow a similar structure with emphasis on literature synthesis and research gap identification. All manuscripts require a separate title page with author anonymization for double-blind review. Supporting materials and data files should be submitted separately.

### Length
Original Articles: maximum 8,500 words total, including abstract, references, tables, figures, and footnotes. This is a strict constraint and considerably tighter than the FIELD_SKILL baseline expectation. Review Articles may exceed this limit if justified; clarify word count for the specific article type at submission.

### Abstract
Unstructured abstract format (single paragraph), maximum 150 words. No subdivisions (Background/Methods/Results/Conclusion) required or expected. The abstract should communicate the practical and theoretical significance of the work.

### Key Points for Practitioners
Required element unique to APJHR: authors must provide 3–5 "key points" highlighting main findings and implications for HR practitioners. Each key point must be a single complete sentence (no bullet fragments), maximum 15 words per point. These are written at practitioner level and will be published in the article's front matter; they serve as a summary aid for busy practitioners who skim the journal.

### Citations
Author-date citation system (parenthetical references: Author Year). In-text citations follow standard conventions (Smith 2020) or (Smith and Jones 2020) for two authors; for three or more authors, use first author et al. unless the journal specifies otherwise. Full bibliographic format follows author-date conventions with authors' initials, italicized titles/container names, and publication year.

References are not subject to a fixed style requirement under the journal's "Free Format" submission policy; authors may use any consistent author-date variant (Chicago, Harvard, APA author-date), as long as formatting is uniform throughout. However, author-date is the standard.

### Reference Limit
80 references maximum for original articles. Review Articles may exceed this limit if necessary; confirm with editors before submission of unusually long reference lists.

### Headings
Hierarchical numbered or lettered heading system is common in published articles. Use numbered sections (1, 1.1, 1.2, etc.) or letter-based subsections (A, B, C) for clarity. Headings should be descriptive and follow standard IMRaD structure: Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion, Conclusions. Do not use decorative or overly casual heading language.

### Tables and Figures
Tables and figures must include legends/captions. Figures should be submitted in the highest resolution possible. Tables should be editable and formatted as separate files if submission system permits, or embedded with clear table formatting. Limit tables and figures to essential data; explanatory tables should contain specific values or novel comparisons, not rehashed prose content.

### Submission Process
Manuscripts are submitted via Research Exchange (Wiley's submission platform) after author anonymization and removal of any identifying information from the main text (author websites, social media links, identifying acknowledgments must be omitted). A separate title page containing author names, affiliations, and author-contribution statements is submitted separately. Double-blind review process: authors do not know reviewer identities, and reviewers are not told author identities.

### Ethical Requirements
All authors must disclose any conflicts of interest (financial ties, board memberships, consultancies, stock/patent ownership). If no conflict exists, this must be explicitly stated. Ethical approval statements are required for primary data collection from human participants (interviews, surveys, etc.). Data sharing is encouraged; authors are asked to archive supporting data and materials in a public repository where possible and to provide a data availability statement.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- No author identifiers in the main manuscript during review (anonymized submission required).
- Do not exceed the 8,500-word limit for Original Articles without prior editorial approval.
- Supplementary materials must not contain author names or affiliations.
- References should not exceed 80 unless the article is a Review and editors agree.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Article Length Constraint (Significant Tightening)
**RELAXATION/STRUCTURAL DELTA:** The FIELD_SKILL baseline does not specify word limits, leaving this to individual journal variation. APJHR enforces a strict 8,500-word ceiling, which is notably tighter than typical ABS 4/4* journals that often allow 10,000–12,000 words. This requires more concise presentation of methods, results, and discussion than the FIELD_SKILL baseline might suggest; authors must prioritize substance and cut ornamental prose.

### Practitioner-Facing Key Points (Journal-Specific Innovation)
**ENFORCED/PRACTICE ONLY:** The 3–5 key-points requirement (max 15 words each, practitioner-facing) is unique to APJHR and not mentioned in the FIELD_SKILL baseline. This reflects the journal's applied orientation and signals that theoretical contribution alone is insufficient; authors must explicitly translate findings into HR practitioner implications. This is more explicit and formalized than the FIELD_SKILL norm of mentioning practical implications in the discussion.

### Structured vs. Unstructured Abstract
**GUIDELINE ONLY (Enforced at APJHR):** FIELD_SKILL does not specify abstract structure. APJHR requires unstructured (single-paragraph) abstracts, whereas some ABS 4* journals in HRM prefer or allow structured abstracts (Background/Methods/Results/Conclusion). The unstructured norm here is slightly more permissive and narrative-friendly, allowing for greater emphasis on context-setting or conceptual framing than structured formats do.

### Citation Format Flexibility
**GUIDELINE ONLY/RELAXATION:** The journal's "Free Format" submission policy permits references in any author-date variant, so long as formatting is consistent. This is a relaxation compared to journals with strict APA or Harvard enforcement. However, the author-date system itself (as opposed to numbered citations) is enforced.

### Anonymization for Double-Blind Review
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (double-blind review is standard in HRM top-tier journals); no delta.

### Voice, Person, Register
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. No detectable delta in the level of formality, use of first-person plural, or narrative license observed in published articles.

### Methods and Ethical Reporting
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Ethical approval statements and conflict-of-interest disclosures are standard across the field; APJHR enforces these in line with field practice.

### Discussion and Implications
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm, with the addition of the formal "key points" requirement (see above) which heightens emphasis on practitioner-facing implications without changing the discussion section itself.

## Dimensions With No Delta

- Voice and person: FIELD DEFAULT (formal, impersonal register with first-person plural permitted)
- Sentence and paragraph style: FIELD DEFAULT (medium to long sentences with subordination; paragraphs structured around themes)
- Argumentation and CARS structure: FIELD DEFAULT (establish significance, identify gap, state contribution)
- Results reporting: FIELD DEFAULT (tables for quantitative data; thematic organization for qualitative; minimal interpretation in results section)
- Citation density: FIELD DEFAULT (highest in intro/lit review, lower in results, rising in discussion)
- Hedging intensity: FIELD DEFAULT (moderate to high throughout, highest in discussion/conclusion)
- Qualitative data handling: FIELD DEFAULT (thematic findings with illustrative quotations)
- Quantitative reporting: FIELD DEFAULT (narrate key findings without re-deriving full statistics; coefficient values in tables, prose interpretation separate)

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Exceeding the word limit.** The 8,500-word constraint is strict and tighter than competing journals. Expect to cut 10–15% if your first draft is in the 9,500–10,000 range. Prioritize methods, results, and discussion; front-load key contributions and trim peripheral literature discussion.

2. **Omitting or poorly crafting the "key points" section.** Reviewers and editors scrutinize whether the 3–5 points authentically distill practitioner-relevant insights or merely restate abstract results. Each point must be complete, clear, and actionable from a practitioner's perspective; avoid jargon or overly academic phrasing in this section.

3. **Structured (Background/Methods/Results/Conclusion) abstracts instead of unstructured.** The journal explicitly requires unstructured abstracts. Submissions with labeled sections in the abstract are flagged by editors as not following guidelines and may be desk-rejected or returned for revision before review.

4. **Inadequate anonymization.** Leaving identifying citations, author websites, acknowledgments to named colleagues, or other identifying info in the main manuscript violates the double-blind process. Ensure a clean anonymized main text and submit author details only on the separate title page.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

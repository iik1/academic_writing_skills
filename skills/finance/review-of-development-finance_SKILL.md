# Journal Writing Skill: Review of Development Finance

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** FINANCE
- **Publisher:** Africagrowth Institute / Chartered Institute of Development Finance (CIDEF); formerly co-published with Elsevier (2011–~2018) before returning to CIDEF/Sabinet hosting.
- **Scope:** Theoretical and empirical micro and macro analysis of financial systems, with emphasis on applied, policy-oriented research on the deepening, outreach, efficiency, and stability of financial systems in developing and emerging markets.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.globalcidef.com/manuscript-criteria/ (current, accessible); ScienceDirect guide-for-authors page (legacy Elsevier era) returned 403/inaccessible.
- **Field baseline:** finance-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 1 full abstract read in full (Panizza, "Long-Term Debt Sustainability in Emerging Market Economies: A Counterfactual Analysis," Vol. 15(1), 2025) plus title/author/issue metadata for ~14 additional articles across 2018–2026 (e.g., Duan & Cheok on bank housing-loan risk; Badran et al. on techno-financial synergy; Lizarzaburu et al. on large shareholders and corporate risk; Imeri et al. on BRICS bond yields). Abstracts for the latter were not retrievable through public search/RePEc/journals.co.za (403 or no-abstract index pages) — see Sources and access-limitation note.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Ordered as: Title & Abstract (separate page) → Main Text → References → Appendix. Submitted via Editorial Manager (www.editorialmanager.com/rdf). A cover letter explaining fit for the journal is required at submission.

### Length
Maximum 30 pages, 1.5 line spacing throughout (including tables, references, and footnotes). This is a firm page cap, not a word-count target — considerably tighter than the unstated, effectively-open length norm typical of ABS 4*/4 finance journals.

### Abstract
Maximum 100 words, on its own separate page, with the main text beginning on a new page after it. This is well below the 150–250 word range in FIELD_SKILL.

### Citations
Harvard (author-date) method, consistent with FIELD_SKILL. Deviation: in-text citations omit page numbers for references to a work as a whole; page numbers are included only when quoting directly, formatted "(Author, Year: page)". References are listed alphabetically on a new page in unnumbered format.

### Headings
Explicit three-tier numbered system: "1. MAIN HEADING" (bold, numbered), "1.1 Sub Heading" (bold), "1.1.1 Minor Heading" (italics). More prescriptive than FIELD_SKILL, which only notes Arabic-numeral section numbering without specifying case/weight/style per tier.

### Tables and Figures
Positioned at first reference or as near to it as possible. Numbered sequentially with Arabic numerals. Table number and heading appear ABOVE the table, bold and left-aligned. Figure number and heading appear BELOW the figure, bold and left-aligned. Tables/figures should be self-explanatory, with descriptive footnotes where needed.

### Explicit Prohibitions
No submission fee. Desk rejection applies to manuscripts judged preliminary, incremental, or weakly justified without proceeding to external review. A $300 editing/formatting fee is charged before publication (waived if the paper is rejected) — an administrative/financial requirement rather than a style rule, but worth noting for authors.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
The mandated separate title/abstract page followed by main text starting on a new page is a stricter formatting requirement than FIELD_SKILL describes; treat as ENFORCED given it is stated in the current manuscript-criteria page (spot-check sample too thin on full-text access to independently confirm compliance — GUIDELINE ONLY / presumed enforced at submission stage).

### Abstract Length
100-word cap is a clear RELAXATION/tightening relative to FIELD_SKILL's 150–250 word norm — this is a hard constraint distinctive to this journal, not observed elsewhere in the field baseline. ENFORCED (stated in guidelines; consistent with the journal's shorter, applied-paper format).

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Insufficient full-text access to confirm or refute FIELD_SKILL norms on voice, hedging, citation density, or statistical reporting at this journal specifically. No deltas observed in the material actually reviewed (title patterns and one full abstract read like standard applied empirical finance/development-economics writing, consistent with FIELD_SKILL). Given the ABS 2 tier, some relaxation of the enumerated-contributions/explicit-magnitude norms in FIELD_SKILL's introduction structure is plausible but unconfirmed — do not state as a journal norm.

## Common Pitfalls
- Writing a FIELD_SKILL-length abstract (150–250 words) will exceed this journal's strict 100-word cap — cut aggressively.
- Placing table/figure captions in the wrong position (captions below tables or above figures) violates the journal's explicit above/below convention.
- Including page numbers in every in-text citation (rather than only for direct quotations) does not match the journal's Harvard-style convention.
- Exceeding the 30-page cap, or fitting within it only by using single spacing rather than the required 1.5 spacing, risks administrative rejection independent of content quality.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Pension Economics and Finance

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** FINANCE
- **Publisher:** Cambridge University Press
- **Scope:** The only academic journal focused specifically on the economics and finance of pensions and retirement income — pension fund management, regulation, valuation of liabilities, corporate governance, political economy of pensions, and financial planning for retirement. Papers from actuarial science, mathematics, or statistics are accepted only if framed with a clear economics or finance context. Co-sponsored by IOPS and the OECD.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-pension-economics-and-finance/information/instructions-contributors and https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-pension-economics-and-finance/information/author-instructions/submitting-your-materials (the underlying "Style Guide for Contributors" PDF was not directly retrievable in this environment)
- **Field baseline:** finance-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 (front-matter/abstract-level access only; full text is paywalled). Titles: "The importance of pension and financial knowledge for pension plan participation in Italy" (2025); "Toward an economic reformulation of public pension funding"; "The role of the peer effect in forming pension expectations among the middle-aged: existence and mechanisms" (2024); "Coping with demographic change: macroeconomic performance and welfare inequality effects of public pension reform" (2023); "Introduction to the 20th Anniversary Special Issue" (2025); plus guideline-page cross-checks. This is a smaller good-faith sample appropriate to an ABS 2 journal with thin public full-text access.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No mandated section template beyond standard scholarly structure; Introduction and Conclusion are always present. Author instructions do not prescribe section names.

### Length
Manuscripts "ordinarily should not exceed 35 pages in length." (One secondary source referenced a 55-page ceiling elsewhere on the Cambridge author-instructions site; the 35-page figure comes from the primary "Submitting your materials" page and is treated as authoritative.) Double-spaced text, tables, and figures; 8.5x11" or A4 page size.

### Abstract
Unstructured, **100 words or less** — noticeably shorter than the FIELD_SKILL 150–250 word norm. Abstract, author name/affiliation/email, and up to **6 keywords** must appear on the manuscript's first page.

### Citations
No journal-specific deviation confirmed beyond standard CUP conventions; author-year in-text citation (consistent with articles observed) with JEL codes displayed alongside keywords.

### Headings
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (Arabic numeral sectioning observed in spot-checked articles).

### Tables and Figures
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm; guidelines require figures/tables double-spaced in the submitted Word/Excel files, no other journal-specific rule identified.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Standard CUP publishing-ethics policy: no simultaneous submission, plagiarism strictly prohibited, ORCID mandatory for corresponding authors, written permission required for reuse of copyrighted material.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
Unlike the FIELD_SKILL norm that "a standalone Literature Review section is essentially never used," multiple spot-checked JPEF articles use an explicit, separately numbered **"Literature review"** section (e.g., "3. Literature review: where does our paper fit?"; "2. Literature review" in the Italy pension-literacy paper) rather than folding related work into the introduction. This appears in 2 of 6 sampled articles and is common enough to note as a genuine journal-level pattern (PRACTICE ONLY — not stated in guidelines, but recurring).

Institutional/country-context sections (e.g., "The institutional background of the Italian pension system") appear more frequently than the FIELD_SKILL "common but not universal" framing suggests, reflecting the journal's cross-country pension-systems scope — consistent with FIELD_SKILL's own allowance for this, so treated as FIELD DEFAULT rather than a delta.

### Abstract Length
100-word cap is a RELAXATION/tightening relative to FIELD_SKILL's 150–250 word norm — a hard constraint, not merely observed practice (ENFORCED: stated in guidelines and consistent with the short abstracts seen on article pages).

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
No other confirmed deltas. Voice/register, hedging, citation density, and results-reporting conventions in the sampled abstracts and section lists are consistent with FIELD_SKILL; full-text paywalling limited verification of paragraph-level prose norms.

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting an abstract in the 150–250 word FIELD_SKILL range instead of JPEF's 100-word cap.
- Omitting a distinct Literature Review section when the paper's positioning would benefit from one — this journal accepts and often features it as a standalone section, unlike top-tier finance journals.
- Missing ORCID registration for the corresponding author at submission (mandatory for this journal).

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

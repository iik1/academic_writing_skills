# Field Writing Style: HRM&EMP

## Scope and Intended Use
This baseline describes writing norms shared across the top tier of the
Human Resource Management and Employment Relations (HRM&EMP) field, as
evidenced in ABS 4* and ABS 4 journals. It applies to empirical
(quantitative and qualitative), conceptual, and review-type articles
published in this field. Journal-specific skills should be read as
amendments to this baseline: they record only points of departure
(e.g., a journal that favours interpretivist/sociological framing over
psychological/managerial framing, or one with distinct methods
expectations). Where a journal-specific skill is silent on a dimension,
assume this baseline applies.

## Typical Article Structure
At the ABS 4*/4 level, empirical articles follow a recognisable IMRaD-with-
theory macro-structure: title and abstract, keywords, introduction,
literature review and theoretical framing (sometimes merged with
hypothesis development in quantitative papers), methods, results/findings,
discussion, and conclusion (often including theoretical contributions,
practical/policy implications, limitations, and future research
directions). A separate literature review section is near-universal in
quantitative papers, where it typically culminates in explicit hypotheses;
in qualitative and sociologically oriented papers the equivalent section
is framed as conceptual or theoretical grounding rather than a hypothesis
list, and findings are typically organised thematically rather than by
hypothesis. Acknowledgements, ethics statements, and author contribution
statements are treated as standard back matter. Practice varies on
whether the literature review is a standalone section or interwoven with
the introduction: both are accepted, and reviewers do not treat this as a
quality signal either way.

## Voice, Person, and Register
Leading journals in this field expect a formal, impersonal academic
register overall, but with the first-person plural ("we argue," "our
data show") permitted and increasingly common, especially in the
introduction, methods, and discussion sections to state authorial
choices and interpretive moves. Passive constructions remain common in
methods reporting and in summarising prior literature. Register is
consistently formal: colloquialisms are avoided, but the field tolerates
more narrative and interpretive prose than does economics or finance,
particularly in qualitative and sociological submissions. Direct
quotation from research participants is a normal and expected feature of
qualitative papers' results sections, presented as evidence rather than
ornamentation.

## Sentence and Paragraph Style
Sentences are typically medium to long, with frequent subordination to
accommodate theoretical qualification and citation support. Paragraphs
in the literature review and discussion tend to be structured around a
single claim or theme, opened with a topic sentence and closed with a
link back to the paper's contribution or argument. Signposting language
("in what follows," "we now turn to," "having established X, we next
consider Y") is common at section transitions, more so than in
economics-style writing, reflecting this field's closer proximity to
management and organisational sociology conventions.

## Argumentation and Rhetorical Moves
Introductions at this level consistently follow a CARS-like structure:
establishing the practical and/or theoretical importance of a workplace
or employment phenomenon, identifying a gap in existing understanding
(empirical, theoretical, or contextual), and stating how the paper
addresses that gap. Contributions are typically framed in relation to
theory (extending, integrating, or challenging an existing theoretical
lens such as AMO theory, social exchange theory, job demands-resources,
or institutionalist/labour process perspectives) as well as in relation
to practice or policy relevance for HR practitioners, managers, unions,
or policymakers. Explicit statements of contribution ("this study
contributes to X literature by...") are the norm, usually appearing at
the end of the introduction and revisited in the discussion. Gaps are
often justified by reference to under-researched contexts (sectors,
occupations, countries, worker groups) or under-theorised mechanisms
rather than purely by citation counts.

## Methods Conventions
Methods sections are expected to be detailed enough to support
replication or, in qualitative work, to support judgements of
trustworthiness and rigour. Quantitative papers report sample
construction, measures (with reliability statistics), and estimation
strategy in a standardised sequence, typically in past tense throughout.
Robustness checks and discussion of endogeneity, common method bias, or
alternative explanations are expected in quantitative work, though the
degree of econometric sophistication demanded is lower and less uniform
than in economics-field journals: practice varies from straightforward
regression-based designs to more advanced panel or multilevel modelling,
and no single estimation approach is treated as obligatory. Qualitative
papers are expected to report sampling and access strategy, data
collection method (interviews, ethnography, documents), and an explicit
account of the analytic approach (e.g., thematic analysis, grounded
theory, template analysis), along with reflexivity statements that are
common but not universal. Ethical approval statements are a near-
universal requirement for primary data collection.

## Results and Reporting Norms
In quantitative papers, results are reported with reference to tables
and are then narrated in prose that restates the direction and
significance of key effects without re-deriving statistics in full;
precise coefficient values belong in tables, while prose emphasises
substantive interpretation. In qualitative papers, results/findings
sections are consistently organised thematically, combining short
illustrative quotations with interpretive commentary; the balance leans
toward interpretation supported by evidence rather than long uninterrupted
quotation blocks. Interpretation of findings is generally deferred to a
separate discussion section rather than folded into the results section,
though some journals tolerate limited interpretive commentary within
results, particularly in qualitative work. Null or unexpected findings
are reported straightforwardly and are not omitted or minimised.

## Discussion and Conclusion Norms
Discussion sections at this level are expected to revisit the stated
contributions explicitly, distinguishing theoretical contributions from
practical or policy implications, and typically close with limitations
and directions for future research as a distinct subsection or clearly
signalled paragraph. Practical implications are treated as a required
component of the discussion in this field, reflecting its applied
orientation, even in more theoretically oriented papers. Conclusions are
generally concise and avoid introducing new evidence or citations, though
practice varies on whether the discussion and conclusion are combined
into a single section or kept separate.

## Citation and Literature Engagement
Citation density is highest in the introduction and literature review
sections and lowest in the results section, rising again in the
discussion as findings are related back to prior work. Paraphrase is
strongly preferred over direct quotation when engaging prior scholarship;
direct quotation of other scholars' text is rare and reserved for
precise definitional or conceptual points. Disagreement with prior
literature is framed diplomatically and constructively — as extension,
qualification, or boundary-condition specification rather than direct
refutation — consistent with a field that treats theory-building as
cumulative. Citing seminal, frequently-used theoretical frameworks
(e.g., AMO, social exchange theory, psychological contract, job
demands-resources) alongside recent empirical work in the same sentence
or paragraph is a common and expected practice.

## Hedging and Epistemic Caution
Hedging intensity is moderate to high throughout, and highest in the
discussion and conclusion sections when generalising from findings.
Causal language is used cautiously in observational quantitative designs,
typically qualified with terms such as "associated with," "linked to," or
"suggests," reserving stronger causal claims for designs that can support
them. Qualitative papers hedge generalisability explicitly, typically
including an explicit statement about the contextual or sample-based
limits of transferability. Overclaiming practical impact beyond what the
data support is treated unfavourably by reviewers at this level.

## What This Baseline Does NOT Specify
This baseline does not specify word or page limits, reference list
formatting or citation style (e.g., APA vs Harvard variants), abstract
word counts, number of keywords, submission or peer-review procedures,
required theoretical frameworks, or journal-specific preferences for
quantitative versus qualitative methods. Practice varies across journals
on the degree of emphasis given to psychological/managerial framing
versus sociological/institutional framing, and on how much space is
devoted to policy implications versus organisational/managerial
implications; no consensus norm exists on this balance, and
journal-specific skills should specify where a given outlet leans.
Journal-specific skills should also specify any distinctive structural
requirements (e.g., a required "implications for practice" subsection)
and any house style deviations from the norms above.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

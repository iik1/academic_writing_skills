# Journal Writing Skill: Environment and Development Economics

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Cambridge University Press
- **Scope:** Environmental, resource, and development economics; policy issues and methodological contributions across developing and developed economies
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/environment-and-development-economics/information/author-instructions (PDF instructions available for download; public webpage does not expose full details)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 (all open-access 2026 articles from Cambridge Core)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict. Full details available in downloadable PDF from journal website; the following are inferred from submission page and observed practice.

### Structure
Standard empirical paper structure: Introduction → Literature Review (may be integrated) → Methodology/Data → Results → Discussion/Conclusion. Optional sections include Theoretical Framework (some papers include, others omit). Appendix/Supplementary Material is standard.

### Article Types Accepted
- Research Articles
- Perspectives (may not present new evidence; require "substantiated argument, based on evidence")
- Review Articles
- Summaries
- Book Reviews

### Length
Not specified in accessible guidelines. Inferred from sample: most research articles range 8,000–12,000 words including front matter and appendices.

### Abstract
Unstructured prose format (not divided into Background/Methods/Results/Conclusions). Length approximately 150–200 words. Includes research scope, methodology, and key quantitative findings.

### Citations
Author-year format (both in-text and parenthetical acceptable). No numbered footnote citations. Follows FIELD_SKILL patterns: high density in introduction/literature review, medium in methods, low-to-medium in results.

### Headings
Main section headings unnumbered (preferred) or numbered with Arabic numerals and decimal subsections (less common, not standardized). No Roman numerals observed.

### Tables and Figures
Tables include descriptive titles, detailed notes explaining abbreviations and methodology, source citations, and statistical notation (standard errors, significance markers, clustering information). Figures illustrate key outcome variation, distributional patterns, and event-study designs. Both are common and expected.

### Explicit Prohibitions
None stated in accessible guidelines. No mention of word limits, required formatting, or forbidden content types.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Required Metadata Sections (Cambridge Core Standard)
All articles include four formally labeled metadata sections **not mentioned in FIELD_SKILL**:
- **Competing Interests:** authors declare financial/personal conflicts
- **Funding:** sources of financial support explicitly stated
- **Author Contributions:** each author's role listed
- **Data Availability Statement:** indicates where data/code/materials are accessible or explains restrictions

**Classification:** ENFORCED (universal across sample; appears to be Cambridge Core/open-access publisher requirement, not journal-specific, but mandatory for submission)

**Implication:** These sections must appear in final manuscript, typically before References.

### Policy Recommendation Emphasis (Optional but Common)
Five of six articles in sample include explicit policy recommendations, often in the conclusion framing (e.g., "Conclusion and Policy Recommendation" as heading) or as a dedicated closing paragraph.

**Classification:** PRACTICE ONLY (not mandated in guidelines; common enough to expect but not required)

**Implication:** Policy relevance is valued; conclude with actionable implications where appropriate.

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Voice and person:** First-person plural ("we") standard, matching FIELD_SKILL exactly. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Active voice preference:** Strongly preferred; passive reserved for data generation not under authors' control. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Sentence length and complexity:** Medium-to-long sentences justified by technical content. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Paragraph density:** High; typically 4–10 sentences per paragraph. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Technical vocabulary:** Discipline-specific terminology used without definition. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Hedging intensity:** Low; claims defended through identification, robustness, and transparency rather than verbal hedging. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Identification strategy:** Required element in empirical papers; stated clearly and assumptions defended. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Results reporting:** Coefficients reported with quantitative magnitudes (e.g., "9 per cent reduction," "1.3-point rise"). Statistical significance and economic significance both reported. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Robustness checks:** Standard; common formats include alternative specifications, placebo tests, endogeneity checks (IV, spatial models, fixed-effects logit). FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Citation format and density:** Author-year parenthetical or in-text; high in introduction, low in conclusion. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Disagreement handling:** Precise framing of differences from prior work; explains sources of discrepancy. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Conclusion length:** Short relative to paper; typically 1–3 pages. FIELD DEFAULT.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Omit formal metadata sections (Competing Interests, Funding, Author Contributions, Data Availability Statement).** These are mandatory for Cambridge Core submission; include them in final submission even if not explicitly stated in downloadable guidelines.

2. **Fail to position work relative to specific prior studies.** Editors expect precise articulation of what the paper does differently from closely similar work; vague gap-framing ("limited research exists") is insufficient.

3. **Report statistical significance without economic/policy magnitude.** The journal values applied policy relevance; report effect sizes alongside p-values.

4. **Neglect supplementary material.** Robustness checks and detailed proofs should be in online appendix, not main text, but must be explicitly referenced with DOI.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

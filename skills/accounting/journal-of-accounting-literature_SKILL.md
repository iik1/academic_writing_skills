# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Accounting Literature

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** ACCOUNT
- **Publisher:** Emerald (acquired the journal in 2022; previously Elsevier 2013-2019, University of Florida 1982-2012)
- **Scope:** An interdisciplinary forum publishing both synthesis/literature-review articles and original empirical research covering "virtually all aspects of accounting."
- **Guidelines URL:** Author guidelines page returned 404/403 during this review; findings below are inferred from Emerald's standard journal template (confirmed via consistent article-level evidence) and Emerald's general Harvard-referencing and structured-abstract policies.
- **Field baseline:** account-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 8 (Bhattacharya & Gupta 2025 "Diligence, objectivity, quality and accuracy"; Zhong et al. 2025 "Industrial robots and firm innovation"; Muttakin & Khan 2025 "CEO tenure, board monitoring and competitive corporate culture"; Bisogno, Abate & Citro 2025 "Environmental and sustainability accounting, budgeting and reporting"; Hussinki et al. 2025 "Accounting for intangibles: a critical review"; Olbert 2025 "Industry-specific stock valuation methods"; Barani et al. 2025 "How environmental management accounting drives performance"; unnamed 2025 "Neurodiversity, corporate boards and corporate performance")

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No distinct journal-specific structure requirement found beyond Emerald's standard template. Articles otherwise follow conventional IMRaD-style organization consistent with FIELD_SKILL, adapted for review-type papers (see Structural Deviations below).

### Length
Not confirmed from guidelines page (inaccessible). No journal-specific limit identified in sampled articles.

### Abstract
**ENFORCED.** JAL requires Emerald's standard structured abstract with mandatory sub-headings: **Purpose**, **Design/methodology/approach**, **Findings**, and **Originality/value**, plus conditional sub-headings (Research limitations/implications, Practical implications, Social implications) where applicable. Confirmed across all 8 sampled articles regardless of method — including a difference-in-differences archival paper, a meta-analysis, and multiple structured literature reviews. Maximum abstract length is 250 words per Emerald house policy (word count includes keywords and classification).

### Citations
Emerald house style uses the Harvard (author-date) system, with references in alphabetical order in the end-of-paper list. This aligns with FIELD_SKILL's author-date convention; no journal-specific deviation observed beyond Emerald's standard alphabetization requirement.

### Headings
Not independently confirmed beyond Emerald's standard template; no evidence of Roman-numeral/ALL-CAPS convention (contrast with AAA-journal style noted in FIELD_SKILL).

### Tables and Figures
No journal-specific rule identified beyond FIELD_SKILL norms.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Emerald policy (journal-wide, not JAL-specific): generative AI may not be used to draft/compose new manuscript text; AI use is permitted only for copy-editing author's own original work. Ethical-approval statement required for studies involving human participants (institution, approval number, date, consent confirmation).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
**ENFORCED / journal-defining.** JAL's article population is dominated by literature-review, bibliometric, and meta-analytic papers (structured literature reviews, systematic literature reviews, critical reviews, bibliometric analyses) alongside original empirical papers, more so than the FIELD_SKILL baseline (drawn from ABS 4*/4 journals where empirical/experimental papers dominate and reviews are rare). Review-type articles follow a review-specific shape: Purpose/motivation, methodology of the review (search strategy, database, date range, number of papers screened — e.g., "136 peer-reviewed journal articles... using the Scopus database"), thematic Findings, and a Research Agenda/future-research section in place of hypothesis-driven Results. Authors targeting JAL should expect this structure to be a first-class, reviewer-normal format, not a minor variant.

### Abstract Format
See Hard Constraints above — this is the single largest delta from FIELD_SKILL, which describes an unstructured single-paragraph abstract as the field norm. JAL enforces Emerald's labeled sub-heading format universally, including for archival/quantitative papers that would otherwise follow FIELD_SKILL's unstructured convention.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
JEL codes appear in the keyword/classification field for empirical papers (e.g., "C23, C26, G12, G14, G24, M41"), consistent with FIELD_SKILL but delivered as part of the structured abstract's classification block rather than a separate field.

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting an unstructured, single-paragraph abstract — even for a purely archival/quantitative paper, JAL requires Emerald's labeled sub-headings (Purpose, Design/methodology/approach, Findings, Originality/value).
- Treating a literature-review submission as a lesser or unusual format — JAL's article mix indicates structured/systematic literature reviews and bibliometric analyses are a mainstream, expected article type, and should include an explicit search methodology (database, years covered, screening criteria) comparable in rigor to an empirical Data/Methods section.
- Omitting the Originality/value sub-heading, which is mandatory under Emerald's structured-abstract template but has no equivalent requirement in the FIELD_SKILL baseline.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

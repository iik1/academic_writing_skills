# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Economic Issues

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Taylor & Francis on behalf of Association for Evolutionary Economics (AFEE)
- **Scope:** Institutional and heterodox economics; analysis of economic phenomena through formal and informal rules regulating resource use, technology, economic power distribution, and human agency. Welcomes papers on public policy, development, environmental issues, labour relations, monetary management, and institutional theory.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://afee.net/jei-author-guidelines/ (summary); detailed guidelines available from publisher
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 9 (Gary Dymski, Charles Whalen, Danielle Guizzo, Alicia Girón, Larry Wigger from Vol. 58 Issue 2, 2024; additional articles Vol. 59-60, 2025-2026)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows FIELD_SKILL norms. Typical institutional economics articles exhibit standard section architecture (Introduction, substantive body sections, Conclusion). Institutional focus means explicit attention to rules, institutions, and power relationships often more prominent than in mainstream economics papers.

### Length
No explicit word limit stated in publicly available guidelines. Observed articles range 8,000–30,000 words depending on type (short policy briefs ~3,000 words; full research articles 15,000–25,000 words typical). Abstracts deliberately concise.

### Abstract
**200 words maximum.** Structured abstract preferred but not mandatory. Keywords (3–5) and JEL classification codes (3–5) required. No abstract author affiliations; title and author name(s) precede abstract.

### Citations
**Chicago Manual of Style (CMOS), 17th edition, author-year format (parenthetical preferred).** Direct quotation rare; paraphrase with attribution is the norm. Footnotes carry supplementary material, not primary citations. Reference list must be complete and accurate; papers with improperly formatted references are returned for correction prior to peer review.

### Headings
Section headings use Arabic numerals or Roman numerals; both acceptable. No hierarchical requirement specified. Subheadings may be unnumbered. Capitalize title case.

### Tables and Figures
Save each table and figure in a separate file, not embedded in text. Minimum font size: 8 point. **Mandatory source notation:** all tables and figures containing empirical data MUST include a "Source:" line citing data origin. This is strictly enforced. Journal page dimensions: 4.75" × 7" text area; design accordingly.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Papers with Chicago-style reference formatting errors are returned to authors for revision before being sent to review. Font choices outside Goudy Old Style, Times New Roman, and Arial will trigger a request for reformatting.

### Submission Method
Submit via Editorial Express (https://editorialexpress.com/jei) or by email to editor. Manuscripts must be Microsoft Word compatible.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Institutional Focus and Language
Deviates from FIELD_SKILL baseline in rhetorical emphasis. Whereas top-tier economics journals foreground equilibrium analysis and rational-actor framing, JEI articles prominently feature institutional analysis: formal and informal rules, power structures, historical context, and collective action. Language reflects this: terms like "ceremonial," "instrumental," "evolutionary," "transaction," and "institutional environment" appear frequently and carry domain-specific theoretical weight absent from neoclassical journals. This is not stylistic preference—it is disciplinary commitment. **ENFORCED** (stated in journal scope; confirmed in all sampled articles).

### Methodological Pluralism and Heterodox Frameworks
Deviates from FIELD_SKILL baseline. Top-tier economics journals assume a default analytical vocabulary (utility maximization, general equilibrium, rational expectations). JEI articles routinely deploy post-Keynesian, Marxian, institutional, and ecological frameworks without defending their analytical legitimacy. Multiple theoretical frameworks coexist within a single issue without hierarchical ranking. This reflects institutional economics' deliberate rejection of methodological monism. Authors need not reconcile their framework to neoclassical assumptions. **ENFORCED** (observed across all nine articles; stated in journal mission).

### Explicit Attention to Power and Distribution
Deviates from FIELD_SKILL baseline. While top-tier journals discuss distributional effects, JEI articles foreground power relations, class dynamics, and institutional capacity for coercion as primary analytical objects, not secondary observations. Articles frequently examine who controls resources, whose interests are served by institutional arrangements, and how power asymmetries shape economic outcomes. Language is direct: "power," "domination," "privilege," "marginalization" appear in abstracts and titles. This reflects the journal's heterodox commitment and institutional economics' historical roots in critiques of corporate power. **ENFORCED** (observed in Dymski, Guizzo, Girón, Whalen articles; stated in journal scope).

### Citations of Foundational Heterodox Texts
Deviates from FIELD_SKILL baseline. Articles routinely cite Veblen, Commons, and other original institutionalists (1880s–1960s figures) alongside contemporary work. This is not antiquarian—these citations carry live theoretical weight. A JEI author might deploy Veblen's concept of "ceremonial" or Commons' "transaction" to frame contemporary analysis. This practice is rare in top-tier journals, where foundational classics are typically cited for historical context only. Here they shape current argument. **ENFORCED** (all nine articles cite institutional economics canon; Dymski article 386–394 makes this explicit as methodological debate).

### Discussion of Institutional Design and Alternatives
Observed emphasis (but not stated as requirement) on constructive proposals for institutional change or alternatives. Several articles (e.g., Wigger on supply chains, Guizzo on universities) conclude with discussion of how institutions could be redesigned to serve different ends. This reflects institutional economics' applied policy tradition—the journal welcomes prescriptive analysis alongside diagnosis. **PRACTICE ONLY** (observed in 5 of 9 articles; not stated as requirement).

### Mixed-Tense Narrative on Economic History and Institutional Evolution
Follows FIELD_SKILL precedent but with stronger emphasis on historical narration. Articles frequently sketch institutional history (e.g., rise of financialization, evolution of corporate forms, technological change) as integral to explaining present conditions. This historical framing is common in institutional work but more pronounced here than in field baseline. **FIELD DEFAULT** (consistent with FIELD_SKILL; no deviation, merely stronger emphasis).

## Dimensions With No Delta
- **Voice and Person:** First-person plural ("we") is standard; active voice preferred. Follows FIELD_SKILL norm exactly.
- **Sentence and Paragraph Style:** Medium to long sentences justified by technical content. High paragraph density. Follows FIELD_SKILL norm exactly.
- **Argumentation and Rhetorical Moves:** Introduction announces contributions and results; conclusion revisits findings and implications. Structure matches FIELD_SKILL archetype.
- **Hedging and Epistemic Caution:** Low hedging intensity. Clear claims preferred. Identification strategy and robustness checks present where empirical. Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.
- **Statistical Reporting:** When results presented, quantitative specificity expected alongside statistical significance. Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.
- **Results and Interpretation:** Results and interpretation integrated; separate Discussion sections less common but acceptable. Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.
- **Conclusion Norms:** Conclusions are shorter than introductions, synthesize findings, draw implications, acknowledge limitations. Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.

## Common Pitfalls Specific to JEI

1. **Uncited empirical data in tables/figures.** The "Source:" notation is non-negotiable; papers lacking it are returned for correction before review. This is more strictly enforced than in mainstream journals.

2. **Chicago-style reference errors.** Author-date format must be precise; missing publication details, inconsistent formatting, or non-compliance with CMOS 17th edition will trigger a formatting request. Allocate extra review time for reference checking.

3. **Unclear institutional focus.** Papers that read as mainstream economics with a heterodox label appended tend to receive critical reception. JEI reviewers expect genuine engagement with institutional analysis (formal/informal rules, power, path dependence), not merely application of heterodox rhetoric. Ensure institutional mechanisms are central to your argument, not peripheral.

4. **Methodological defensiveness.** Conversely, authors new to heterodox work sometimes over-defend their framework against imagined neoclassical objections. JEI does not require you to justify departing from utility maximization or general equilibrium. State your framework clearly and proceed; the journal's readers expect pluralism.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

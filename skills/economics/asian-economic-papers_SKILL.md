# Journal Writing Skill: Asian Economic Papers

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** MIT Press (journal sponsored by the Center for Sustainable Development at Columbia University, the Korea Institute for International Economic Policy, the Economic Research Institute for ASEAN and East Asia, and Liaoning University)
- **Scope:** Rigorous analysis of key economic issues facing a particular Asian economy or the broader Asian region, with an explicit orientation toward policy solutions. Papers originate primarily from presentations at Asian Economic Panel meetings (held three times a year), with a subset selected for publication.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://direct.mit.edu/asep/pages/submission-guidelines (accessed via search-engine cache; direct fetch returned 403)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 titles with abstracts read (Veeramani & Basu 2025 "Why Is Labor Receiving a Declining Share of Income in India?"; Kam, Lee & Park 2025 "Remote Work as a Policy Tool for Easing Megacity Overcrowding"; Pham et al. "External Financing and Innovation in SMEs — The Case of Vietnam"; Chun, Fukao, Kwon & Park 2024 "Why Do Real Wages Stagnate in Japan and Korea?"; Seok & You 2025 "The Macroeconomic Effects of Income Tax Changes in Korea" [title/authors confirmed, abstract inaccessible]; Xia, "Comments on Strategic Ambiguity in the Time of a Second Cold War" 2024) plus ~13 additional titles/author lists reviewed across Vol. 24 Issues 1–3 (2025) for topical and format pattern-checking. Full text was not accessible for any article (paywalled); abstracts and metadata only. This is a thinner sample than higher-tier journals in this batch, consistent with ABS 1 access constraints — documented per instructions.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Order specified explicitly by the guidelines: title page, abstract, main text of paper (including footnotes), appendix (if applicable), references, tables, and figures — with tables and figures placed at the end of the manuscript on separate pages, not embedded in text. This end-loaded placement is a formatting instruction, not a content-structure claim; it does not indicate anything about section headings within the main text.

### Length
No explicit word/page limit stated in the found guidelines beyond formatting mechanics (double-spaced, 1.25-inch margins, 12-pt Times New Roman, 10-pt acceptable for tables/figures).

### Abstract
Explicit hard limit: **100 words maximum**, "in a form suitable for the Journal of Economic Literature." This is stricter than FIELD_SKILL, which does not specify a word limit. The sampled abstract (Veeramani & Basu) runs close to this ceiling, suggesting authors use the full allowance rather than writing shorter.

### Citations
In-text/footnote citations give only author name(s), year, and optionally a page or chapter number — consistent with FIELD_SKILL's author-year norm. Reference list requirement is stricter than typical: full first and last names of authors (not initials) and full journal names (no abbreviations), alphabetical by author surname then year ascending.

### Headings
Text should follow Chicago Manual of Style, 15th ed., and Merriam-Webster's Collegiate Dictionary, 11th ed. No specific heading-numbering scheme mandated beyond this.

### Tables and Figures
Each table/figure self-explanatory, one per page, placed at manuscript end in order. Figures must be black and white, no grey backgrounds, submitted as Excel files or preferably EPS/TIFF at 900–1200 dpi. This resolution/format requirement is unusually specific and has no FIELD_SKILL analog.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Submissions must be original, unpublished work not under review elsewhere (standard). Submission is via a dedicated portal (https://aep.jci.edu.my/) or, per older guideline text, direct email to the editor — reflecting the journal's Asian Economic Panel–linked submission pipeline rather than a standard journal portal.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
AEP publishes short standalone **discussant "Comments" articles** alongside main papers — e.g., "Comments by Senmao Xia, on Strategic Ambiguity in the Time of a Second Cold War." This is a direct legacy of the journal's origin as a publication of papers and discussant remarks from Asian Economic Panel meetings. These comment pieces are brief, non-IMRAD, and not held to the empirical/theory paper structures in FIELD_SKILL. This is ENFORCED as a recurring journal-specific article type (observed across multiple issues), not a one-off. Authors submitting a main paper should not expect this format to apply to them, but should be aware discussant comments are a normal accompanying feature of the journal's issues.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Topical scope is narrower than FIELD_SKILL implies: every sampled article addresses a specific Asian country or subregion (India, Korea, Vietnam, Japan, China, ASEAN) with an explicit policy angle, consistent with the journal's stated mission of "creative solutions to Asian economic issues." This is FIELD DEFAULT in argumentative structure but a GUIDELINE ONLY / PRACTICE scope constraint: papers lacking a clear Asia-specific empirical or policy setting are a poor fit regardless of technical quality.

## Common Pitfalls
- Exceeding the 100-word abstract cap — FIELD_SKILL sets no ceiling, so authors drafting to general ECON norms may overshoot.
- Embedding tables/figures in the text body rather than placing them at the end of the manuscript as required.
- Abbreviating journal names or using initials-only author names in the reference list, contrary to the explicit full-name/full-journal-name requirement.
- Submitting a paper with no clear Asia-specific country or regional focus, which is a poor scope fit even if technically sound.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

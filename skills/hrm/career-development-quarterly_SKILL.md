# Journal Writing Skill: Career Development Quarterly

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** HRM&EMP (Career Counseling, Guidance)
- **Publisher:** Wiley-Blackwell (on behalf of NCDA)
- **Scope:** Official journal of the National Career Development Association. Publishes empirical and conceptual articles on career counseling, career development, career education, coaching, and management across the lifespan, with emphasis on practical implications for practitioners.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/page/journal/21610045/homepage/forauthors.html (author portal); https://www.ncda.org/aws/NCDA/pt/sp/cdquarterly_submissions (NCDA submission page)
- **Field baseline:** hrm-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 4 (editorials and article metadata from Vol. 72, 2024; full-text access limited by paywall)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
All manuscripts must include in this order:
- Title
- Abstract (150 words maximum, not to exceed)
- Keywords (number not specified in guidelines)
- Introduction
- Method
- Results
- Discussion
- Limitations and Future Directions (as distinct subsection or clearly marked paragraph)
- Conclusion
- References

**Deviations from FIELD_SKILL:** The explicit "Limitations and Future Directions" as a required *separate* subsection is journal-mandated; FIELD_SKILL notes this is common but variable. CDQ makes it required.

### Length
- Maximum 8,000 words for main manuscript text
- This is notably shorter than typical ABS 4*/4 journals (which range 10,000–15,000); ABS 2 tier reflects this constraint
- Word limit includes title, abstract, keywords, main body, headings, and in-text citations but may exclude references (guidelines unclear on precise count method)

**Deviations from FIELD_SKILL:** Stricter than field norm; expect tighter argument compression.

### Abstract
- Structured format required (follows APA 7 conventions)
- Must include summary of Introduction, Method, Results, and Discussion in sequence, one sentence per section minimum
- 150-word hard ceiling
- Written as single paragraph

**Deviations from FIELD_SKILL:** Field baseline does not specify structured format; CDQ enforces it.

### Citations
- APA 7th edition (Publication Manual of the American Psychological Association, 7th ed.)
- Author-date in-text citations (e.g., Jones, 2020; Smith & Brown, 2021)
- Complete reference list, alphabetically ordered by author surname
- U.S. spelling throughout

**Deviations from FIELD_SKILL:** Field allows APA variant flexibility; CDQ mandates APA 7 specifically.

### Headings
- Follows APA 7 heading hierarchy (no specific deviation from standard APA visible in guidelines or examples)
- "Limitations and Future Directions" appears to be a Level 2 heading in typical submissions

**Deviations from FIELD_SKILL:** Follows field norm. No journal-specific modification observed.

### Tables and Figures
- Guidelines silent on specific requirements (Wiley standard practices apply)
- Must include captions and source attribution per APA 7

**Deviations from FIELD_SKILL:** Follows field norm. No deviation noted.

### Language and Tone
- U.S. spelling mandatory (explicitly stated: "The Career Development Quarterly uses U.S. spelling")
- No footnotes or endnotes allowed; all material must be incorporated into main text
- Formal academic register (consistent with field norm)

**Deviations from FIELD_SKILL:** Explicit prohibition on footnotes/endnotes is journal-specific; FIELD_SKILL does not address this.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- **No footnotes or endnotes:** All content must be woven into main text
- **No undefined acronyms:** Spell out on first use, then acronym in parentheses (APA 7 standard)

**Deviations from FIELD_SKILL:** Footnote prohibition is explicit here; field baseline does not address this issue.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Practical Implications as Non-Negotiable Requirement
CDQ mandates that all manuscripts "include implications for practice" in the discussion/conclusion. This aligns with FIELD_SKILL's expectation that practical implications are "required," but CDQ enforces it as a hard constraint at submission, not merely as a reviewer expectation.

**Classification:** ENFORCED. This is stated in author guidelines and evidenced by editorial framing that journal is "dedicated to producing articles that help enhance career development."

### Narrower Scope for Theoretical Frames
While FIELD_SKILL notes no consensus on psychological/managerial framing versus sociological/institutional framing, CDQ editorials (2025) emphasize **social constructionism, vocational identity development, and professional identity** — suggesting a tilt toward counseling-psychology and identity-theory frames over pure organizational HRM theory (e.g., less emphasis on organizational behavior theories like AMO).

**Classification:** PRACTICE ONLY / LOW-PREVALENCE. Not stated in guidelines; inferred from two editorials. Reflects journal's NCDA/counseling background rather than organizational focus. Authors from organizational-psychology or industrial-relations traditions may still publish successfully.

### Word Compression and Argument Density
The 8,000-word limit is significantly lower than field norm (10,000–15,000+). This creates **pressure to compress literature review, streamline methods descriptions, and embed literature engagement into the introduction and discussion** rather than as a discrete "literature review" section.

**Classification:** RELAXATION (if viewed as a field-level constraint). The field baseline assumes space for a standalone literature review; CDQ's 8,000-word limit may require integrating review into introduction. This is a *relaxation* of the structural space available, not a new requirement.

### Methodological Breadth Without Hierarchy
FIELD_SKILL notes that "no single estimation approach is treated as obligatory" in HRM quantitative work. CDQ editorial guidelines and published articles confirm this field practice, but also actively welcome **qualitative, mixed methods, and literature reviews** equally — no implicit preference for quantitative work evident (unlike some ABS 4 economics journals).

**Classification:** FIELD DEFAULT. CDQ follows and exemplifies field practice; no deviation.

### No Explicit Hedging or Causal-Language Guidance in Guidelines
FIELD_SKILL emphasizes "moderate to high" hedging and causal-language caution. CDQ guidelines contain no explicit statement on this. Editorial practice (editorials reviewed) shows normal academic hedging ("suggest," "may," "appear"), consistent with field norm, but no journal-specific intensity or style guidance visible.

**Classification:** FIELD DEFAULT. No delta detected in available materials.

## Common Pitfalls Specific to Career Development Quarterly

1. **Weak or absent practical implications.** CDQ reviewers expect clear, actionable takeaways for career counselors, educators, or practitioners — not just theoretical contributions. A paper strong on theory but vague on "so what for practitioners?" will be rejected or heavily revised.

2. **Exceeding word limit.** With an 8,000-word ceiling (vs. field norm of 10,000+), authors often fail to compress methods or literature engagement enough. Expect rejection for length if you exceed the limit even slightly.

3. **Footnotes or endnotes in lieu of main-text clarity.** CDQ prohibits footnotes; authors accustomed to using them for asides or secondary points must integrate those into the text. This forces tighter writing but causes formatting friction for those unfamiliar with the rule.

4. **Unclear abstract structure.** CDQ requires a structured abstract (Intro, Method, Results, Discussion in sequence). Authors who write a narrative abstract or omit one of the four required components will face desk rejection or revision request.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

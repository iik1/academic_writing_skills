## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict. Based on Inderscience standard requirements applicable to IJEV.

### Structure
Follows standard empirical structure (based on article titles and topics in recent issues): Abstract, Keywords, Introduction (with literature review integrated or as a separate section for longer papers), Methods/Methodology, Results, Discussion (often with subsections on implications and/or contributions), Limitations and Future Research, Conclusion, and References. Conceptual/review papers and meta-analyses substitute thematic organization but retain the scholarly arc. Appendices for supplementary materials, measurement instruments, and robustness checks are standard.

### Length
Manuscripts should normally be **5000–7000 words** (excluding figures, tables, references, and appendices). High-quality articles exceeding 7000 words will be considered. This is RELAXATION from the ABS 4*/4 baseline (which permits longer theoretical papers); IJEV shows a tighter word envelope.

### Abstract
**Structured or unstructured** abstract, **less than 150 words**. Must briefly cover the aim of research, methodologies used, and conclusions. Abstract should be precise and factual. Keywords: approximately **10–15 words or phrases** (emphasized as important for online discoverability).

### Citations
**Harvard style (author-date)** in text (e.g., "Hamel (2000) suggests..." or "(Nonaka & Takeuchi 1995)") with full alphabetical reference list at end. All author names and initials, publication date, title of article/chapter, title of source (italicized for books/journals), volume and issue number, publisher (for books) or journal name, page numbers. No footnote citations. This is ENFORCED across all Inderscience journals.

### Headings
Follows standard numbered or unnumbered hierarchical scheme. Specifics not detailed in publicly available guidelines; likely follows Inderscience default (centered main heads, left-aligned subheads, or numbered sections depending on template). FIELD_SKILL conventions (clear labeling, logical nesting) apply in absence of explicit journal override.

### Tables and Figures
Standard scholarly presentation. Tables and figures should be self-contained (clear captions, legends). No explicit restrictions found; FIELD_SKILL conventions (correlation tables, regression output, path diagrams, interaction plots) are standard. Figures depicting effects and models are integral to empirical and meta-analytic articles.

### Language and Register
**UK English and terminology** required. Register is formal, professional academic tone. First-person plural voice ("we") is standard (per FIELD_SKILL), active voice preferred. No explicit restrictions on hedging or declarative language found; FIELD_SKILL norms apply.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Articles must not have been previously published or be under consideration elsewhere (standard dual-submission prohibition).
- Conference papers acceptable only if "completely re-written."
- Briefs and research notes are not published in this journal; full-length empirical, conceptual, review, or case-study articles only.
- Double-blind peer review; all authors must agree on corresponding author and author order before submission.
- Changes to authorship only permissible during review with Editor-in-Chief approval.
- AI tools must be disclosed in Acknowledgements (except grammar checkers and reference managers); AI cannot be listed as author.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Exceeding 7000 words without strong justification.** IJEV enforces a tighter envelope than top-tier journals. Submissions over 7000 words are not rejected out of hand but require high quality and will face more scrutiny for necessity. Prune literature reviews and methodological exposition.

2. **Failing to clearly enumerate contributions.** While FIELD_SKILL already mandates explicit contribution framing, IJEV's tight word limit makes it critical to front-load contributions in the introduction and map them back in the discussion with precision—wasted words on vague framing will hurt your submission.

3. **Insufficient detail on construct measurement or sampling for online-panel studies.** IJEV publishes entrepreneurship and SME research heavily reliant on survey panels (Prolific, MTurk, convenience samples). Editors expect detailed disclosure of sampling procedures and construct reliability (Cronbach's alpha). Brief methods sections that shortcut these details invite desk rejection or major revisions.

4. **Ignoring the "briefs and research notes" exclusion.** IJEV does not publish short form or preliminary findings. If your work reads as a pilot study or preliminary investigation, it will not be considered. Ensure your article is a complete, standalone empirical or conceptual contribution.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

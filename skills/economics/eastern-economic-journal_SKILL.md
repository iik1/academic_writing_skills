# Journal Writing Skill: Eastern Economic Journal

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Springer Nature (Palgrave Macmillan imprint), on behalf of the Eastern Economic Association
- **Scope:** General-interest economics journal publishing applied micro, labor, public, regional, sports, and methodological/pedagogical economics; broader and more heterogeneous in topic and rigor than ABS 4*/4 general-interest journals.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://link.springer.com/journal/41302/submission-guidelines (accessible via search-engine cache/snippets only; direct fetch redirects to a Springer IDP login wall in this environment)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 13 (abstract/front-matter level, via RePEc/SSRN/search-snippet access) — "Why are Schools Reclassifying Their Economics Major?" (Mahon & Asarta, 2024); "Innovation and Employment Cyclicality: Evidence from U.S. States" (Hovhannisyan & Schwartz, 2026); "The Carbon Footprint of U.S. Monetary Policy" (Amialchuk, Sapci & Sapci, 2026); "State-Level Institutions and Entrepreneurship: A Spatial Hierarchical Probit Approach" (Hall, Rubenstein & Lacombe, 2026); "The EITC and Employment of Women with Disabilities" (Baughman & Houtenville, 2026); "Balls and Strikes: The Effects on Domestic Violence" (Mudrow, 2026); "The Desire to Win: Compensating Wage Differentials in the NBA" (Kahane, 2026); "The Impact of Marijuana Decriminalization on Crime: Evidence from Atlanta" (Meehan, Sharpe & Weitkamp, 2026); "The Impact of Remittances on Food Security in Times of Crisis" (Cannonier & Burke, 2026); "An Analytical History of the Yule-Frisch-Waugh-Lovell Theorem" (Basu, 2026); "Information Disclosure, Bidder Entry, and Auction Revenues: Evidence from eBay" (Eddhir, 2026); plus two additional 2026 titles confirmed only by headline/author (abstract not recoverable).

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No mandatory section structure is specified in the public guidelines beyond standard IMRaD-type conventions; initial submissions explicitly need not follow any formatting guidelines at all — formatting is deferred until after acceptance, when a professional typesetter applies house style. Authors are instructed not to format layout or appearance themselves at submission stage.

### Length
No stated word or page limit found in accessible guideline text.

### Abstract
Structured differently from FIELD_SKILL's implicit norm: abstract must be **no more than 100 words** (short, hard cap), accompanied by **1–3 JEL classification codes** and **2–5 keywords**. FIELD_SKILL does not specify an abstract length; this is a journal-specific hard constraint.

### Citations
Reference style is essentially Chicago Manual of Style author-date, not a numbered or footnote system: references arranged alphabetically by first author's surname; only the first author's name is inverted in multi-author entries; in-text citations carry only author name and date (no page/locator clutter); only works actually cited in text or notes are listed. This is consistent with FIELD_SKILL's author-year norm (FIELD DEFAULT) but the Chicago-specific formatting mechanics are a journal-specific detail.

### Headings
No journal-specific heading convention found beyond general typesetter-applied house style; no delta from FIELD_SKILL identified.

### Tables and Figures
No specific rules found in accessible guideline text beyond standard typesetting deferral (do not self-format at submission).

### Explicit Prohibitions
Do not format the manuscript's layout/appearance at initial submission (deferred to typesetter). Formatting-mechanics prohibitions only (spacing, blank lines, line breaks) — not content-related.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
**Topical and rigor range is wider than the ABS 4*/4 baseline (RELAXATION).** The sampled articles include applied micro/labor/public economics of the type FIELD_SKILL describes, but also sports economics (NBA contracts, MLB umpire calls), a pedagogy/institutional-classification paper (economics major reclassification), and a methodological/historical paper on an estimation theorem (YFWL theorem) written for a broad audience rather than a specialist one. This is consistent with an ABS 1 general-interest journal accepting a wider range of contribution types than top-tier outlets, not a distinct voice or structural norm. Authors targeting this journal should not assume the identification-strategy rigor or novelty bar of an ABS 4 paper is required, but should still follow FIELD_SKILL's core reporting and argumentation conventions.

## Common Pitfalls
- Exceeding the 100-word abstract cap — this is materially shorter than many authors default to and shorter than typical practice at higher-tier econ journals.
- Supplying the wrong number of JEL codes or keywords (must be 1–3 JEL codes and 2–5 keywords, not open-ended).
- Attempting to apply final typeset formatting (fonts, spacing, layout) at initial submission instead of leaving this to the post-acceptance typesetter.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

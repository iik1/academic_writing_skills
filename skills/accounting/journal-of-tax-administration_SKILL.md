# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Tax Administration

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ACCOUNT
- **Publisher:** University of Exeter Business School (Tax Administration Research Centre) / Chartered Institute of Taxation
- **Scope:** Peer-reviewed, open-access journal providing a forum for academics, tax policymakers, and tax practitioners to share knowledge and research on all aspects of tax administration (established 2015).
- **Guidelines URL:** https://jota.website/jota/author-preparing-your-manuscript, https://jota.website/jota/author-general-formatting-requirements
- **Field baseline:** account-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 articles across volumes 8-10 (2023-2025): Griffiths & Handford (tax exceptionalism), Purba & Tran (profit shifting), Thiemann (cryptocurrencies), Wilson & Oats (literature review), plus structural review of articles on audit quality and media discourse.

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
- **Anonymized submission:** Articles and Short Notes must be double-blind reviewed with all identifying information removed from manuscript body; separate title page required with author names, affiliations, contact details, and corresponding author designation.
- **Section heading hierarchy:** Level 1 headings (12pt, bold, left-aligned, numbered: 1., 2., 3.); Level 2 headings (12pt, bold, left-aligned, unnumbered); Level 3 headings (12pt, italicized, left-aligned); Level 4 headings (12pt, bold and italicized, left-aligned).
- **Placement of tables and figures:** Within the text at appropriate points, not at end; numbered consecutively (Table 1, Fig. 1); titles placed above in italics, left-aligned (e.g., "Table 1: Title"); source attribution below in 10pt Times New Roman.
- **Title page formatting:** Separate document titled "Paper Title – Month Year" with author names, affiliations, contact information, and corresponding author specification.

### Length
- **Abstract:** Maximum 120 words (non-structured, single paragraph).
- **Keywords:** Up to 6 keywords listed after abstract.
- **No explicit overall word limit stated** in guidelines; practice varies but typical articles range 30–50 pages.

### Abstract
- **Format:** Unstructured single paragraph.
- **Word limit:** 120 words maximum.
- **Required for:** Articles, Short Notes, Commentaries.
- **Not required for:** Reviews section.
- **Heading:** "Abstract" (12pt, bold, centered).

### Citations
- **Style:** Author-date format (specific citation format not explicitly detailed in guidelines; articles show standard academic conventions with extensive referencing).
- **References heading:** "Bibliography" or "References" section mandatory for all submissions.

### Headings
- **Paper title:** 14pt, bold, centered, fully capitalized.
- **Level 1 (major sections):** 12pt, bold, left-aligned, numbered (1., 2., 3.).
- **Level 2 (subsections):** 12pt, bold, left-aligned, unnumbered.
- **Level 3 (sub-subsections):** 12pt, italicized, left-aligned.
- **Level 4 (further subdivisions):** 12pt, bold and italicized, left-aligned.
- **Abstract heading:** 12pt, bold, centered.

### Tables and Figures
- **Placement:** Within the text at appropriate points, not grouped at end.
- **Numbering:** Consecutive (Table 1, Table 2; Fig. 1, Fig. 2).
- **Titles:** Positioned above tables/figures, italicized, left-aligned (e.g., "Table 1: Descriptive Statistics").
- **Source attribution:** Below tables/figures in 10pt Times New Roman.
- **Permissions:** Third-party content requires explicit permissions.
- **Format:** Submit electronically in clear quality.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- **AI-generated content:** Papers written using AI tools (e.g., ChatGPT) are not accepted.
- **Originality:** All papers must be original; proper attribution and permissions required for cited works.
- **Ethics:** Research ethics approval must be obtained before submission (implicit for human-subjects research).
- **Data availability:** Raw study data must be available to reviewers and readers.

### General Formatting
- **Font:** 12-point Times New Roman throughout.
- **Spacing:** Double-spaced.
- **Alignment:** Text justified; margins wide.
- **Language:** British or U.S. English (consistent style required).
- **File format:** Microsoft Word or OpenOffice.
- **Footnotes vs. endnotes:** Footnotes preferred; use superscript numbers placed outside punctuation. Citations belong in main text, not footnotes.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Readership and Technical Calibration
**DELTA — ENFORCED:** The journal explicitly specifies a diverse, non-specialist readership (academics, policymakers, tax administrators, practitioners). Technical and methodological discussion must be tailored for this audience: lengthy mathematical derivations must be placed in appendices rather than main text. This is a stricter requirement than FIELD_SKILL's generic assumption of a "specialist in accounting research." Authors should write introduction, conclusion, and abstract sections for non-specialists, even if the methods section assumes some technical knowledge.

### Abstract Structure
**DELTA — ENFORCED:** Guidelines specify maximum 120 words (not stated in FIELD_SKILL). Additionally, FIELD_SKILL mentions "structured summary," but this journal's guidelines state abstracts should be unstructured (single paragraph). This is a meaningful constraint: do not use structured abstract fields (Background/Methods/Results/Conclusion).

### AI Prohibition
**DELTA — ENFORCED:** Explicit policy: papers written using AI tools such as ChatGPT are not accepted. This is a new, journal-specific hard constraint not covered in FIELD_SKILL.

### Heading Format
**DELTA — ENFORCED:** Specific numeric heading style (1., 2., 3. for Level 1) with detailed font size and formatting prescriptions. FIELD_SKILL notes that "practice varies" and journals use "Roman vs. Arabic numerals"; this journal mandates Arabic numerals with period (1., 2., 3.), not Roman or alphabet.

### Figure and Table Placement
**DELTA — ENFORCED:** Tables and figures must be placed within the text at appropriate points, not grouped at end. This aligns with editorial guidance to support a diverse (non-specialist) readership that may benefit from seeing figures and tables close to the relevant prose.

### Double-Blind Review and Anonymization
**DELTA — ENFORCED:** Articles and Short Notes must remove all identifying information from the manuscript body and include a separate, signed title page. This is a strict anonymization requirement. Commentaries and Reviews do not require anonymization but still need a title page with author information.

### Voice and Register
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Articles observed show active voice, first-person plural ("we"), and formal register consistent with the field baseline. No deviation detected.

### Statistical Reporting
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Articles show precise reporting of coefficients, significance levels, and standard errors, with careful hedging of mechanistic interpretations. No journal-specific deviation observed.

### Citation Density
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Introduction sections are citation-rich (15–40+ works); results sections cite when interpreting findings relative to prior work. Density aligns with FIELD_SKILL expectations.

### Structural Deviations
No significant deviations beyond the hard constraints noted above. Articles follow a traditional structure: abstract, introduction, literature/background, methods/data, results, discussion/conclusion, references. This aligns with FIELD_SKILL.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- Voice and person: FIELD DEFAULT (first-person plural, active voice throughout).
- Sentence and paragraph style: FIELD DEFAULT (medium to long sentences, dense paragraphs in methods and results).
- Methodological detail and identification strategy: FIELD DEFAULT (high procedural detail, explicit identification discussion in archival papers).
- Robustness and supplemental analyses: FIELD DEFAULT (expected; typically 3–5 robustness checks).
- Discussion and conclusion tone: FIELD DEFAULT (confident but appropriately hedged; limitations acknowledged explicitly).
- Disagreement with prior work: FIELD DEFAULT (diplomatic framing; reliance on phrasing like "our findings differ from…potentially because…").
- Paraphrase vs. quotation convention: FIELD DEFAULT (paraphrase is the norm; direct quotation from prior scholarship is absent in quantitative articles).

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Submitting with AI-generated content:** The journal has an explicit policy against papers written using ChatGPT or similar AI tools. Ensure full human authorship and declare no AI use in the cover email.
2. **Over-technical presentation for diverse audiences:** Remember the readership includes policymakers and practitioners who are not specialists in quantitative methods. Place complex mathematical derivations in appendices and write introduction/conclusion for non-specialists.
3. **Failing to anonymize properly:** For articles and short notes, ensure the manuscript body contains no author identifying information; use a separate title page. Commentaries and reviews are not anonymized but still require a title page.
4. **Violating the 120-word abstract limit:** The journal enforces a strict 120-word maximum for abstracts. Unstructured, single-paragraph format only. Do not use labeled sections (Background, Methods, Results, Conclusion).

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

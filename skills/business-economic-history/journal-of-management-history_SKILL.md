## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Journal of Management History follows a standard academic article structure with the following components:
- **Structured Abstract:** Required. Must use 4–7 subheadings (typical subheadings in management history include: Purpose, Design/Methodology, Findings, Research Limitations/Implications, and Practical Implications). Abstract is separate from the main text and formatted as a distinct section.
- **Main Body:** Sections organized per FIELD_SKILL norms (introduction with historiographical positioning, methods, substantive sections organized chronologically or thematically, conclusion). No novel structural requirement specific to this journal beyond the structured abstract requirement.
- **Acknowledgments and Author Biographies:** Must be uploaded as separate files (not in main manuscript).

### Length
- **Word Count Target:** Approximately 8,000–12,000 words (standard Emerald journal practice; specific limit for Journal of Management History not independently verified due to access restrictions). Word count includes structured abstract, body text, references, and all text within tables and figures.
- **Abstract:** Maximum 250 words total, including keywords and article classification.
- **Each table or figure:** Adds approximately 280 words to the word count calculation.

### Abstract
- **Format:** Structured (mandatory).
- **Subheadings:** 4–7 subheadings required. Standard practice for management and business journals: Purpose, Design/Methodology or Approach, Findings, Research Limitations/Implications, and Practical Implications.
- **Word Limit:** Maximum 250 words (including keywords, classification codes, and all text under subheadings).
- **Content:** Non-technical summary of the puzzle, argument, contribution, methods, and findings; follows Emerald standard format.

### Citations
- **Style:** Emerald-Harvard (author-date system). This uses parenthetical citations in the text (e.g., "Smith, 1995") and a reference list at the end, not footnote/endnote style. This deviates from the Chicago-style conventions sometimes used in business history but is Emerald's standard.
- **Density and Practice:** Citation density should follow FIELD_SKILL norms (high throughout, not concentrated in literature review section). Direct quotation from primary sources is valued; secondary sources are paraphrased. Disagreement with prior scholarship is expressed directly but courteously (per FIELD_SKILL).

### Headings
No journal-specific deviation from FIELD_SKILL stated. Headings should follow the section structure (Introduction/Problem Setting, Literature/Historiography, Methods/Sources, Substantive Sections, Conclusion). Emerald uses numbered or lettered headings in some formatting; check author guidelines or submission template at point of submission.

### Tables and Figures
- Tables and figures must be clearly sourced and accompanied by prose interpretation (per FIELD_SKILL).
- Each table/figure contributes ~280 words to the stated word count.
- No special caption or notation requirements stated beyond standard academic practice.

### Language and Generative AI
- **Copy-editing with Generative AI:** Permitted. Authors may use generative AI tools to improve structure, clarity, language, and grammar in their own original work, provided they adhere to overarching principles (principally: the work remains the author's original intellectual contribution, and the AI tool is disclosed transparently if required by the journal's open-science policies).

### Explicit Prohibitions
- **Submission Method:** Manuscripts must be submitted exclusively through the ScholarOne system accessible via the Emerald website. Submission by email or through third-party agents is prohibited.
- **Originality:** Manuscripts must be original work not previously published elsewhere.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structured Abstract Requirement
**ENFORCED DELTA.** FIELD_SKILL specifies "compact, non-technical summaries" but does not mandate a structured abstract format. Journal of Management History requires a structured abstract with 4–7 subheadings and a 250-word cap. This is a more formalized, regimented approach than the unstructured abstract norm in top-tier business history journals (which favor prose abstracts). This reflects Emerald's journal-wide standard and represents a relaxation of the flexibility afforded by higher-tier journals in the field, where abstract format varies more widely.

### Citation Style (Emerald-Harvard vs. Chicago)
**ENFORCED DELTA.** FIELD_SKILL does not specify a citation style (noting that this is journal-specific). However, leading ABS 4 journals in BUS HIST & ECON HIST (Business History, Economic History Review) predominantly use Chicago style with footnote/endnote citation. Journal of Management History mandates Emerald-Harvard (author-date system with parenthetical citations and a reference list), a more social-science-oriented standard than the humanities-leaning Chicago style. This may feel less aligned with historiographical tradition and more aligned with economics/management publishing norms.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Unstructured Abstract:** Submitting a prose abstract instead of the required 4–7 subheading format. Verify at submission that your abstract is labeled with explicit subheadings and does not exceed 250 words total.
2. **Wrong Citation Style:** Using Chicago footnote/endnote style (common in business and economic history) instead of Emerald-Harvard author-date. Convert all citations to parenthetical in-text form with a reference list.
3. **Word Count Miscalculation:** Forgetting that your word count must include the structured abstract, references, and all text in tables and figures. This can push a tight manuscript over the limit. Plan for ~8,000–12,000 words as a realistic target.
4. **Submission Outside ScholarOne:** Attempting to submit via email or a third-party platform. All submissions must go through the official ScholarOne system linked from the Emerald journal homepage.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: European Review of Economic History

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** BUS HIST & ECON HIST
- **Publisher:** Oxford University Press (journal migrated from Cambridge University Press to OUP; current hosting is academic.oup.com)
- **Scope:** Publishes quantitative and analytical economic history research, spanning pre-industrial to modern periods, with an editorial emphasis on rigorous empirical methods applied to historical questions (official journal of the European Historical Economics Society).
- **Guidelines URL:** https://academic.oup.com/ereh/pages/general_instructions
- **Field baseline:** business-economic-history-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 17 (title/abstract level, across Vol. 28 Iss. 3 [2024], Vol. 29 Iss. 1 [2025], Vol. 30 Iss. 1 [2026], and Advance Articles [2026]): "Does speculation increase volatility in grain futures markets?"; "Age structure and age heaping..."; "Across the Sea to Ireland..."; "Gender inequality and occupational segregation in white-collar jobs..."; "Wealth inequality in northwestern Anatolia..."; "After da Gama: Real Wages in Western India..."; "To Block or Not..."; "Scarring Through the 1923 German Hyperinflation"; "A Whirligig of Revolutionary Presidents..."; "Government, Trusts, and the Making of Better Roads..."; "Is There a Refugee Gap?..."; "How Deep Are the Roots of Swedish Egalitarianism?..."; "Measuring the Edwardian Property Slump..."; "Labor-Saving Durables, Women's Labor-Force Participation..."; "Procuring Promising Provisions..."; "Fuel Consumption and Welfare Ratios..."; "Enclosures and Productivity in Eastern Sweden..."

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Manuscript order for accepted papers: main text (double-spaced) → footnotes (collected at end, double-spaced, kept minimal) → tables and figures (placed at end, with in-text placement indicators) → acknowledgements → references. Footnotes, not endnotes-as-citations — citations use author-date in text, footnotes reserved for substantive asides and are to be kept short.

### Length
Full articles: maximum 10,000 words. Research notes / debate contributions: under 5,000 words, intended specifically to advance new ideas or approaches rather than full empirical papers.

### Abstract
100-word abstract required (unstructured, single paragraph) — noticeably shorter than typical field/journal abstracts. No JEL codes or keywords observed on published abstract pages.

### Citations
Author-date system, e.g., "(Persson 1993, p. 105)". Reference list format is prescriptive: author surnames in caps (e.g., "PERSSON, K. G. (1993)"), journal articles cite volume and page range, books cite place and publisher.

### Headings
No specific heading hierarchy prescribed in guidelines; follows FIELD_SKILL norm otherwise.

### Tables and Figures
Placed at the end of the manuscript (not embedded in text), with explicit in-text markers indicating intended placement — a production/formatting requirement, not a content convention.

### Explicit Prohibitions
No previously published or simultaneously-submitted work. English-language submissions only. Data, programs, and code must be deposited in the OPEN ICPSR repository with a Data Availability Statement at the end of the article before publication — a stricter, journal-specific replication mandate not covered in FIELD_SKILL.

### Text Conventions (mechanical, ABS-3 specific detail not in FIELD_SKILL)
American English spelling; foreign terms italicized (except proper names, quotations, common usage); double quotation marks for main quotes, single for nested; block quotes for 50+ words, indented, no quotation marks; numbers one through ten spelled out, numerals thereafter; standard contractions for large numbers (e.g., "m" for million).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
ENFORCED: End-of-manuscript placement of footnotes and tables/figures (a submission-formatting requirement) and the OPEN ICPSR data-deposit mandate are both journal-specific and stricter than anything in FIELD_SKILL, which does not specify data-sharing requirements at all.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
RELAXATION (ABS 3): The 100-word abstract is markedly shorter and more compressed than is typical for empirically dense economic history papers; authors must compress puzzle, data, method, and finding into a single short paragraph, more so than FIELD_SKILL's general "compact, non-technical summary" description implies. All spot-checked abstracts (2024-2026) were quantitative/econometric economic history (grain futures volatility, age-heaping, migration, wage/wealth series, hyperinflation, enclosures) — consistent with FIELD_SKILL's note that quantitative economic history converges toward applied-economics norms; no purely narrative/archival business-history pieces were observed in this sample, suggesting this journal's practice skews more quantitative than the FIELD_SKILL baseline as a whole.

## Common Pitfalls
- Writing an abstract in the 150-250 word range typical of many economics journals; EREH wants roughly 100 words.
- Embedding tables/figures inline in the submitted manuscript instead of collecting them at the end with placement markers.
- Omitting the Data Availability Statement and OPEN ICPSR deposit — accepted articles will not be published without this.
- Using numeric reference styles (e.g., bracketed numbers) instead of the required author-date in-text citation and capitalized-surname reference-list format.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

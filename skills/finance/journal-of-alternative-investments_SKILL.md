# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Alternative Investments

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** FINANCE
- **Publisher:** Portfolio Management Research (PMR), formerly Institutional Investor Journals; official journal of the Chartered Alternative Investment Analyst Association (CAIA)
- **Scope:** Practitioner-oriented research on non-traditional investments — hedge funds, private equity, managed futures, commodities, real estate, private credit, and related alternative asset classes — aimed at institutional portfolio managers.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://jai.pm-research.com/authors (full guidelines rendered here; a legacy mirror at hwmaint.jai.pm-research.com/misc/ifora.dtl is an empty stub)
- **Field baseline:** finance-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 (abstracts/metadata only, full text paywalled) — "Prolonged Private Equity Holding Periods: Six Years Is the New Normal" (Joenväärä, Mäkiaho, Torstila, 2022, vol. 25(1):65); "Hedge Fund Alpha: Cycle or Sunset?" (Sullivan, 2021, vol. 23(3):55); "Persistence in Hedge Fund Performance" (Kat and Menexe, 2003, vol. 5(4):66); "Asset Based Style Analysis for Equity Strategies" (2007, vol. 10(1):10); "A Performance Update—Hedge Funds versus Hedged Mutual Funds" (2020, vol. 23(2):35); "Hedge Fund Alpha: What about Drawdowns?" (Sullivan, 2021 early view, jai.2021.1.143). All accessed via search-engine-extracted abstracts, ResearchGate/Aalto repository mirrors, and publisher metadata pages — no full-text access available (institutional login required at pm-research.com; hwmaint mirror unpopulated).

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Three-part front matter, distinct from the field default: (1) first page = title, date, author names/affiliations/contact info, corresponding-author designation; (2) second page = title, date, abstract, keywords, JEL codes; (3) third page onward = main text starting directly with introductory material (no heading before it), numbered sections, exhibits embedded in text, conclusion, footnotes, then references. Three "key takeaways" highlighting core findings are a required, separate deliverable (not necessarily printed inline, but mandated at submission) — this has no analogue in FIELD_SKILL.

### Length
Target ~4,000 words excluding abstract, exhibits, and references. Articles under 2,500 or over 7,500 words are "rarely accepted." This is far shorter than typical ABS 4*/4 finance-journal length norms.

### Abstract
Target 160 words. Must be non-technical, contain no references, and define acronyms on first use. Appears only on the open (unrestricted) portion of the site, not immediately before the main text as in most journals.

### Citations
Author-date format per Chicago Manual of Style, 17th edition, Ch. 15 — not the economics/finance-typical but unspecified author-year style; CMS is an explicit, named standard here.

### Headings
Exactly three permitted heading levels, each with a specific typographic treatment: Level 1 = bold uppercase; Level 2 = indented and bold; Level 3 = bold inline paragraph heading. No heading precedes the introductory material.

### Tables and Figures
All tabular and graphical material is called "Exhibit" (e.g., "Exhibit 1"), never "Table" or "Figure" — numbered consecutively regardless of type. Must be submitted as native Word objects (tables as Word tables, charts as embeddable Word chart objects), not as pictures or Excel files, so the publisher can edit underlying data. Color is encouraged but must remain legible in black-and-white print. Equations are numbered consecutively in the right margin, no trailing punctuation.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Strict post-publication distribution ban unusual for an academic journal: after acceptance, authors must remove all prior versions from SSRN/ArXiv/any website, and after publication may not post to SSRN or ResearchGate, distribute at conferences, include in course packs, or allow book-chapter reprinting. Derivative works require at least one-third new content. AI use is permitted only "for enhancing readability and language," not for substantive content generation — an explicit disclosure requirement not seen in FIELD_SKILL.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
ENFORCED delta, strongly stated in guidelines: audience is investment professionals, not academics. Guidelines explicitly state manuscripts "written primarily for an academic audience will be rejected at the preliminary review," and instruct authors to "write in plain English, minimizing technical jargon, and emphasizing clarity and brevity." This is a substantial relaxation/redirection from the FIELD_SKILL norm of high technical density and freely used domain jargon (identification, fixed effects, etc.) — RELAXATION, consistent with ABS 2 tier and the journal's stated practitioner mission rather than a contradiction of field norms for top-tier work.

### Structural Deviations
ENFORCED: the introduction is explicitly required to function as a second, expanded abstract — "strong introductory paragraphs that serve as functional abstracts" plus a roadmap paragraph — and titles are capped at 12 words with subject terms placed early. FIELD_SKILL describes a similar roadmap convention but does not require the introduction to duplicate abstract content, nor cap title length.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
ENFORCED: mandatory "three key takeaways" as a distinct submission element. GUIDELINE ONLY (not independently confirmed in the accessible abstracts, which predate or don't display this newer requirement): could not verify presence of takeaways boxes in the sampled older articles (2003–2022), as only abstract-level content was accessible; this appears to be a more recently formalized PMR-wide requirement.

Spot-checked abstracts (Sullivan 2021; Joenväärä et al. 2022) preview concrete numeric findings in the abstract itself (e.g., "average holding period lengthened to 5.8 years," "alpha... down from 3.4 percent... to -1.0 percent"), consistent with FIELD_SKILL's norm of previewing quantitative magnitudes — FIELD DEFAULT, not a delta.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- Citation density by section: FIELD DEFAULT
- Economic-vs-statistical significance emphasis: FIELD DEFAULT
- Conclusion structure and brevity: FIELD DEFAULT
- Hedging/epistemic caution calibration: FIELD DEFAULT (though overall technical hedging is naturally lighter given plain-English mandate — see Voice and Register above)
- Treatment of prior literature (non-adversarial framing): FIELD DEFAULT

## Common Pitfalls
- Writing in a register suited for ABS 4*/4 academic finance journals (heavy jargon, dense technical prose) — this journal will desk-reject on preliminary review for being "too academic."
- Using "Table"/"Figure" labels instead of "Exhibit," or submitting charts as static images instead of editable Word objects.
- Submitting an abstract that reads as an academic summary rather than the required ~160-word, reference-free, non-technical version, or omitting the mandatory three key takeaways.
- Posting the manuscript to SSRN/ResearchGate after acceptance — explicitly prohibited and distinct from most journals' post-publication norms.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

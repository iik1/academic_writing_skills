# Writing Style Skill: International Journal of Production Economics

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** OPS&TECH
- **Publisher:** Elsevier B.V. (Netherlands)
- **Discipline / Scope:** Interdisciplinary — the interface between engineering and management. Covers all aspects of manufacturing and process industries and production in general, including the full product life cycle (research, design, development, test, launch, disposal) and the material flow cycle (supply, production, distribution). Key thematic areas: supply chain management, inventory theory, scheduling, pricing, production planning, digital transformation in operations, sustainability in operations, and operations economics. ## Author Guidelines (Hard Rules)

> These are the journal's stated requirements. They take precedence over observed patterns when there is a conflict.

### Manuscript Structure
No single prescribed section sequence is mandated by the guidelines. Articles should be divided into clearly defined and numbered sections. Subsections are numbered 1.1, 1.2, 1.1.1, 1.1.2, etc. Subsections may have a brief heading, which should appear on its own line. The abstract is not included within section numbering. Common practice (not a hard rule): empirical papers follow Introduction → Literature Review → Methodology → Results → Discussion → Conclusions; analytical papers follow Introduction → Model Setup → Analysis → Discussion → Conclusions.

### Length Requirements
Manuscripts should not normally exceed approximately 15 printed journal pages (approximately 10,000 words). Review papers may be permitted to exceed this limit. No per-section word limits are stated in the guidelines. The 10,000-word norm is widely reported and consistent with published article lengths.

### Abstract
- Format: unstructured narrative (no labeled fields such as Background, Methods, Results)
- Word limit: maximum 250 words
- Content: briefly state purpose, principal results, and major conclusions
- Must be self-contained: avoid references; if essential, cite author(s) and year only
- Avoid non-standard or uncommon abbreviations; define any used at first mention
- No section numbering applied to the abstract

### Citations and References
- Style: author-date (Harvard/APA-like) in-text citations
- Single author: (Surname, Year) e.g., (Holt, 2012)
- Two authors: (Surname1 and Surname2, Year)
- Three or more authors: (Surname1 et al., Year)
- Multiple citations: separated by semicolons — (Holt, 2012; Mahowald and Schenck, 2005)
- Reference list format for journal articles: Surname, Initials., Year. Article title. Journal Name Volume, Page range.
- Example: Holt, R., 2012. US election: Politicians should think like scientists. Nature 489, 493–494.
- Web references include URL and access date
- Note: some reference manager templates label this journal as "elsarticle-num" (numbered), but in-text format is author-year. Use author-year.
- Paraphrase is the norm; direct quotation is rare

### Headings
- Sections: numbered (1, 2, 3, ...)
- Subsections: numbered hierarchically (1.1, 1.2, 1.1.1, 1.1.2, ...)
- Three levels of heading (1 / 1.1 / 1.1.1) is standard practice; more levels are uncommon
- Headings appear on their own line; kept brief and descriptive
- Abstract is not numbered

### Tables and Figures
- Follow standard Elsevier formatting conventions
- Figures must be submitted in high resolution; preferred file types: TIFF, EPS, PDF, or MS Office formats
- Graphical abstract (optional but encouraged): minimum 531 x 1328 pixels (h x w), readable at 5 x 13 cm at 96 dpi
- Generative AI must not be used to create or alter figures, images, or graphical abstracts; any AI-assisted artwork requires explicit disclosure in the methods section (including model name, version, and manufacturer)
- All figures and tables must be cited in order in the text

### Language and Tone Requirements
- English (American or British, applied consistently throughout)
- Inclusive language required: avoid bias based on age, gender, race, ethnicity, culture, sexual orientation, or disability; use gender-neutral constructions (plural nouns preferred as default)
- Language should be precise, formal, and academically appropriate
- Elsevier recommends non-native English speakers consider language editing services before submission
- Economic and financial consequences of the studied phenomenon should be traceable in the analysis — this is a core journal expectation, not merely a stylistic preference

### Prohibitions
- AI tools may not be listed as authors
- If AI tools were used in manuscript preparation (text writing, editing, language polishing), an explicit AI disclosure statement must appear before the references section, naming the tool and its version
- Generative AI must not produce or manipulate figures or images (except when the AI tool is itself the subject of research, in which case its use must be fully documented in the methods section)
- Do not submit simultaneously to another journal
- Do not include author names, affiliations, or other identifying information in the manuscript text or figures at submission (double-blind peer review)
- Self-citations that identify the authors must be removed or anonymized at submission
- Systematic reviews that merely describe publication patterns without generating novel critique are unlikely to be sent for review

## Observed Writing Patterns

### Voice and Person
First-person plural ("we") is standard and widely used across both empirical and analytical papers. Passive voice is used selectively, particularly in methods sections when describing procedures ("data were collected," "models were estimated"), but active voice dominates in introductions, contributions statements, and discussions. First-person singular ("I") is rare — most IJPE papers are co-authored and "we" is the assumed default. Impersonal constructions ("this paper," "this study," "the present study") appear as alternatives to "we" in framing sentences, particularly at the start of the introduction and abstract.

### Sentence and Paragraph Style
- Sentences are typically moderate in length (20–35 words), mixing shorter declarative sentences with longer complex sentences
- Paragraphs are medium length (4–8 sentences), with clear topic sentences
- Analytical and modelling papers tend toward more compact, formally structured prose; empirical papers have longer discursive passages
- Each paragraph advances a single main point; transitions are explicit
- Mathematical notation is integrated inline for modelling papers; equations are numbered and referenced in the text
- Numbered lists of contributions are commonly used in the introduction

### Hedging and Epistemic Stance
Hedging is present but moderate — consistent with a field that values both rigor and practical applicability. Common hedging markers:
- "suggest that," "indicate that," "appear to," "may," "tend to"
- "the results suggest," "our findings indicate," "evidence supports"
- Causal language is used where relationships are established empirically or analytically, but authors hedge when generalizing beyond the sample or model
- Analytical papers are somewhat less hedged when presenting proofs or propositions; empirical papers hedge more in the discussion and when extrapolating from specific samples

### Transitional and Cohesive Phrases
- Section openers: "Building on this foundation, ..."; "To address this gap, ..."; "The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: ..."
- Literature-to-gap transitions: "However, ..."; "Despite this progress, ..."; "Nevertheless, limited attention has been paid to ..."; "To the best of our knowledge, ..."
- Results transitions: "The findings reveal that ..."; "Consistent with our hypotheses, ..."; "Contrary to expectations, ..."
- Discussion transitions: "These results are consistent with ..."; "This finding extends ..."; "This suggests that ..."
- Conclusion openers: "This study examined ..."; "The primary contribution of this work is ..."; "Drawing on ..., this paper ..."

### Formality and Register
High formal register throughout. No colloquialisms. Technical and domain-specific terminology used precisely and consistently. Acronyms defined at first mention in both the abstract and the body text (separately). Field-specific vocabulary (supply chain, production economics, operations management, game theory, SEM, optimization) used with precision and without over-explanation. Interdisciplinary reach means authors often briefly define concepts that straddle engineering and management boundaries.

### Guideline vs. Practice Discrepancies
1. **Section structure:** The guidelines say no prescribed section sequence, but in practice empirical papers follow Introduction → Literature Review / Theoretical Background → Methodology → Results → Discussion → Conclusions almost universally; analytical papers follow Introduction → Model Setup → Analysis → Managerial Insights / Discussion → Conclusions.
2. **Word limit:** The guidelines list no formal limit in current text, but the approximately 10,000-word / 15-page norm is real and consistently observed.
3. **Citation style label:** Some reference manager templates call this "elsarticle-num" (numbered), but actual in-text citations are author-year (Harvard). Use author-year.
4. **Analytical modelling paper prevalence:** Empirical papers are not the modal article type. Analytical and game-theoretic modelling papers are equally if not more prevalent in any given issue.

## Section-by-Section Writing Guide

### Abstract
- Length: 150–250 words (aim well under 250)
- Format: single block paragraph, no headings or bullets
- Implicit structure: (1) problem context and motivation, 1–2 sentences; (2) objective or research question, 1 sentence; (3) method / data / approach, 1–2 sentences; (4) key findings, 2–3 sentences; (5) contribution / implication, 1 sentence
- Typical opening: "This paper examines ..."; "This study investigates ..."; "We develop a model to ..."; "We propose a framework for ..."
- Use "we" freely in the abstract
- Avoid references, unexplained acronyms, and jargon that non-specialist readers might not parse
- Do not repeat the title verbatim as the first sentence

### Introduction
- Length: approximately 800–1,500 words
- Follows CARS (Create a Research Space) logic:
  - Move 1 — Establish territory: situate the phenomenon in broader industrial or managerial context; cite recent high-impact work to show the field is active
  - Move 2 — Establish niche: identify a gap or limitation using contrast markers ("However, ..."; "Despite ..."; "To our knowledge, ..."); gap should be specific, not vague
  - Move 3 — Occupy niche: state research objective, approach, and contributions explicitly
- Research objectives stated explicitly, often in a dedicated paragraph or as a numbered list
- Contributions (theoretical and/or practical) are listed explicitly near the end of the introduction, sometimes as bullet points
- End of introduction: brief paper organization paragraph ("The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: Section 2 reviews ...")
- Citation density: high — 1–3 citations per paragraph is standard
- Tone: formal but accessible; avoid overstating the importance of the problem

### Methods
- Length: approximately 600–1,200 words for empirical papers; longer for analytical models
- Tense: past tense for data collection and procedures ("data were collected," "we distributed questionnaires"); present tense for descriptions of the model or analytical framework ("the model assumes," "the optimization problem is formulated as")
- Subsections typically include: research design / model setup, data collection or data source description, sample characteristics (empirical), variable operationalization or model parameters, estimation method or solution algorithm
- Data sources cited with specific identifiers (database names, survey periods, sample sizes)
- For SEM/survey papers: sample size and response rate reported; common method bias addressed; reliability and validity statistics (Cronbach's alpha, AVE, CR) reported
- For analytical papers: assumptions stated explicitly; model notation defined clearly; propositions or lemmas numbered
- Level of procedural detail is high — sufficient for replication or verification

### Results
- Length: approximately 600–1,200 words
- Tense: past tense for what was found ("results showed," "the coefficient was significant")
- Statistics reported inline: coefficient value, standard error or t-statistic, p-value or confidence interval, e.g., "beta = 0.43, p < 0.01"
- Tables and figures used extensively to present model fit, parameter estimates, or optimization outcomes; not repeated verbatim in prose
- Text highlights key findings and directs reader to tables and figures
- Interpretation: results sections typically report what was found with minimal interpretation; deeper interpretation is reserved for the Discussion section or a combined Results and Discussion section
- For analytical papers: propositions are proven or numerically verified; sensitivity analyses report how results change with parameter values

### Discussion
- When present as a separate section: approximately 600–1,000 words
- Connects results back to prior literature ("consistent with X, we find that ..."; "in contrast to Y, our findings suggest ...")
- Explains unexpected or nuanced results
- Theoretical implications: how findings extend or challenge existing theory
- Managerial and practical implications: explicitly expected in IJPE — managers, practitioners, and policy implications are a core requirement
- Limitations: presented honestly but compactly, typically 2–4 specific limitations
- Future research directions: brief (3–5 suggestions), phrased as open questions ("future research could examine ..."; "future work should explore ...")

### Conclusion
- Length: approximately 400–700 words
- Summarizes main findings concisely (no new results introduced)
- Restates contributions, often mirroring the contribution list in the introduction
- Ends with a forward-looking statement on implications or future directions
- Typical opener: "This study examined ..."; "Drawing on ..., this paper developed ..."; "The purpose of this paper was to ..."
- Does not reproduce abstract verbatim; provides synthesis, not summary
- Limitations and future research may be combined here if no separate Discussion section exists

### References
- Author-date format (Harvard)
- Listed alphabetically by first author surname
- Full article titles included
- Journal name spelled out in full (no abbreviations)
- DOI included where available
- Example journal article: Smith, J., Jones, A., 2023. Supply chain resilience under disruption: A game-theoretic analysis. International Journal of Production Economics 265, 108961. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijpe.2023.108961

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Missing or malformed Highlights.** IJPE requires 3–5 research highlights at submission (Elsevier standard: each bullet must be no longer than 85 characters including spaces). Omitting highlights or exceeding the character limit is likely to trigger a desk-review flag. Write highlights as concise noun phrases summarizing what is new and what was found.
2. **Abstract over 250 words or in a structured format.** The 250-word cap is enforced. Do not use labeled fields (Background:, Methods:, Results:). A single continuous paragraph is required. This catches authors migrating from journals that require structured abstracts.
3. **Insufficient manuscript anonymization for double-blind review.** All author names, affiliations, grant identifiers tied to named institutions, and self-citations phrased as "in our previous study" must be removed or replaced before submission. Failure at this step results in administrative rejection.
4. **Undisclosed or prohibited AI use.** If any AI tool (including large language models for writing assistance or language polishing) was used in preparing the manuscript, a brief AI disclosure statement is mandatory before the references section. AI-generated or AI-manipulated figures are prohibited unless the AI tool itself is the research subject.
5. **Calibrating to the wrong modal article type.** IJPE is unusual in OPS&TECH for the very high prevalence of analytical and game-theoretic modelling papers alongside empirical survey work. Authors should not assume the empirical SEM paper is the reference format. Modelling papers use formal propositions, proofs, and sensitivity analysis; empirical papers should not over-formalize to mimic that style.
6. **Vague or underdeveloped contribution statement.** The guidelines explicitly require an "essential novel scientific contribution" and that economic and financial consequences be traceable in the analysis. A paper without an explicit contribution list in the introduction and without managerial or practical implications will struggle in review.

## Representative Phrase Bank

### Introduction phrases
- **Gap statement:**
  - "However, little is known about how ..."
  - "To the best of our knowledge, no prior study has simultaneously examined ..."
  - "Despite growing interest in X, the relationship between X and Y remains poorly understood."
  - "Prior research has predominantly focused on ..., leaving the role of ... largely unexplored."
  - "While extant literature has addressed ..., scholars have paid limited attention to ..."
- **Objective statement:**
  - "This paper examines the effect of ... on ... using a sample of ..."
  - "We develop an analytical model to characterize optimal ... decisions under ..."
  - "The purpose of this study is to investigate how ... moderates the relationship between ... and ..."
  - "This paper aims to fill this gap by proposing a framework that ..."
  - "Drawing on [theory], this study investigates ..."

### Methods phrases
- **Procedure description:**
  - "We collected data via a structured questionnaire administered to ... in ..."
  - "The model is formulated as a mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) problem, solved using ..."
  - "We estimated the model using partial least squares structural equation modelling (PLS-SEM)."
  - "The sample consists of ... firms drawn from ..., covering the period ... to ..."
  - "To test the robustness of our findings, we conducted ... additional analyses."
- **Data source statement:**
  - "Data were obtained from the [Database Name] database for the period [year–year]."
  - "Primary data were collected through a survey, yielding ... usable responses (response rate: ...%)."
  - "Secondary data on ... were sourced from [official source] and matched with ..."

### Results phrases
- **Statistic inline:**
  - "The coefficient for ... is positive and significant (beta = 0.43, SE = 0.09, p < 0.01), supporting H1."
  - "The optimization model yields a cost reduction of ...% compared to the benchmark."
  - "Model fit statistics indicate acceptable fit: CFI = 0.96, RMSEA = 0.05, SRMR = 0.06."
  - "Proposition 1 establishes that the optimal wholesale price increases with ..."
- **Figure reference:**
  - "Fig. 2 illustrates the relationship between ... and ... across different parameter values."
  - "As shown in Table 3, the standardized path coefficients confirm ..."
  - "The sensitivity analysis results, presented in Fig. 3, reveal that ..."

### Discussion phrases
- **Connecting to literature:**
  - "These findings are consistent with [Author, Year], who argue that ..."
  - "This result extends the work of [Author, Year] by demonstrating that ... even when ..."
  - "In contrast to [Author, Year], we find that ..., which may be explained by ..."
  - "Our results lend support to [theory name], suggesting that ..."
- **Limitation framing:**
  - "This study is not without limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; future research employing longitudinal data would strengthen these conclusions."
  - "The sample is limited to ... industry in ..., which may affect the generalizability of our findings to other contexts."
  - "Our model assumes ..., which may not hold in all practical settings."

### Conclusion phrases
- **Summary opener:**
  - "This study examined the relationship between ... and ..., using a sample of ... and employing ..."
  - "Drawing on [theory], this paper developed an analytical model to characterize ..."
  - "The primary objective of this research was to investigate ... We find that ..."
- **Future direction:**
  - "Future research could extend this work by examining ... in the context of ..."
  - "We encourage scholars to explore the moderating role of ... using longitudinal designs."
  - "An important avenue for future research is to incorporate ... dynamics into the model."

### Core principle and scope

Improve the clarity and voice of AI-assisted *academic* writing while keeping the precise, evidence-bound voice that scholarship requires and matching the author's own style. Preserve every number, result, and citation. This is not a tool for evading AI-use disclosure.

**Scope.** Editing or reviewing academic prose: paper sections, abstracts, rebuttals, related work, and **funding proposals** (NSF Project Summary/Description, NIH Specific Aims, ERC, NFR, fellowship and foundation proposals; see Layer 6). **Not** for blogs, marketing, or personal essays, and **never** inject opinion, humor, or first-person "personality" into a manuscript. For technical writing, neutral and precise *is* the human voice. One caveat for proposals: their register is different from a paper's, since they are sold on vision and feasibility, so the ambition language a paper would trim is appropriate there; apply Layer 6, not the paper layers' stricter trimming, to vision statements.

**Core principle.** Academic writing already has a correct human voice: neutral, precise, third-person plural ("we"), every claim tied to its evidence. The job is to (1) strip the AI *tells* without casualizing, and (2) enforce the discipline a general humanizer misses: **every claim earns its number, figure, or citation, and no verb is stronger than its evidence.**

### Process

1. **Read** the manuscript and any author writing sample; note the document type (paper vs. funding proposal) and the target venue or funding agency. For proposals, also apply Layer 6 and preserve appropriate vision.
2. **Audit** (do not edit yet): list each detected pattern with its location and proposed fix, and each empirical claim's evidence status.
3. **Rewrite**: same structure and content, all claims and citations preserved, tells removed, over-claims matched to evidence, legitimate hedging kept.
4. **Report**: cleaned text plus a short change log (patterns removed, claims softened or given evidence pointers, voice notes). Cover everything the original covered: if it had five paragraphs, so does the rewrite. See Output below.

### Layer 1: General AI tells

Scan for and fix these general patterns, subject to the academic exceptions in Layer 3. Connectives, vague quantities, formulaic openers, emphasis openers, hedging, and novelty claims are covered in Layer 2.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** An occasional triple is fine ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad in every paragraph is a tell, and so is a triple added as padding. Vary the structure.
3. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, inflated significance ("marking a pivotal moment"), and "dramatically" as an intensifier. Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance. For "significantly," see 2.1.
4. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "testament," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "underscore," "pivotal," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
5. **No promotional or figurative language.** Avoid "rich," "vibrant," "groundbreaking," and similar praise or imagery for any subject. Describe it plainly. (For praise of your own contribution, see 2.4.)
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits": these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **No vague attributions.** Avoid "experts argue" and similar attributions with no citation attached. Cite the source or cut the attribution.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No padding verb phrases or copula avoidance.** Replace "serves to," "serves as," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents ("serves as" -> "is").
10. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end. It should not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
11. **No elegant variation.** Do not cycle synonyms for one referent. Pick one term and keep it.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way. Split overlong, clause-stacked sentences (2.11).
13. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above": residue from copy-pasted chat output.
14. **No em dashes.** Remove em dashes (the long dash, U+2014) entirely. Recast with a period, comma, colon, or parentheses, or split into separate sentences, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
15. **No antithesis padding or negative parallelisms.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") and "not just X, but Y" unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
16. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
17. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot." State the point in the sentence itself. Structural run-in labels in a funding proposal (6.4) are not flourish labels.
18. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

**Before:** *Additionally, an enduring testament to the method's value is its ability to delve into intricate dependencies, showcasing a seamless integration that underscores its pivotal role.*
**After:** *The method also captures higher-order dependencies, which the baselines miss (Table 2).*

### Layer 2: Academic AI tells (remove or fix)

#### 2.1 Over-claiming verbs
Empirical work *shows* and *provides evidence*; it does not *prove* or *demonstrate* universal truths.
**Watch:** demonstrate, prove, establish, confirm, guarantee. Use "significantly" only for a stated statistical test, with the test and its result; never as an intensifier.
**Before:** *We prove that our method significantly outperforms all prior approaches.*
**After:** *Our method improves held-out accuracy by 4--7 points over the strongest prior approach (Table 3); the gain is significant at p < 0.01 by a paired test.*

#### 2.2 Significance hype
**Watch:** paves the way for, a crucial/pivotal step toward, has the potential to revolutionize, opens new avenues, sheds light on, of paramount importance, bridges the gap.
**Before:** *This work paves the way for a new paradigm and sheds light on a problem of paramount importance.*
**After:** *This work addresses one failure mode of prior methods: error accumulation under long-horizon rollout (Section 4).*

#### 2.3 Empty intensifiers and vague quantities
**Watch:** extensive/comprehensive/thorough experiments, a wide range of, a range of, a number of, several key, numerous, various, "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
**Before:** *We conduct extensive experiments on a wide range of datasets.*
**After:** *We evaluate on three datasets (ImageNet, CIFAR-100, and iNaturalist).*

#### 2.4 Novelty padding and self-praise
Never characterize your own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking," and drop "to the best of our knowledge" and "for the first time." Say what the work addresses that prior work does not, and let the reader judge novelty. This holds in funding proposals too: Layer 6 keeps vision language, not self-labels.
**Before:** *We propose a novel framework and, to the best of our knowledge, are the first to study this.*
**After:** *We study online calibration under delayed labels, which prior calibration work (offline) does not address.*

#### 2.5 Formulaic and throat-clearing openers
**Watch:** "In recent years, X has attracted increasing attention"; "In recent years, there has been growing interest in..."; "With the rapid development of..."; "With the advent of..."; "In today's rapidly evolving..."; "Despite recent advances,...". Delete and start with the claim.
**Before:** *In recent years, tabular deep learning has attracted increasing attention.*
**After:** *Tabular deep learning has a structural limitation: most models discard feature-type metadata and must relearn it from data.*

#### 2.6 Connective overuse
Do not start a sentence with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "In particular," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said," whether or not the previous sentence did. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it; let logic carry.
**Before:** *Moreover, the method is fast. Furthermore, it is simple. Additionally, it scales.*
**After:** *The method is fast and simple, and it scales to one million rows (Section 5).*

#### 2.7 Contribution-list cliches
Each contribution names a *specific* result, not a restatement of the abstract.
**Before:** *Our contributions are: (1) a novel method; (2) extensive experiments; (3) strong results.*
**After:** *We (1) introduce a metadata-aware encoder that reaches 0.91 AUROC vs 0.86 for the strongest baseline; (2) show it stays within 2 points under 20% label noise where the baseline drops 9; (3) release the benchmark.*

#### 2.8 Citation dumping
Cite the one or two works that matter and say why, not a bracketed list.
**Before:** *Many methods exist [3, 7, 9, 12, 15].*
**After:** *The closest prior method is TabNet [7], which encodes all features jointly; we instead condition on feature-type metadata.*

#### 2.9 Hedging: cut empty hedges, keep evidence-tied ones
Cut hedges that carry no specific, defensible qualification: "somewhat," "relatively," "fairly," "quite," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially." Quantify or cut. Keep evidence-tied hedging, which is correct and required: "suggests," "is consistent with," "we hypothesize that," "may indicate," "appears to" when the claim is genuinely uncertain. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real, and specify its source. Never upgrade a calibrated verb.
**Before:** *Performance is somewhat better and relatively robust.*
**After:** *Accuracy is 3 points higher and varies by less than 1 point across five seeds.*
*Wrong fix:* turning *"the results suggest X"* into *"the results prove X"*: this manufactures over-claiming. Keep the calibrated verb.

#### 2.10 Boilerplate emphasis and interpretive openers
**Watch:** "It is worth noting that", "It should be emphasized that", "Notably,", "Importantly,", "Interestingly,", "Surprisingly,", "Strikingly,". If it matters, the sentence shows it; if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
**Before:** *It is worth noting that, importantly, the gain holds across scenarios.*
**After:** *The gain holds across all three scenarios (Table 4).*

#### 2.11 Overlong, clause-stacked sentences
AI favors long sentences that chain three or four clauses with commas and "which", "that", "while", "with". Split them: one idea per sentence, and cut subordinate clauses that carry no weight. **Watch:** sentences past ~30 words, or with 3+ subordinate clauses.
**Before:** *Existing methods, though promising, are largely empirical, with unclear principles underpinning their behavior, which limits their reliability and further progress.*
**After:** *Existing methods stay empirical. Their principles are unclear, which limits reliability and progress.*

### Layer 3: Preserve these (do NOT over-correct)

A general humanizer flattens legitimate scholarly constructs. Keep them.

- **Evidence-tied hedging is correct and required** (2.9). Keep the calibrated verb when the claim is genuinely uncertain.
- **Passive voice** is fine when the actor is irrelevant: *"Samples were normalized to total protein."*
- **First-person plural "we"** is standard; do not rewrite to avoid it.
- **Semicolons and an occasional triple** are fine in moderation (Layer 1, item 2). Em dashes are the exception: remove them entirely (Layer 1, item 14), recasting with commas, colons, parentheses, or separate sentences.
- **Formal definitions, named methods/metrics, technical terms, equations, and symbols** stay verbatim.
- **Never invent, drop, or alter a number, equation, or citation.** Same content; preserve every cite key.

### Layer 4: Claim-evidence discipline

For every empirical claim, check (a) is it backed by a number, figure, table, or citation in the text, and (b) does the verb match the strength of that evidence?

- **Unbacked claim -> add the evidence pointer or soften.**
  *Before:* *Our method is more robust.*  *After:* *Our method's accuracy drops by 2 points under distribution shift, versus 11 points for the baseline (Figure 3).*
- **Verb stronger than evidence -> downgrade.**
  *Before:* *This demonstrates that our method is universally superior.*
  *After:* *On these three datasets, our method matches or exceeds the strongest baseline (Table 2).*
- **Vague magnitude -> a number or RANGE, attributed.**
  *Before:* *a large improvement.*  *After:* *a 2--6% improvement in balanced accuracy over the strongest baseline.*
  Prefer ranges (e.g., "2--6%") over single averaged values unless the averaging method is stated, and attribute each number to its method, metric, and baseline. When comparing, lead with the comparison against the strongest competitor, not the trivial baseline.

### Layer 5: Voice and venue matching

If the author supplies prior papers, read a sample first and note sentence rhythm, connective habits, level and placement of hedging, how they open sections, notation, and recurring phrasings, then match them. Match the venue's register too: this skill's own voice and register guidance above defines it (in a field skill, "Voice, Person, and Register"; in a journal skill, the journal profile, hard constraints, and observed deltas), and that guidance wins over any generic register default. Absent a sample, default to clean, precise, venue-appropriate prose, not the casual, opinionated voice of a general-purpose humanizer.

### Layer 6: Funding-proposal mode (NSF, NIH)

Apply this layer only to funding proposals. The structure notes below cover NSF and NIH; the same discipline applies to other funders (e.g., ERC, NFR). In a paper, nothing in this layer relaxes the rules above.

A proposal is not a paper. It is sold on **vision plus feasibility**, not on finished results, and reviewers score it. The register shift matters: ambition language that the paper layers would trim ("long-term goal", "pioneer", "transformative", "establish a foundation") is *appropriate and expected* here, provided a credible plan and evidence back it. So in proposal mode, **do not flatten the vision**; enforce a different discipline instead: **claim <-> feasibility**.

#### 6.1 Know the structure; the score lives in the first pages
Reviewers form a score from the opening, then skim the rest to confirm it. Put most editing effort there.
- **NSF.** A one-page **Project Summary** with the three review-criteria heads spelled out: **Overview**, **Intellectual Merit**, **Broader Impacts**, each self-contained. The Project Description then opens with **long-term vision -> this proposal's goal -> the gap -> the specific thrusts/aims -> the payoff**, ideally within the first 1--2 pages, with one overview figure. Broader Impacts must be substantive and integrated, never an afterthought.
- **NIH (R01).** The **Specific Aims page is the whole proposal in one page**, and is the most-read, most-decisive page. Standard arc: (1) opening: the problem, what is known, the **gap / critical need**; (2) the **long-term goal** and the **central hypothesis** with its rationale; (3) "**The objective of this application is...**" plus how the hypothesis was formed; (4) **2--3 Aims**, each a one-line goal + a phrase on approach + the expected outcome; (5) a **payoff** paragraph: what changes if it succeeds. Then **Significance, Innovation, Approach** as separately scored sections.

#### 6.2 First-3-pages primacy (edit these hardest)
By the end of page 1 (NIH Aims) or pages ~2--3 (NSF), the reader must already hold: the **hook** (why it matters, concretely), the **gap** (what is missing and the cost of the gap), the **central idea** (your approach in one sentence), the **aims/thrusts** (crisp and parallel), and the **payoff**. If any is missing or buried, fix that before touching later sections. A reviewer unconvinced by page 3 does not recover on page 10.

#### 6.3 Proposal-specific weak moves to fix
- **Vague importance.** *Watch:* "this is an important/timely problem", "X has many applications".
  **Before:** *Understanding this problem is critically important.*
  **After:** *Without bounds on how measurement noise propagates to diagnosis, clinical models are tuned by trial and error, the inefficiency this proposal removes.*
- **Method-as-aim** (an aim naming a technique instead of a question or outcome).
  **Before:** *Aim 2: Apply transfer learning to the dataset.*
  **After:** *Aim 2: Determine whether fusing wearable and lab signals improves early detection, and for which patient subgroups it helps or hurts.*
- **Dominoed aims** (Aim 2/3 collapse if Aim 1 fails; reviewers flag this as fragile). *Fix:* phrase aims as **parallel and independently valuable**; where one depends on another, state the fallback.
- **Ambition without feasibility.** Every bold claim needs a footing: preliminary data, a prior result/publication, a classical theorem you build on, or a collaborator/letter. *Fix:* attach the evidence beside the claim ("our preliminary result in Fig. X shows...", "building on a classical minimax lower bound...").
- **Boilerplate Broader Impacts / training plan.** *Watch:* "we will mentor students and disseminate via talks and papers." *Fix:* make it concrete, enumerated, and tied to the research: specific programs, named courses or tools, measurable outreach.
- **Hedged central hypothesis.** The Aims-page hypothesis is a falsifiable commitment, not "we will explore whether possibly...". Calibrated hedging belongs in the Approach's interpretation, not the central claim.

#### 6.4 Preserve and deploy (funded-proposal craft)
These read as strength; keep or add them rather than editing them out.
- **Vision/ambition framing**: a bold long-term goal up front, with this proposal as one principled step toward it.
- **Run-in lead-ins for scannability**: bold/italic **Goal:**, **Motivation:**, **Innovation:**, *Thrust/Aim N (one-line mission):*. Reviewers skim; visible structure earns time.
- **A concrete running example or protagonist** to make an abstract method vivid and consistent across aims.
- **Sharp challenge/aim statements posed as questions**: a crisp open question reads as a well-posed problem (a boxed or set-off question per aim works well).
- **Anchoring novel work in deep, named classical results** to signal rigor and lineage: a known inequality, capacity notion, or test that the new method generalizes.
- **Foreground the team's standing as feasibility evidence**: prior funded work, preliminary results, publications, collaborators, and demonstration partners belong *early*, as proof the plan is executable. A real track record is evidence, not boasting; place it where it de-risks the aims. *(Use only the PI's own real, supplied record; never invent funding, results, partners, or letters.)*

#### 6.5 Claim <-> feasibility (the proposal analog of Layer 4)
For every aim and promised outcome, check: is the ambition matched by a credible means, such as preliminary data, a prior method, a classical foundation, a collaborator, or staged de-risking? If yes, keep the ambitious verb. If no, attach the missing evidence or scale the claim to what the plan supports. Never invent preliminary results, prior funding, partners, or letters; if the support does not exist, flag the gap for the author rather than papering over it.

### Output

Return the cleaned text plus a short change report: patterns removed (by type), claims softened or given evidence pointers, and any voice/venue notes. Confirm that no number, equation, or citation was altered.

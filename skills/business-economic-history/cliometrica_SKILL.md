# Journal Writing Skill: Cliometrica

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** BUS HIST & ECON HIST
- **Publisher:** Springer Nature
- **Scope:** Journal of Historical Economics and Econometric History. Publishes quantitative approaches (cliometrics) applied to economic history across all periods and regions, combining historical narrative with econometric analysis and new data construction.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.springer.com/journal/11698/submission-guidelines
- **Field baseline:** business-economic-history-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 7 (Álvarez-Nogal & Prados de la Escosura 2025; Ljungberg 2025; Kisling & Molteni 2025; Huesler & Strobl 2025; Martínez-González 2025; Martinez-Galarraga & Prat 2025; Allen et al. 2025)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No explicit mandate for fixed section structure in accessible guidelines. Observed practice across sample: variations include traditional intro-lit review-methods-results-conclusion structure (particularly quantitative work) or narrative-analytical structure with thematic sections (particularly qualitative/historical work). Field baseline structure applies.

### Length
No explicit word or page limits found in accessible documentation. Springer standard practice for journals varies; no Cliometrica-specific constraint identified.

### Abstract
No explicit format requirement (structured vs. unstructured) found in accessible guidelines. Observed abstracts in sample are compact, single-paragraph, non-technical summaries of puzzle, methods, and contribution (2–3 sentences typical). Length not specified; observed range ~100–150 words in available examples.

### Citations
**ENFORCED:** Author-year format with parenthetical in-text citations required. Examples: (Eisenberg 2000); (Eisenberg 2000; Feldl and Bilham 2006); et al. format for three or more authors (Syvitski et al. 2005). Reference list at end following author-year bibliography style. This applies across all article types (quantitative and narrative-historical).

### Headings
No explicit convention found in accessible guidelines. Field baseline applies.

### Tables and Figures
**GUIDELINE ONLY:** All tables to be numbered using Arabic numerals and cited in text in consecutive numerical order. Each table must include a caption (title) explaining components; previously published material must be sourced in a reference at end of table caption. Figures should be supplied electronically; preferred formats EPS (vector graphics) or TIFF (halftones). Size specifications by journal format (84 mm or 174 mm width for Springer standard journals). This is stated in guidelines but not consistently enforced across sample articles.

### Supplementary Material
Supplementary materials should be submitted in standard formats (PDF for documents, CSV/XLSX for spreadsheets). Include article title, journal name, author names, affiliation, and corresponding author email in each file.

### Compliance Statements
**ENFORCED:** Authors must include a separate section "Compliance with Ethical Standards" covering funding sources, conflicts of interest (financial and non-financial), human subjects informed consent (if applicable), and animal welfare statements (if applicable).

### Explicit Prohibitions
No unusual prohibitions identified in accessible guidelines. Standard Springer journal policy: work must not have been published previously, must not be under consideration elsewhere, must be approved by all authors and institutional authorities.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Citation Density and Style
Cliometrica enforces strict author-year parenthetical citation format, whereas FIELD_SKILL notes that business and economic history use high citation density but does not mandate a specific style (field includes Chicago and author-date variants). Cliometrica's strict author-year requirement is more prescriptive than the field norm, which is more permissive about citation style variation.

### Quantitative vs. Narrative Balance
FIELD_SKILL correctly notes that quantitative economic history converges toward applied-economics structure (methods, data, results, robustness). Cliometrica sample shows this pattern strongly in quantitative articles (e.g., Álvarez-Nogal & Prados de la Escosura on subjective well-being uses econometric structure; Martínez-González on agricultural adaptation uses regression framework). However, narrative-historical articles in the journal (e.g., hurricane technology adoption piece) retain thematic, episodic organization closer to traditional history. No journal-specific delta here; FIELD_SKILL's bimodal description is accurate.

### Data and Methods Transparency
FIELD_SKILL requires procedural detail in methods sections. Cliometrica articles consistently provide explicit data sourcing (e.g., Ljungberg's 24-country consumer price index database construction; Kisling & Molteni's monthly bank lending data 1889–1913). No deviation from FIELD_SKILL; this is field-standard for cliometric work and is reinforced (not relaxed) by Cliometrica practice.

### Supplementary Materials and Data Sharing
FIELD_SKILL does not address supplementary materials or data availability. Cliometrica operates a Type 3 research data policy (social sciences). Authors are encouraged (not required) to share underlying data and materials; a data availability statement is mandatory. This is a FIELD DEFAULT (not addressed in FIELD_SKILL) but consistent with modern research standards.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Citation Style Mismatch:** Using Chicago style or a non-parenthetical author-date system. Cliometrica strictly requires author-year parenthetical citations; submissions with footnote-based or endnote-based citation systems will face revision requests.
2. **Omitting Compliance Statement:** Failing to include a "Compliance with Ethical Standards" section (funding, COI, human/animal subjects if applicable). This is a formal requirement and will delay processing.
3. **Inadequate Data Provenance:** Not providing explicit sourcing and construction details for datasets, especially for newly compiled historical databases. Cliometrica's readership expects transparency about data limitations and provenance.
4. **Unstructured Table/Figure Citations:** Presenting tables and figures without captions or source citations. All tables must be numbered, captioned, and sourced; figures require electronic submission in correct format.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

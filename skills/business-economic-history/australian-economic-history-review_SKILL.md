# Journal Writing Skill: Australian Economic History Review / Asia-Pacific Economic History Review

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** BUS HIST & ECON HIST
- **Publisher:** Wiley-Blackwell
- **Scope:** Social-scientific analyses of Pacific-Asian economic history (after 2023 name change); business history, economic history, industrial relations, demography of the Asia-Pacific region.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/page/journal/2832157x/homepage/forauthors.html (paywalled; guidelines extracted from search and secondary sources)
- **Field baseline:** business-economic-history-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 11 (Broadberry et al. 2026 on Japan; Kobayashi 2026 on Singapore-Britain; Takagi 2026 on Shanghai yen; Ashton 2026 on lascars; Fahey et al. 2025 on Cremorne; Frost et al. 2025 on Melbourne manufacturing; special issue on Manufacturing and Australian Cities 2025; various 2024-2025 articles on women in business history)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

### Structure
Organize manuscript into logical sections with **unnumbered subheadings in capital letters**. Typical sections include introductory section (background and purpose), data and methodology, results, and conclusions. Unlike FIELD_SKILL, heading capitalization is explicitly prescribed.

### Length
Research articles: **8,000–10,000 words** (including endnotes and tables; manuscripts exceeding 12,000 words are unlikely to be accepted). FIELD_SKILL does not specify word limits; this is ENFORCED for this journal.

### Abstract
**100 words or fewer**, non-structured format (not structured "background/methods/results"). ENFORCED and matches FIELD_SKILL convention.

### Citations
**Harvard (author-date) system.** Examples: Carson (2015) or (Carson, 2015); Hunter and Carmody (2015); MacDonald et al. (2002). Reference list: cite all author names when six or fewer; when seven or more, list first three followed by et al. **ENFORCED.**

### Mathematical Equations
All equations must be presented in a way that communicates with non-mathematical, non-quantitative economic historians and the intelligent lay person. Number sequentially with Arabic numerals, ranged right in parentheses. **ENFORCED** and noteworthy: this journal explicitly mandates accessibility of quantitative material to a broad, non-specialist audience.

### Manuscript Metadata
- **Data Availability Statement:** Required. **ENFORCED.**
- **Peer Review:** Double-blind (authors must anonymize their manuscript).
- **Submission Format:** Free Format accepted; references do not need pre-formatting (typesetter handles this).
- **Submission Platform:** ScholarOne Manuscripts (https://mc.manuscriptcentral.com/aehr).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Hard Word Limits
FIELD_SKILL is silent on word limits. This journal enforces **8,000–10,000 words for articles**, with strong discouragement for manuscripts exceeding 12,000 words. **ENFORCED.** This is a notable constraint not present in the field baseline.

### Heading Capitalization
FIELD_SKILL does not specify heading conventions. This journal requires **unnumbered subheadings in capital letters.** **ENFORCED.**

### Quantitative Accessibility Requirement
FIELD_SKILL notes that quantitative economic history converges toward applied-economics norms, but does NOT prescribe that quantitative material must be accessible to non-specialist historians. This journal **explicitly mandates** that equations and quantitative presentation be intelligible to historians and intelligent lay readers. **ENFORCED.** This is a notable delta: it prioritizes rhetorical clarity and accessibility over technical formalism, reflecting the journal's commitment to keeping quantitative work legible to narrative historians.

### Dimensions With No Delta
- Voice and person: FIELD DEFAULT (both first-person and impersonal framing observed in sample articles; no journal-specific norm stated)
- Citation density: FIELD DEFAULT (high throughout, per FIELD_SKILL)
- Hedging and epistemic caution: FIELD DEFAULT (moderate hedging observed in quantitative and narrative pieces; no journal-specific requirement)
- Results reporting structure: FIELD DEFAULT (quantitative articles follow econometric structure; narrative articles integrate evidence throughout; no single enforced norm)
- Discussion and conclusion expectations: FIELD DEFAULT (conclusions restate contribution and situate within historiography, per FIELD_SKILL)

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Exceeding 10,000 words.** Submissions with more than 12,000 words are unlikely to be accepted. Plan carefully to fit the journal's compact word budget while meeting FIELD_SKILL's expectation for detailed methods and substantial evidence.

2. **Opaque quantitative material.** If you include equations, tables, or statistical results, ensure they are explained in prose for historians and educated non-specialists. Avoid assuming readers are econometricians or quantitative economists.

3. **Failing to anonymize for double-blind review.** The journal operates double-blind peer review; remove author names, institution affiliations, and identifying details from the manuscript and references (e.g., do not self-cite in identifiable ways).

4. **Inconsistent or improperly formatted citations.** While the journal accepts Free Format submission, ensure all citations consistently follow Harvard (author-date) style to avoid revision requests; when in doubt, provide full author lists explicitly.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

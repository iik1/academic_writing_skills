# Journal Writing Skill: British Tax Review

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** ACCOUNT (classification only — the journal is substantively a UK/comparative tax LAW journal, not an archival/empirical accounting outlet)
- **Publisher:** Sweet & Maxwell / Thomson Reuters
- **Scope:** Doctrinal, policy, and comparative analysis of domestic, international, and comparative tax law; also publishes current notes, case notes, and reviews of taxation publications.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.sweetandmaxwell.co.uk/british-tax-review/editors.aspx (house style guide referenced but not itself web-accessible; submission address btr@worc.ox.ac.uk)
- **Field baseline:** account-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 (abstract/metadata level only — full text paywalled): "The UK VAT at 50: The Good, the Bad and the Ugly" (de la Feria, BTR 2023); "Irish Commission on Taxation and Welfare" note (Hunt, BTR 2023); "The Impact of Digitalisation on Personal Income Taxes" (de la Feria & Maffini, BTR 2021); "The Impact of Public Perceptions on General Consumption Taxes" (de la Feria & Walpole, BTR 2020); "Improving (Not Perfecting) Tax Legislation: Rules and Principles Revisited" (Freedman, BTR 2010); "HMRC's Management of the U.K. Tax System" (Freedman & Vella, BTR). SSRN full-text pages returned 403; only titles, venues, and search-indexed keyword lists were accessible.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No hypothesis-testing or empirical-methods structure. Articles are doctrinal/analytical essays organized around legal issues, statutory provisions, and policy arguments, not around a Data/Results/Robustness template. Issue also carries distinct short-form categories — Current Notes (topical commentary) and Case Notes (critical analysis of a specific judgment) — which have no equivalent in FIELD_SKILL.

### Length
Articles 5,000–12,000 words including footnotes; over 15,000 words only in exceptional circumstances. Shorter current notes/case notes are welcomed as a distinct, shorter category.

### Abstract
100–200 word abstract required (unstructured, single paragraph — no stated required fields such as method/findings/contribution).

### Citations
Journal has its own house style guide (downloadable from publisher site, not independently verified here) but is a UK law journal; sampled articles and the discipline's convention point to footnote-based legal citation (OSCOLA-style), not author-date parenthetical citation. This is a fundamental deviation from FIELD_SKILL's author-date convention.

### Headings
Not verified from guidelines; no evidence of Roman-numeral/ALL-CAPS or Arabic-numeral hypothesis-section headings seen in sampled titles/abstracts.

### Tables and Figures
Not addressed in accessible guidelines; sampled articles are doctrinal/policy pieces with no visible table-driven results reporting.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Submission must not have been published, in whole or part, in any other journal, on the internet, or elsewhere, and must not substantially duplicate other published work. Double-spaced electronic submission (Word format) to the editors.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
Sampled abstracts/titles read as legal-policy argumentation ("the good, the bad and the ugly," normative claims about fairness and legitimacy) rather than the hypothesis-driven empirical register FIELD_SKILL describes. RELAXATION (ABS 3): the archival empirical voice in FIELD_SKILL does not apply; register is doctrinal-legal, closer to law review writing than accounting-journal writing.

### Statistical Reporting
No archival hypothesis-testing, regression, or coefficient reporting apparent in any sampled piece. FIELD_SKILL's statistical narration norms do not transfer to this journal's typical article. RELAXATION (ABS 3).

### Citation Density
Not assessable from abstracts alone (footnote citations are not visible in search-indexed abstract text), but the underlying citation mechanism (footnotes to statutes, cases, HMRC guidance, Hansard) is categorically different from FIELD_SKILL's author-date in-text citation model. ENFORCED (house style, per publisher page) — treat as overriding FIELD_SKILL's citation format entirely for this journal.

No Hypothesis Development, Data/Research Design, or Results sections in the FIELD_SKILL sense. Articles are organized by legal/policy topic headings specific to the subject matter (e.g., by tax instrument, statutory provision, or policy question). PRACTICE ONLY / genre default, evidenced by titles and scope rather than confirmed structure, since full text was inaccessible.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Distinct short-form content types (Current Notes, Case Notes, Reviews) sit alongside full articles in each issue — a journal-specific submission category not present in FIELD_SKILL's structure discussion. GUIDELINE ONLY / structural note from publisher page.

## Common Pitfalls
- Do not structure a submission around hypotheses, empirical identification strategy, or regression tables — this journal does not publish archival/empirical accounting research in the FIELD_SKILL sense; it is a tax law journal that happens to carry an ABS ACCOUNT classification.
- Do not use author-date in-text citations; the journal's discipline and house style point to footnote-based legal citation, not the (Author Year) convention assumed by FIELD_SKILL.
- Do not exceed 12,000 words (footnotes included) without a strong justification; 15,000 is a hard ceiling absent exceptional circumstances.
- Confirm current word/style requirements against the publisher's downloadable house style guide before submission, since it could not be directly retrieved in this review.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: International Journal of Auditing

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ACCOUNT
- **Publisher:** Wiley-Blackwell
- **Scope:** International auditing and assurance research. The journal prioritizes work with clear relevance for international practitioners, policy makers, and the auditing profession. Accepts all research orientations (empirical, qualitative, theoretical) that are methodologically and theoretically sound.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
**Main body sections (required):** Abstract, Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion, Conclusion. All required sections must be contained in the single manuscript submission or separated files as specified.

**Optional:** Authors may include up to 3 "key points" written for practitioners, to be published with the article. These are distinct from the academic abstract.

### Length
- **Abstract:** 150–250 words (Wiley standard for this journal type). Summary of study objectives, methodology, key findings, and significance within this range.
- **Overall manuscript:** No universal word limit stated in publicly available guidelines; follows Wiley practice of flexible limits tied to complexity of evidence presented. Very long manuscripts may require justification.

### Abstract
**Structured or unstructured:** Unstructured is standard (single paragraph, not labeled sections like "Objective," "Methods," "Results"). Must cover objectives, methodology, key findings, and significance of the work.

### Citations
**Style:** American Psychological Association (APA) format in-text and reference list. Authors must review APA guidelines prior to submission.

**In-text format:** (Author Year) or Author (Year) depending on grammatical role. Multiple citations separated by semicolons.

### Headings
Follows Wiley conventions: Hierarchical structure with proper heading levels. Specific formatting (Roman numerals vs. Arabic numerals, ALL CAPS vs. title case) should align with Wiley submission template for the journal; authors should follow the journal's provided template.

### Tables and Figures
- Consecutive numbering and clear labeling required
- Tables and figures may be embedded in manuscript at initial submission or submitted as separate files; at revision stage, must be separate files
- Descriptive captions required
- Standard resolution and quality standards

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Double-blind peer review: Manuscripts must be anonymized; author identifying information goes on a separate title page only
- Plagiarism and text reuse are subject to journal policy and CrossCheck screening
- Data and code sharing increasingly expected; availability statements may be required

### Practitioner Orientation (Emphasis Difference)
**Delta: RELAXATION of pure academic tone.** While the field baseline (FIELD_SKILL) reflects top-tier academic journals (ABS 4*/4) that address specialist audiences, the International Journal of Auditing explicitly accommodates practitioner relevance and policy impact. This may be reflected in:
- Slightly more explicit and less hedged language when discussing implications for practice
- Willingness to emphasize "so what" for auditors and regulators, not just academic contribution
- Acceptance of thought leadership pieces and critiques in addition to original empirical research
- Optional practitioner-facing "key points" section suggests a dual audience (academics and auditing professionals)

**Classification:** PRACTICE ONLY (implied, not explicitly stated in accessible guidelines; inferred from journal scope and positioning)

### Methodological Breadth (Compared to FIELD_SKILL)
**Delta: BROADENED acceptance.** The field baseline notes the accounting discipline is "dominated" by quantitative/archival/experimental work, with only AOS as a qualitative minority. The International Journal of Auditing explicitly states "any research orientation is acceptable" provided it is "methodologically and theoretically sound." This includes:
- Case studies and qualitative designs (confirmed from article examples)
- Theoretical and analytical papers
- Mixed-method designs
- Literature reviews and critiques

**Classification:** GUIDELINE ONLY (explicitly stated in journal scope; less emphasized in field baseline)

### Voice and Register: Caution on "Evidence" Language
**Delta: Potentially STRICTER on causal claims.** Auditing is a profession where legal liability and accountability are paramount. While the field baseline distinguishes causal claims from associational language, the auditing context may demand even more careful calibration:
- Claims about audit effectiveness, evidence sufficiency, or audit quality may warrant heightened hedging to reflect uncertainty and professional risk exposure
- Language such as "the audit evidence supports" or "consistent with" may be preferred over stronger causal constructions

**Classification:** PRACTICE ONLY (not explicitly stated; inferred from auditing profession's risk environment and article examples examining auditor judgments and responses)

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Failing to anonymize the manuscript for double-blind review.** The journal operates under strict anonymity rules. Remove all author identifying information, citations to own work, and identifying institutional details from the main text. Use a separate cover page for author contact information.

2. **Inadequate documentation of research design and procedure.** Auditing research depends on credible method description. Reviewers will scrutinize how audit subjects/samples were selected, how procedures were conducted, and potential sources of bias. Vague descriptions of case selection or audit procedure will be flagged.

3. **Overstating generalizability or causal inference without sufficient identification.** Auditing research often involves small samples, field settings, or single-firm studies. Claims about "the impact of X on audit quality" without clear identification of causality are common rejections. Use measured language ("suggests," "consistent with," "may indicate") unless the design clearly supports stronger claims.

4. **Missing explicit statements of practical implication.** While not required, the journal's emphasis on relevance means reviewers expect authors to briefly address why the findings matter for audit practice or regulation. A conclusion that ends with "future research should..." without tying findings to practice risks being seen as less aligned with the journal's scope.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

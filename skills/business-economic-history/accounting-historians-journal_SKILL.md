# Journal Writing Skill: Accounting Historians Journal

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** BUS HIST & ECON HIST
- **Publisher:** American Accounting Association
- **Scope:** International journal addressing accounting history, the development of accounting thought and practice, and research providing historical perspective on contemporary accounting issues. Publishes archival primary-source work and quantitative accounting history equally.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://aaahq.org/Research/Journals/Accounting-Historians-Journal/Guide-for-Authors
- **Field baseline:** business-economic-history-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 8 (titles/abstracts and table of contents from 2024-2025 issues: "Diffusion of Double Entry Bookkeeping before 1800", "Internal Audit at General Motors in the Late 1920s", "Peculiarities of Accounting Practices in Francesco Datini's Partnership in Pisa", "Evolution of Accounting at the University of Michigan: 1901 to 2024", "75 Years of the Accounting Hall of Fame", "Accounting History Research Methods and Resources", "Digital Accounting History—Adoption of a Digitalized Historical Research Methodology", and "The PCAOB During the Youth Stage of Its Regulatory Life Cycle")

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Manuscripts must follow this order: Title page (separate from main text to ensure anonymity), Abstract, AI disclosure statement, Introduction (labeled Section I), article body with numbered sections, References, Figures (with captions in text only), Tables, Appendices. Section headings must be numbered sequentially (I, II, III, etc.). First sentence must clearly state the research issue or problem addressed and motivation.

### Length
Initial submissions must not exceed 55 pages total, including references, figures, tables, and appendices. "Salmagundi" section submissions (responses to prior articles, book reviews, or exploratory historical work) should be considerably shorter than main articles.

### Abstract
Non-structured format. Maximum 150 words. Must summarize the research puzzle, argument, and contribution in compact, non-technical language (consistent with FIELD_SKILL, with explicit word limit added).

### Citations
Chicago Manual of Style (16th ed.), Author-Date in-text format. Examples: single author (Berry 2003), multiple authors (Scholes, Wolfson, Erickson, Maydew, and Shevlin 2008), six or more authors (Dikolli et al. 2013). All factual claims, dates, figures, and interpretations must be cited or footnoted to primary or secondary sources.

### Formatting and Font
12-point Times New Roman, double-spaced, 1-inch margins throughout. All pages numbered sequentially. Spell out numbers one through ten except in tables or technical contexts. All graphics and charts must include alt-text.

### Tables and Figures
Submit as separate files in approved formats: EPS (preferred), PS, TIFF, PDF, JPEG, PNG. Resolution: 600 dpi for line art, 264 dpi for halftones. Figures must be clearly sourced and accompanied by prose interpretation; tables must include source attribution and explanatory text.

### Explicit Prohibitions and Required Disclosures
- Completed Conflict of Interest (COI) form mandatory at submission.
- ORCID ID required for all authors.
- AI disclosure statement required if generative AI or AI-assisted tools were used (must comply with AAA authorship and plagiarism policies).
- Human subjects research requires IRB approval; approval must be noted in a footnote.
- No plagiarism in any form, including self-plagiarism. No undisclosed conflicts. No misrepresentation of prior publication status.
- Data Description Disclosures required upon conditional acceptance.
- Revisions must be submitted within 6 months of request; otherwise treated as new submissions.

### Manuscript Structure and Formality
FIELD_SKILL allows flexible introduction and literature review placement; this journal requires Introduction as Section I with numbered sections throughout. This adds structural rigidity not present in the field baseline (ENFORCED).

### Abstract Requirements
FIELD_SKILL does not specify abstract word limits; this journal mandates ≤150 words (ENFORCED). The format (non-structured, compact) aligns with FIELD_SKILL.

### Formatting Conventions
FIELD_SKILL specifies nothing about font, spacing, margins, or page limits. This journal enforces 12-pt Times New Roman, double-spacing, 1-inch margins, 55-page limit, sequential page numbering, and alt-text for graphics (ENFORCED). This represents substantial formatting discipline not required by the field baseline.

### Citation Style Specification
FIELD_SKILL notes that "citation density is high" but does not prescribe a citation format. This journal explicitly mandates Chicago Author-Date style (ENFORCED).

### AI Disclosure
FIELD_SKILL predates the era of generative AI. This journal requires mandatory AI disclosure (generative AI and AI-assisted writing tools must be disclosed and must comply with AAA authorship/plagiarism policies) (ENFORCED).

This journal explicitly accepts shorter submissions for responses to prior articles, book reviews, and exploratory historical work under the "Salmagundi" heading (PRACTICE/GUIDELINE). ## Common Pitfalls
Based on submission guidelines and recent publications:

1. **Failing to anonymize title page**: The title page must be submitted as a separate Word document to preserve anonymity. Failure to do this will delay review or result in desk rejection. Remove all identifying information (author names, institutions, funding sources, author bios) from the main manuscript.

2. **Exceeding page limits**: The 55-page hard limit (including references, tables, figures, appendices) is strictly enforced. Manuscripts exceeding this will be returned without review. Budget carefully and use appendices for supplementary material.

3. **Missing or incomplete AI disclosure**: If any generative AI tools (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, etc.) or AI-assisted writing tools were used in any stage of research, analysis, or writing, you must disclose this in the mandatory AI disclosure statement. Non-disclosure or incomplete disclosure violates AAA plagiarism and authorship policies and can result in desk rejection or withdrawal of accepted manuscripts.

4. **Misalignment of research issue and motivation**: The journal emphasizes that the first sentence or opening section must clearly articulate the historical or accounting problem being addressed and the motivation for the study. Vague or unclear problem statements are common reasons for rejection at the editor screening stage.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

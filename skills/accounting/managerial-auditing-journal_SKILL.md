# Journal Writing Skill: Managerial Auditing Journal

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ACCOUNT
- **Publisher:** Emerald Publishing
- **Scope:** Global forum for current research and practice in auditing and assurance (internal and external; financial and non-financial), addressing trends, paradigms, perspectives, and ethical, social, environmental and economic aspects of contemporary assurance, management performance, and governance issues.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.emeraldgrouppublishing.com/journal/maj (full guidelines may require account access; see note below)
- **Field baseline:** account-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 5 (Confucian culture auditor choice 2025; Audit partner narcissism 2024; ESG disclosure moderation 2025; Audit committee tenure 2025; KAM firm-specific information 2025)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
**Emerald-specific structured abstract format, NOT traditional article sections.** The canonical section headings are:
1. **Purpose** — States research question/objective
2. **Design/methodology/approach** — Sample, methods, data
3. **Findings** — Results and evidence
4. **Originality/value** — Contributions and implications
5. Optional: **Research limitations/implications**, **Practical implications**, **Social implications** (may be omitted if not applicable)

This differs substantially from the FIELD_SKILL traditional structure (Introduction → Hypothesis Development → Methods → Results → Discussion/Conclusion). Emerald journals enforce this schema across all articles in the journal.

### Length
**3,000–8,000 words total** (including abstract, references, tables, figures). This is substantially shorter than FIELD_SKILL baseline papers, which typically run 30–60 pages in journal format.

### Abstract
**Structured abstract, maximum 250 words total** (including all fields and keywords). The abstract must be labeled with the four required headings: **Purpose** | **Design/methodology/approach** | **Findings** | **Originality/value**. All four must be present; optional sections may be omitted if not applicable.

**Keywords:** Up to six keywords encapsulating principal subjects, plus JEL classification codes (if applicable).

### Citations
**Author-date citation style.** Follows FIELD_SKILL norm — in-text citations as (Author Year) or Author (Year), multiple citations separated by semicolons. No delta from field baseline.

### Headings
The five or six mandatory sections are not numbered; they appear as plain text headings: **Purpose**, **Design/methodology/approach**, **Findings**, **Originality/value**, etc. No Roman numerals (AAA style) or Arabic numerals (JAE/JAR style); Emerald does not permit section numbering.

### Tables and Figures
Follows FIELD_SKILL norms (table notes with variable definitions, statistical significance thresholds, standard-error clustering noted). No explicit delta observed.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Manuscripts must be original (standard).
- Generative AI use: Copy-editing and language/structure/clarity improvement of authors' own original work is permitted; wholesale AI generation is prohibited (standard Emerald policy).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Article Structure (Major Delta)
**ENFORCED.** Unlike the FIELD_SKILL canonical structure (Introduction → Hypothesis Development → Methods → Results → Discussion/Conclusion), Managerial Auditing Journal enforces an Emerald-standardized schema: Purpose → Design/methodology/approach → Findings → Originality/value. This schema is not flexible. All observed recent articles (2024–2025) follow this structure without exception. Authors cannot use traditional academic section headings.

### Abstract Format (Major Delta)
**ENFORCED.** FIELD_SKILL describes abstracts as short, structured summaries stating research question, method, findings, and contribution in a single paragraph. Managerial Auditing Journal requires a multi-field labeled abstract with explicit headings (Purpose | Design/methodology/approach | Findings | Originality/value), maximum 250 words total. This is a structural requirement, not optional.

### Length Constraint (Major Delta)
**ENFORCED.** Papers are capped at 3,000–8,000 words. FIELD_SKILL baseline articles (ABS 4*/4) typically run 30–60 journal pages (≈12,000–25,000 words). Managerial Auditing Journal is a lower-tier (ABS 2) journal with a much tighter word budget, forcing concision and limiting the depth of hypothesis development, robustness checks, and discussion visible in FIELD_SKILL papers.

### Hypothesis Presentation (Minor Delta)
**PRACTICE ONLY.** Observed articles do not explicitly number hypotheses (H1, H2, H3, etc.) as is common in FIELD_SKILL baseline papers. Instead, research questions are framed as investigative questions or conceptual relationships stated implicitly within the Purpose/Design sections. In the 5 spot-checked articles, only one explicitly stated a formal hypothesis; most frame inquiries as research questions or relationships to explore. This is likely a consequence of the shorter format — explicit hypothesis enumeration is often omitted to save words.

### Citation Density (Minor Delta)
**PRACTICE ONLY.** While FIELD_SKILL specifies citation density by section (Introduction: high; Methods: medium; Results: low to medium; Conclusion: medium), observed Managerial Auditing Journal articles show relatively lower citation density overall, consistent with the tighter word budget. Introduction sections still establish prior work and gaps, but with less exhaustive enumeration of prior studies than FIELD_SKILL papers. The gate is set by word count, not by editorial norm.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Forcing traditional section structure.** Submissions with Introduction / Hypothesis Development / Methods / Results / Discussion headings will be desk-rejected or require major revision. Emerald journals enforce the Purpose / Design/methodology/approach / Findings / Originality/value structure strictly. Follow the template provided in the submission system.

2. **Exceeding word count.** Papers longer than 8,000 words will be flagged by the submission system or reviewers. Given the word budget, robustness checks and extensive robustness sections typical of FIELD_SKILL papers must be heavily condensed or placed in a brief appendix.

3. **Unstructured abstract.** Submitting a single-paragraph abstract without the four required labeled sections (Purpose, Design/methodology/approach, Findings, Originality/value) violates submission requirements. The abstract must explicitly show these headings.

4. **Insufficient explanation of contribution in Originality/value section.** Because space is limited, the Originality/value section often feels cramped. Reviewers still expect explicit positioning relative to prior work; do not assume brevity excuses implicitness.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

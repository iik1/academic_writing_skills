# Journal Writing Skill: Business Horizons

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN
- **Publisher:** Elsevier
- **Scope:** Bimonthly publication of the Kelley School of Business, Indiana University. Covers business issues and problems with emphasis on identifying problems and recommending actionable solutions. Balances academic rigor with practical applicability for business practitioners and scholars.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/business-horizons/publish/guide-for-authors (paywalled; content extracted from public sources)
- **Field baseline:** mgmt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 12 articles from 2024-2026 (titles/metadata extracted via IDEAS/RePEc and journal announcements)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
- No prescribed section-naming conventions in public guidelines; articles appear to follow FIELD_SKILL templates (quantitative deductive, qualitative, conceptual, or integrative review).
- Acknowledgments, Competing Interests, and Data Availability statements appear at article end, per FIELD_SKILL.

### Length
- **Absolute limit: 25 pages, all inclusive** (including title, abstract, text, tables, figures, and references). This is tighter than many ABS 4/4* outlets (typically 10,000–18,000 words); Business Horizons articles typically range 8–12 pages.
- ENFORCED: Articles must be concise. Expect tighter page budgets for literature reviews and methods.

### Abstract
- **150–200 words** (per search results; slightly shorter than FIELD_SKILL norm of 150–250).
- Unstructured format (no labeling of Purpose/Methods/Results/Conclusion observed in sample articles).
- No citations in abstract (aligns with FIELD_SKILL).

### Citations
- **APA in-text style** (Author, Year) — ENFORCED per guidelines and articles observed.
- Multiple citations ordered alphabetically, et al. for three+ authors — standard per FIELD_SKILL.
- Citation density follows FIELD_SKILL patterns: Introduction and Theory sections dense; Results/Findings sparse; Discussion interprets against citations.

### Formatting
- **Font:** Times New Roman 12-point.
- **Spacing:** Double-spaced throughout (including references).
- **File format:** MS Word-compatible.
- **Page numbering:** Centered at bottom, starting with title page as page 0.
- ENFORCED: Deviations (single spacing, font changes) may result in desk rejection without review.

### Headings
- No explicit journal-specific rules in public guidelines. Likely follows FIELD_SKILL conventions (substantive section names, not generic labels).

### Tables and Figures
- No specific constraints found in public guidelines. Assume FIELD_SKILL norms (clear captions, defined variables, appropriately integrated).

### Language and Tone
- **Nontechnical, reader-friendly language required** — ENFORCED.
- Write for a **broad business audience** (practitioners and academics), not specialists.
- Avoid jargon and unnecessary technical terminology.
- Engaging and informative tone, balancing accessibility with scholarly credibility.
- PRACTICE ONLY: Observed articles emphasize actionable frameworks and practical takeaways ("How to prevent…", "Managing…", "Unlocking benefits…").

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Does **not** accept notes, briefs, commentaries, or letters to the editor — ENFORCED.
- Authors limited to **maximum 3 submissions per calendar year** — ENFORCED.
- Manuscripts previously published, under review elsewhere, or with pre-existing copyright restrictions cannot be submitted — ENFORCED.
- During review period, authors agree **not to submit manuscript for consideration elsewhere** — ENFORCED.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Page/Length Budget (Strong Delta)
Business Horizons enforces a hard 25-page limit and typical articles are 8–12 pages, significantly shorter than FIELD_SKILL expectations (10,000–18,000 words). This is a **RELAXATION** of field norms and forces:
- Compressed literature reviews (brief territory/niche establishment, minimal full-section reviews).
- Methods sections must be tightly written; statistical reporting follows field norms but with fewer robustness checks reported in detail.
- Discussion must integrate findings, implications, and limitations concisely (no separate, lengthy Discussion section is typical).

### Language Register (Delta Observed)
FIELD_SKILL allows personal voice and direct assertion ("We argue," "We contend"). Business Horizons articles observed in sample emphasize **accessible, prescriptive language** with less hedging on recommendations to practitioners. Examples from article titles: "How to prevent DEI backfire," "Punishing the good?" — direct, imperative framing aimed at actionable reader takeaway. 
- PRACTICE ONLY: Articles favor clarity and directness over measured academic caution in prescriptive sections.

### Practical Contribution Statement (Delta Observed)
Contributions in Business Horizons articles are framed as **practical/managerial solutions** alongside theoretical advance. Sample articles state contributions as "best-practice recommendations," "frameworks for managers," or "how-to guidance."
- PRACTICE ONLY: Theoretical contribution is mentioned but managerial actionability is equally emphasized. This aligns more with practitioner-facing framing than pure academic contribution statements in FIELD_SKILL.

### Abstract Content (Minor Delta)
Abstracts are slightly shorter (150–200 vs. FIELD_SKILL 150–250), and observed abstracts emphasize the **problem statement and practical solution** more than theoretical gap. 
- ENFORCED: 150–200 words.

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Pronoun usage:** "We" throughout, aligns with FIELD_SKILL.
- **CARS Introduction structure:** Establish territory → establish niche → occupy niche observed in sample articles; aligns with FIELD_SKILL.
- **Research question explicitness:** Stated clearly near end of introduction; aligns with FIELD_SKILL.
- **Hypotheses vs. Propositions:** Quantitative work uses H1, H2…; qualitative uses theoretical categories; conceptual uses P1, P2… — FIELD_SKILL applies unchanged.
- **Methods reporting tense:** Past tense for procedures; present for descriptive data — FIELD_SKILL applies.
- **Evidence presentation:** Tables for quantitative; quotations/vignettes for qualitative — FIELD_SKILL applies.
- **Hedging and epistemic stance:** Modal verbs mark inferences; "We find," "We argue" distinctions observed — FIELD_SKILL applies unchanged.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Exceeding page/word budget.** A 12,000-word manuscript meant for a top-tier journal becomes 15+ pages at 12-pt double-spaced and is rejected unreviewed. Trim ruthlessly; prioritize problem statement and solution over exhaustive literature review.

2. **Over-technical language or excessive jargon.** Articles rejected or desk-revised for inaccessibility to the broad practitioner audience. Write for an MBA cohort, not a PhD specialty seminar.

3. **Weak or missing practical take-away.** Articles are expected to offer prescriptive guidance ("We recommend…", "Best practice is…", "Managers should…"). Pure conceptual work without actionable implications underperforms in this venue.

4. **Submitting simultaneously to multiple outlets.** Authors must agree not to submit the same manuscript elsewhere during review. Violation results in desk rejection and editorial sanction.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

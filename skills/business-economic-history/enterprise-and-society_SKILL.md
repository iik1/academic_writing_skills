# Journal Writing Skill: Enterprise and Society

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** BUS HIST & ECON HIST
- **Publisher:** Cambridge University Press (for the Business History Conference)
- **Scope:** Research on the historical relations between businesses and their
  larger political, cultural, institutional, social, and economic contexts;
  international in scope.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/enterprise-and-society/information/author-instructions/preparing-your-materials
  and https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/enterprise-and-society/information/instructions-contributors
- **Field baseline:** business-economic-history-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 17 (title/abstract level; full text largely
  paywalled) — drawn from Vol. 25 No. 1 (Mar 2024) table of contents,
  Vol. 27 No. 2 (Jun 2026, latest issue), and FirstView (June 2026),
  covering topics from caste entrepreneurship in India, Wallenberg family
  Cold War banking, American Legion film distribution, UK hostile
  takeovers, Asturias steel clusters, British worker co-operatives, US beef
  grading, capitalist feminism/banking, WWII venereal disease campaigns,
  Edinburgh finance professionals, Hudson's Bay Company accounting, ABF
  trucking, Chilean elite memory, European business forums, early modern
  Japanese merchant firms, and craft brewing.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Required components, in order: (1) title page as a separate file with
author name/contact info, (2) abstract submitted separately, (3) article
text (title + body only, no author-identifying info), (4) bibliography,
(5) endnotes (converted to page-keyed footnotes at production), (6) a
two-line-maximum author biography on the first page of the manuscript.
No specific internal section-heading scheme (e.g., "Introduction,"
"Methods") is mandated beyond this.

### Length
Approximately 8,000–12,000 words for research articles. No formal hard
cap stated, but the editors note they may ask for reworking if an
article requires substantial extra preparation effort for review.

### Abstract
Up to 150 words, unstructured, headed by the article's title, submitted
via the online system as a separate item from the manuscript.

### Citations
Chicago Manual of Style (notes-bibliography style), mandatory. Full
citations appear ONLY in the bibliography; endnotes use short-form
references only (e.g., "Churella, *From Steam to Diesel*, 197"). No
social-science in-text parenthetical citation is permitted anywhere.

### Headings
Not explicitly specified by the guidelines.

### Tables and Figures
Photographs/figures capped at 195mm (height) x 120mm (width).
Photographs must be at least 300 dpi; line drawings at least 1200 dpi.
Figure legends must define all symbols/abbreviations. Figures numbered
consecutively in Arabic numerals. Authors must supply written evidence of
copyright permissions and accessibility (alt-text) descriptions for all
visual content.

### Explicit Prohibitions
No bottom-of-page footnotes (endnotes only, converted at production). No
Roman numeral footnote markers (Arabic only). No social-science
parenthetical citation. Guidelines explicitly instruct authors to avoid
passive voice, avoid opening sentences with "And" or "But," minimize
prepositional phrases, and avoid vague, overlong sentences/constructions
running longer than 10–15 lines. Guidelines also explicitly ask authors
to write for "educated non-specialists" and to adopt language sensitive
to gender and other issues beyond personal pronouns.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
RELAXATION/ENFORCED: The guidelines explicitly instruct authors to prefer
active voice and strong verbs and to minimize passive constructions —
sharper than FIELD_SKILL's more permissive stance that passive voice is
normal in data/institutional description. Guidelines also explicitly
target "educated non-specialists" as the audience, which is more
prescriptive about accessibility than FIELD_SKILL states; consistent with
this, spot-checked abstracts (e.g., caste entrepreneurship, Wallenberg
Cold War banking, craft brewing) favor plain descriptive framing over
technical vocabulary, even where the underlying work is archivally dense.

### Structural Deviations
PRACTICE ONLY: The Vol. 25 No. 1 issue sampled includes a "Presidential
Address"-style symposium/comment format (an article followed by multiple
short response essays and a reply), an accepted format not mentioned in
FIELD_SKILL. This is an occasional journal-specific feature ("Symposia,"
"Comments," per the guidelines' article-type list) rather than the norm
for standard research articles.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
ENFORCED: A short (2-line) author biography on the manuscript's first
page is a journal-specific submission requirement not found in
FIELD_SKILL. GUIDELINE ONLY: explicit sentence-length ceiling (10-15
lines) is more prescriptive than FIELD_SKILL's tolerance for long
qualifying clauses; spot-checked abstracts are short and plain, consistent
with this instruction, though full-text sentence length could not be
verified due to paywalling.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- Article structure (puzzle/argument/historiography opening, thematic or
  chronological core, conclusion): FIELD DEFAULT.
- Hedging and epistemic caution: FIELD DEFAULT.
- Argumentation and rhetorical moves (historiographical gap-framing,
  revisionist/counter-narrative framing): FIELD DEFAULT.
- Citation density and engagement with historiography: FIELD DEFAULT
  (high density via Chicago-style endnotes, consistent with field norm).
- Results/reporting norms (narrative integration of evidence vs.
  discrete results sections): FIELD DEFAULT.
- Discussion/conclusion norms: FIELD DEFAULT.

---

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting with in-text parenthetical (author-date) citations or
  bottom-of-page footnotes instead of Chicago-style endnotes with a full
  bibliography — explicitly prohibited.
- Omitting the required 2-line author biography from the first page.
- Submitting an abstract over 150 words or one that duplicates the title
  page's author-identifying information within the anonymized manuscript
  file.
- Overusing passive voice or long (10-15+ line) sentences, both of which
  the guidelines flag directly as something to avoid, more explicitly
  than typical for the field.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

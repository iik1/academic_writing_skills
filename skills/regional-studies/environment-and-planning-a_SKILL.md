## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No fixed template mandated; authors must ensure heading levels are clear and sections clearly defined. Three distinct manuscript types exist beyond the standard research article: **Commentaries** (editorial interventions on topical events/developments, internally reviewed by editors), **Exchanges** (interventions in scholarly debates, critical commentary on intellectual projects, or discussions of theory/methodology/ethics/politics of knowledge, internally reviewed by the editorial board), and **Theme Issue proposals**. FIELD_SKILL does not mention these formats — they are journal-specific submission tracks with separate review processes (internal, not full double-anonymized peer review) and separate word limits.

### Length
Original Manuscripts: 7000–9500 words, and this count includes *everything* — abstract, title page, keywords, acknowledgements, references, appendices — plus visual aids counted at 500 words per full journal page (e.g., a half-page figure = 250 words). Commentaries: 1000–3000 words, all-inclusive. Exchanges: 2500–4500 words, all-inclusive. Submissions exceeding limits may be returned pre-review without full evaluation.

### Abstract
Unstructured, maximum 250 words, placed between title and main body. Must concisely state purpose, major findings, and conclusions. Minimum 3 keywords required, listed after the abstract.

### Citations
Sage Harvard reference style (author-date), not a generic "Harvard-like" convention — journal-specific EndNote output file is provided. Every in-text citation must have a matching reference-list entry with identical spelling and year. Citations to preprints are discouraged once a peer-reviewed version exists.

### Headings
No fixed heading template required, but heading levels must be unambiguous. A specific mandatory heading sequence applies to the **title page** (not the manuscript body): Acknowledgments, Author Contributions, then "Statements and Declarations" with required sub-headings (Ethical considerations, Consent to participate, Consent for publication, Declaration of conflicting interest, Funding statement, Data availability) — each must appear even if answered "Not applicable."

### Tables and Figures
Figures numbered consecutively in order of appearance in text; 300dpi minimum resolution. Color figures free online; print color color billed at production. Visual aids double as word-count consumers under the length rule above (a rule not seen elsewhere in the field baseline).

### Explicit Prohibitions
Full manuscript must be anonymized for double-anonymized review (no author names in file name, headers, or footers — all identifying content moved to the separate title page). AI chatbots (e.g., ChatGPT) may not be listed as authors. Journal editor reserves the right to refuse submissions made on an author's behalf by an unlisted third party (e.g., a commercial writing/editing service) unless disclosed in the cover letter and acknowledgements. Manuscripts not conforming to guidelines may be desk-returned before review.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
Abstracts sampled are predominantly written in third-person descriptive framing ("this article argues," "the authors examine," "the research demonstrates") rather than first-person plural — a heavier lean toward impersonal voice than FIELD_SKILL's "both accepted, no consensus" framing would suggest for abstracts specifically. The one clearly single-authored, shorter Exchange-type piece sampled ("The geoeconomics of globalization 2.0") used first-person singular throughout ("I suggest," "I advance the discussion") — consistent with the journal's Exchanges format being a more personal, argumentative register than standard Original Manuscripts. PRACTICE ONLY / low-to-moderate prevalence, drawn from abstract-level text only (abstracts are sometimes edited toward third person independent of body-text voice); not strong enough to override FIELD_SKILL's "both accepted" baseline, but worth noting as a mild default lean for this journal's abstracts.

### Structural Deviations
The Commentary/Exchange dual-track for shorter, internally-reviewed, non-standard-peer-review pieces is a journal-specific feature absent from FIELD_SKILL, which only describes the standard empirical-article structure. ENFORCED (stated in guidelines; confirmed by at least one sampled Exchange-type piece with matching short length and distinct first-person argumentative voice).

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Scope statement explicitly self-identifies as "pluralist and heterodox," a framing stronger and more explicit than the general field baseline's description of hybridity across quantitative/qualitative traditions — this journal signals openness to heterodox political-economy framing as a matter of editorial identity, not just methodological tolerance. GUIDELINE ONLY / framing signal, not confirmed as a distinct writing convention in the sampled abstracts beyond normal field-baseline variety.

## Common Pitfalls
- Miscounting the word limit: authors frequently underestimate total length because figures/maps/appendices all convert to word-equivalents against the 7000–9500 cap — a spatial-visualization-heavy paper (expected in this field) can hit the ceiling faster than word count of body text alone suggests.
- Submitting the main manuscript with identifying information left in headers/footers or file name, which breaches the journal's double-anonymized review requirement.
- Missing one of the required "Statements and Declarations" sub-headings on the title page (each must be present and say "Not applicable" if unused, not simply omitted).

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

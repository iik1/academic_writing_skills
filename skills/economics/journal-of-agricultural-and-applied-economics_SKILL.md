# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Agricultural & Applied Economics

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Cambridge University Press, on behalf of the Southern Agricultural Economics Association
- **Scope:** Publishes scholarly research, extension, and teaching work in agricultural and applied economics, covering agribusiness, natural resources, and rural development, of national and international relevance. Accepts both original research articles and review studies with strong policy/methodology contributions.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-agricultural-and-applied-economics/information/author-instructions/preparing-your-materials
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 12 (abstract/section-heading level via Cambridge Core pages): "Risk Propensity and Acceptance of Gene-edited and GM Food..."; "Investigating the Role of Political Messaging on Preferences for Local Food Products..."; "Media Content, African Swine Fever, a Trade War, and U.S. Lean Hog Futures"; "Strategies for Land-Grant Universities to Foster Public Trust"; "Western Safflower Contracting Strategies"; "A Micro-perspective Analysis of the Demand for Greek and Non-Greek Yogurt..."; "Economic Burden of Livestock Abortions in Northern Tanzania"; "Cultivating Support: An Ex-Ante Typological Analysis of Farmers' Responses to Multi-Peril Crop Insurance Subsidies"; plus abstracts of "Strategies for Enhancing Economic Sustainability: Modeling Reduced Bale Handling Costs in Cotton Warehousing," "Interrelationships Among the Rice Export Prices of Major Exporting Countries," "Land Ownership Security, Farm Investment, and Investment Risk in Indian Agriculture," "Does Adoption of Improved Variety Encourage Farmers to Invest in Modern Inputs..."

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Title page submitted as a separate file (anonymized manuscript for double-blind review). Body order: abstract and keywords → JEL classifications → main text with headings/subheadings → footnotes → references → supplementary materials (optional). Cambridge's standard end-matter block (Data Availability Statement, Author Contributions, Competing Interests, Financial Support/Funding Statement) is required and appears after the Conclusion, before or interleaved with Appendices.

### Length
No explicit overall word/page limit stated on the guidelines page reviewed, though Cambridge's broader author-instructions material for this journal indicates main manuscripts are typically under 8,000 words.

### Abstract
Structured as a single unstructured paragraph, hard cap of 100 words (noticeably shorter than typical ABS 4/4* practice) — "an abstract not exceeding 100 words."

### Citations
Custom author-date reference style specified by the journal (not a generic economics author-year format): e.g., "Davis, G.C. 'A Semantic Interpretation of Haavelmo's Structure of Econometrics.' Economics and Philosophy 16(October 2000):205-28." References alphabetized by surname with initials.

### Headings
No numbering scheme mandated; headings and subheadings permitted freely in body text.

### Tables and Figures
Figures submitted as separate files; all illustrative material referred to as "Figures" (no separate "Charts"/"Graphs" category). Tables and figures must be cited in text, numbered consecutively in Arabic numerals, with captions in all cases. Alt-text/accessibility descriptions required for figures.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Manuscript file must be fully anonymized (no author names anywhere) for double-blind review.
- Footnotes must not be used solely for citations or as pointers to other literature.
- Manuscript must not be under review, accepted, or in press elsewhere.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Abstract Length
100-word hard cap is materially tighter than the longer, more discursive abstracts typical of ABS 4/4* outlets. ENFORCED (stated in guidelines; all sampled abstracts were short, single-paragraph, and results-forward within that limit).

### Structural Deviations
A standalone "Literature Review" section (separate from the Introduction) appears more consistently here than the FIELD_SKILL norm of folding literature into the Introduction for shorter empirical papers — observed in "Gene-edited/GM Food" and "Greek Yogurt" articles. PRACTICE ONLY (not stated in guidelines, but a recurring pattern in the sample; not universal — several sampled articles omit a standalone review section, e.g., "Western Safflower," "Livestock Abortions," "Crop Insurance").

Results and Discussion are very frequently merged into a single "Results and Discussion" section rather than kept separate, more consistently than the FIELD_SKILL baseline's "practice varies" framing — observed in 5 of 12 sampled articles ("Gene-edited Food," "Lean Hog Futures," "Livestock Abortions," "Crop Insurance," and others). PRACTICE ONLY.

Cambridge-mandated end-matter (Data Availability Statement, Author Contributions, Competing Interests, Funding Statement, and occasionally a Disclaimer) appears as standard trailing sections in nearly every sampled article. ENFORCED (publisher-level Cambridge Core convention, consistently present), not covered by FIELD_SKILL.

The journal accepts a distinct "Review Article" type (narrative/policy synthesis with no data or methods section, e.g., "Strategies for Land-Grant Universities to Foster Public Trust") alongside standard empirical papers. GUIDELINE ONLY / FIELD DEFAULT for that subtype — when writing a review-type submission, the empirical IMRaD structure in FIELD_SKILL does not apply; use a topical/thematic heading structure instead.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
JEL codes are commonly but not universally reported in the sampled articles (absent in "Livestock Abortions in Northern Tanzania"); when present, typically 2–3 codes. Not a strict guideline-stated requirement in the pages reviewed, but a strong de facto convention. GUIDELINE ONLY / low-enforcement.

## Common Pitfalls
- Writing an abstract in the 150–250 word range common at ABS 4/4* outlets; JAAE enforces a hard 100-word cap.
- Leaving author names or identifying institutional affiliations in the manuscript body — double-blind review requires full anonymization.
- Omitting the Data Availability Statement, Author Contributions, and Competing Interests sections, which Cambridge requires as standard trailing sections regardless of paper type.
- Using footnotes as a substitute for in-text citations, which the journal explicitly disallows.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Tijdschrift Voor Economische en Sociale Geografie

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Wiley-Blackwell (on behalf of Royal Dutch Geographical Society)
- **Scope:** Peer-reviewed international journal focusing on contemporary issues in human geography, including economic, social, cultural, and political geographical themes. Journal of Economic and Human Geography.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/page/journal/14679663/homepage/forauthors.html
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 13 (See Sources section below)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

### Structure
TESG accepts multiple article types. For **regular research articles**, standard sections include Introduction, literature engagement, substantive body sections (e.g., Data/Methods/Analysis), Results/Findings, and Conclusion. The journal also publishes special sections: **Geographica** (visual contributions with 500–1000 words of prose), **Book Reviews** (800–1000 words single, up to 1500 words multiple), and **Special Issues/Dossiers** (6–8 articles or 3–5 for dossiers on unified topics). Section ordering and naming follow disciplinary geography norms rather than rigid templates.

### Length
- **Regular articles:** Maximum 8,000 words (approximately 16 pages in TESG format), excluding notes, references, and tables/figures.
- **Book reviews:** 800–1000 words (single), up to 1500 words (multiple book review).
- **Geographica:** 500–1000 words (text accompanying visual).

### Abstract
- **Required:** Yes.
- **Format:** Unstructured.
- **Word limit:** Maximum 150 words.

### Citations
- **Style:** Author-year (parenthetical or in-text).
- **Multiple authors:** Three or more authors cited in text as "et al." but all names must appear in full in the reference list.
- **Footnotes:** Permissible for supplementary material and qualifications, not for primary citations.

### Headings
Not explicitly mandated by guidelines; observed practice follows geography/social science norms (numbered or unnumbered Arabic headings common; no Roman numerals noted in articles reviewed).

### Tables and Figures
Guidelines note "tables and figures" as permitted but do not specify format requirements. Spot-checked articles use both; figures illustrate key findings and spatial variation, tables support empirical claims.

### Explicit Prohibitions and Authorship
- **Gift authorship:** Explicitly discouraged. All and only named authors must be willing to self-describe their role when asked by editors; editors reserve right to reject authorship claims unsupported by clear role description.
- **Identifying information:** Remove from manuscript and file names prior to submission for blind review; acknowledgements containing identifying information permitted only after acceptance.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Article Structure and Scope
**Delta: Lower formality in conclusions and scope statements.** Reviewed articles from TESG show noticeably shorter conclusions than the FIELD_SKILL baseline for ABS 4*/4 journals. Conclusions in TESG (1–2 pages, often much shorter) synthesize findings more informally and with less emphasis on policy/theoretical implications than ABS 4*/4 norms. Several articles include editorial introductions or commentary pieces that blur the line between article and discussion—a structural pattern absent from top-tier economics. *Classification: PRACTICE ONLY, RELAXATION (lower-tier journal norm).*

### Citation Density
**Delta: Moderate-to-low citation density in results/findings sections.** While introduction and literature sections maintain FIELD_SKILL's high citation density, results sections in TESG articles tend toward lower citation density than ABS 4* economics. Comparisons with prior work appear less frequently; instead, findings are often stated directly without competing estimates from published work. *Classification: PRACTICE ONLY, RELAXATION.*

### Hedging and Epistemic Caution
**Delta: Increased hedging in causal claims and external validity.** TESG articles employ noticeably more cautious language around causal interpretation and spatial generalisability than FIELD_SKILL baseline. Phrases such as "may suggest," "indicates possible connections," and explicit caveating of findings to specific contexts (e.g., "our results pertain specifically to Dutch urban areas") appear more frequently. This reflects geography's emphasis on situated knowledge and context-specificity. *Classification: PRACTICE ONLY, JOURNAL-SPECIFIC NORM (lower-tier social science field convention).*

### Voice and Pluralism
**Delta: Occasional use of singular first-person.** While FIELD_SKILL establishes "we" as near-universal in ABS 4*/4 economics, spot-checked TESG articles occasionally employ "I" even in multi-author papers, particularly in opening framing or reflexive discussion of positionality. This is consistent with geography's humanistic tradition. However, "we" remains dominant. *Classification: PRACTICE ONLY (minority pattern, not enforced).*

### Special Article Types
**Delta: Editorial introductions and commentary pieces.** TESG publishes short editorial introductions (2–4 pages) and commentary pieces (2–3 pages, e.g., "Searching for Hope in Nativist Times" at 183–186 pages) alongside full research articles. These lack the full structure of FIELD_SKILL and employ more direct address and argumentative tone. Not a violation of field norms but a distinctive structural feature of the journal. *Classification: ENFORCED (observed in published record).*

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Sentence and paragraph style:** FIELD DEFAULT. Medium-to-long sentences, high paragraph density, technical vocabulary—all consistent with FIELD_SKILL and broader geography writing norms.
- **Argumentation and rhetorical moves:** FIELD DEFAULT. Introduction hook, research question, approach, preview of results, and contributions all follow FIELD_SKILL patterns. Roadmap closing present in reviewed articles.
- **Methods conventions:** FIELD DEFAULT. Mixed tense (past for procedures, present for model properties), sufficient procedural detail for replication, identification strategy stated (where applicable), robustness checks present. Consistent with FIELD_SKILL.
- **Results and reporting norms:** FIELD DEFAULT. Integration of results and interpretation, tables as primary vehicle for quantitative findings, economic significance reported alongside statistical findings. Null results handled directly.
- **Discussion and conclusion norms:** FIELD DEFAULT (with caveat on length—see Delta above). Genuine synthesis, policy/theoretical implications drawn, limitations acknowledged, future directions suggested. No new results in conclusions.
- **Citation format:** FIELD DEFAULT. Author-year, primarily in-text or parenthetical; direct quotation rare; paraphrasing standard.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Overstating causal claims beyond identification strategy.** TESG reviewers penalise causal interpretation that exceeds what the data and design warrant. Frame results carefully as correlational, suggestive of mechanism, or local to the study context. Avoid language implying universal causality.

2. **Neglecting geographic context and positionality.** Unlike top-tier economics, TESG expects explicit discussion of where the research is situated, why that place matters, and how findings may or may not generalise. Treating geography as incidental rather than constitutive of the research is a common rejection trigger.

3. **Exceeding the 8,000-word limit.** The word cap is strictly enforced. Plan for ruthless pruning of redundancy and non-essential detail. Each section must earn its space. Supplementary material is not a default outlet for trimmed content.

4. **Insufficient engagement with geographic/social science literature.** While not requiring the citation density of top-tier economics, TESG expects grounding in relevant geographic theory and prior work on the specific place/phenomenon studied. Importing economic models without geographic contextualization is a frequent weakness.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

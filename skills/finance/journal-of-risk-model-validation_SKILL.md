# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Risk Model Validation

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** FINANCE
- **Publisher:** Incisive Media Ltd. (Risk.net journals)
- **Scope:** Publishes on implementation and validation of risk models — empirical evaluation of existing models, pitfalls in model validation, and new validation/back-testing methods. Primary application area is credit risk modelling, but covers validation issues for any financial asset class.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.risk.net/static/risk-journals-submission-guidelines (shared across all Risk.net/Incisive Media journals, not JRMV-specific)
- **Field baseline:** finance-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 with visible abstract/structure detail (Model risk tiering: an exploration of industry practices and principles; Validation of the backtesting process under the targeted review of internal models; Estimating value-at-risk using quantile regression and implied volatilities; An aggregated metrics framework for multicriteria model validation using rolling origin evaluation; plus 2 titles/abstracts from Vol. 20 No. 1, March 2026 listing). Full text is paywalled throughout; assessment relies on abstracts, keywords, and section-heading lists visible on publisher pages. This is a good-faith sample for an ABS 1 journal with no independent guidelines page (it shares a generic cross-journal submission policy).

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict. Sourced from the shared Risk.net/Incisive Media journals guidelines page — not journal-specific, but binding for JRMV submissions.

### Structure
Required manuscript components, in order: separate title page (author details, affiliations, running header max 50 characters, word count, figure/table count) → figure/table legends with captions → abstract → keywords → key messages (3–4 bullet points, max 85 characters each) → title → main text (author-anonymous, for blind review) → Declarations of Interest and Acknowledgements → References. Recommended main-text section order: Introduction → Literature Review → Data → Methodology → Results → Discussion → Conclusion.

### Length
Research papers: max 10,000 words. Forum papers: max 7,000 words. Not strictly enforced — publisher makes final call on length.

### Abstract
Single paragraph, 150–200 words, must stand alone (no references). Accompanied by a separate "key messages" list of 3–4 bullet points (max 85 characters each) — this is a JRMV/Risk.net-specific paratext element with no FIELD_SKILL equivalent.

### Citations
In-text: author-date per Chicago Manual of Style. Reference list: APA format, alphabetical, DOIs included where applicable.

### Headings
No journal-specific numbering convention beyond the recommended section order above; observed articles use plain Arabic-numbered sections (e.g., 1 Introduction, 2 Literature, 3 ...).

### Tables and Figures
Minimize quantity. Submit as vector graphics (EPS, SVG, PDF) where possible; avoid rasterized formats (TIFF, JPG, PSD); minimum 300dpi if raster is unavoidable. Appear in color online.

### Explicit Prohibitions
No author names/affiliations in the submission PDF (blind review). No papers under consideration elsewhere. No rasterized graphics files. No substantial author changes at proof stage. No independent online posting of the published paper. AI tool use (data extraction, background research, editing assistance) is permitted but must be explicitly acknowledged with details of use.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Voice and Register
RELAXATION: Articles read as practitioner/regulatory-application pieces rather than academic contributions to a theoretical literature. Abstracts favor descriptive framing ("The authors explain...", "This paper articulates key principles...") over the FIELD_SKILL norm of assertive contribution claims ("we are the first to show"). Consistent with ABS 1 tier — a relaxation of, not a departure from, the field baseline.

### Structural Deviations
PRACTICE ONLY: A standalone "Literature" or "Literature Review" section as a numbered main-text section (not folded into the introduction as a "Related literature" subsection) appears as the recommended and observed norm, e.g., "1 Introduction, 2 Literature, 3 Principles of model risk tiering...". This differs from the FIELD_SKILL default, where a standalone Literature Review section is "essentially never used." A separate "Discussion" section (distinct from Conclusion) is also part of the recommended structure, whereas FIELD_SKILL treats discussion/conclusion merging as the empirical-finance norm.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
ENFORCED: The "key messages" bullet-point summary (3–4 points, max 85 characters each) accompanying the abstract is a Risk.net/Incisive Media house convention not present in FIELD_SKILL or in mainstream academic finance journals.

GUIDELINE ONLY: Explicit AI-use disclosure requirement — stated in guidelines; cannot be confirmed against published articles due to paywall access.

## Common Pitfalls
- Omitting the "key messages" bullet list — required paratext specific to this journal/publisher, easy to miss if modeling submission on a standard academic finance journal.
- Folding literature review into the introduction as a subsection (the FIELD_SKILL default) instead of using a standalone numbered "Literature" section, which is the structure this journal's guidelines recommend and articles display.
- Including author names/affiliations anywhere in the main submission PDF — blind review is enforced at submission.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

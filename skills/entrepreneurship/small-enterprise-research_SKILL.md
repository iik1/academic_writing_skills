# Journal Writing Skill: Small Enterprise Research

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ENT-SBM
- **Publisher:** Taylor & Francis
- **Scope:** International, peer-reviewed journal (established 1992) publishing rigorous empirical, theoretical, and methodological studies on micro, small, and medium-sized enterprises (MSMEs) and entrepreneurship. Official journal of the Small Enterprise Association of Australia and New Zealand (SEAANZ).
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.tandfonline.com/journals/rser20 (full guidelines inaccessible in this environment; see Sources below)
- **Field baseline:** entrepreneurship-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 4 article titles/metadata (limited accessibility)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict. Note: Full manuscript guidelines were inaccessible during this review; constraints are inferred from Taylor & Francis standards for similar journals, publisher information, and article metadata.

### Structure
Follows Taylor & Francis journal standard structure. Typical empirical article order: Abstract, Keywords, Introduction, Methods/Literature Review, Results, Discussion, Limitations and Future Research, Conclusion, References. Multi-study designs and appendices (sampling procedures, robustness checks, measurement detail) are common and expected.

### Length
General Taylor & Francis norms apply (typically 8,000–12,000 words for full-length research articles); specific word limits for SER were not accessible.

### Abstract
Structured or unstructured abstract (precise format not confirmed, but Taylor & Francis journals typically request 100–250 words). Includes objective, methods, results, and conclusions. Keywords: typically 5–6 keywords required.

### Citations
Taylor & Francis uses Chicago Manual of Style or numeric citation formats (varies by journal); verify on journal submission page. Paraphrase dominates per field norm.

### Headings
Taylor & Francis standard: first-level headings in bold, second-level in bold italics. Sentence case preferred for initial capitals (proper nouns capitalized).

### Tables and Figures
Standard academic format with numbered captions. Figures and tables integrated throughout text (not relegated to end). High citation density in introduction and theory sections; lower in methods and results.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Double-blind peer review: anonymize author and institutional identifying information in manuscript body. No author name, affiliation, or identifying details in main text or running headers.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates from the ENT-SBM baseline.

### Practitioner Orientation
**Relaxation**: Small Enterprise Research explicitly welcomes practitioner-authored contributions and positions itself as serving practitioners, policymakers, and academics equally. Unlike the top-tier journals (ETP, JBV, SEJ) that center researcher-to-researcher positioning, this journal invites "well-supported practitioner or policy perspectives relating to small enterprise and entrepreneurship." This affects framing: practical relevance and actionability may be emphasized earlier and more directly than in pure research articles in the field baseline, though the underlying research rigor and structure remain constant. This is an ABS 1 journal tier, reflecting a more applied/practitioner orientation than the ABS 4*/4 baseline.

### Methodological Pluralism
**Practice Only**: The journal explicitly welcomes qualitative, mixed methods, and quantitative approaches with equal weight. The field baseline (derived from ABS 4* journals) reflects a heavy empirical/quantitative tradition with some qualitative/conceptual alternatives. SER's explicit call for all three methodological traditions suggests a slightly broader methodological welcome than the baseline, though no delta in article structure or voice results from this.

### Scope Specificity
**Relaxation**: The journal's scope explicitly centers MSMEs and entrepreneurship, with no emphasis on other management subfields (as seen in some ABS 4 journals). This is narrower and more focused than the baseline field, which spans multiple business management contexts. A researcher publishing in SER can assume a more specialized, expert audience in MSMEs than in a broad-scope management journal.

- Citation density and paraphrase dominance: FIELD DEFAULT
- Sentence and paragraph style: FIELD DEFAULT (medium to long sentences, dense multi-citation paragraphs in theory sections)
- Methods procedural detail and transparency: FIELD DEFAULT (extensive sampling, screening, and data quality detail)
- Results reporting norms (coefficient precision, effect sizes, significance levels): FIELD DEFAULT

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Identifying information in masked review:** Taylor & Francis uses double-blind review; ensure manuscript body contains no author names, affiliations, or identifying details that could reveal authorship.
2. **Practitioner framing without rigor:** While practitioner contributions are welcome, submissions must still meet full research rigor standards (robust methods, transparent reporting, causal/generalizability limitations acknowledged).
3. **Off-scope submissions:** The journal is specialist in MSMEs and entrepreneurship; submissions in adjacent management subfields (e.g., pure strategy, pure organizational behavior without entrepreneurial context) may be desk-rejected despite strong methods.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

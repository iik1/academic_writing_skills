# Journal Writing Skill: International Journal of Critical Accounting

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ACCOUNT
- **Publisher:** Inderscience Publishers
- **Scope:** Critical perspectives in accounting; accepts theoretical and empirical contributions addressing emerging trends and fast-changing concerns faced by corporations, government, and regulators. Scope includes auditing, political economy of accounting, regulatory frameworks, management accounting, government accountability, and intersections with social issues (gender, class).
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.inderscience.com/jhome.php?jcode=ijca (author guidelines referenced at site but specific formatting details not publicly detailed)
- **Field baseline:** account-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 3 (partial samples; full-text access limited by paywall)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

### Structure
Follows standard Inderscience template structure: Title, Abstract, Keywords, Text (with numbered sections), References, Tables and Figures (end of document or appendix). Briefs and research notes not published; full research articles required.

### Length
Inderscience standard: manuscripts typically 20-25 pages single-spaced or equivalent (journal-specific word limits not explicitly stated in publicly accessible guidelines).

### Abstract
100-150 words (Inderscience standard). Structured format: research question, method/data, key findings, main contribution in single paragraph. Separate Keywords field required (approximately 10-15 words/phrases).

### Citations
Harvard author-date style (Name Year) throughout. In-text citations format: (Author Year) or Author (Year) depending on grammatical role. References alphabetically ordered, full bibliographic detail at end.

### Headings
Publisher template uses Arabic numerals with title-case headings (1. Introduction, 2. Data and Methods, etc.) as standard Inderscience format, though journal-specific section naming conventions not confirmed in available guidelines.

### Tables and Figures
Placed at end of manuscript or in appendix. Standard Inderscience requirement: table and figure captions, variable definitions in notes, explicit reporting of statistical choices.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- No previously published material (English versions only)
- Conference papers must be substantially rewritten and expanded
- Simultaneous submission to other journals prohibited
- All co-authors must declare agreement with submitted content and ethical guidelines
- AI tool usage must be disclosed in Acknowledgements section

### Review Process
Double-blind peer review by 2-3 expert reviewers. Authors must declare 4 proposed reviewers from different institutions and countries.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Scope and Topic Breadth
IJCA explicitly welcomes qualitative, historical, and interpretive work alongside quantitative/empirical studies. The field baseline notes AOS as qualitative exception; IJCA positions critical accounting (inclusive of qualitative traditions) as core identity, not an exception. Articles sampled include historical accounts (e.g., "African accounting: past and present"), not just hypothesis-testing structures.

**Classification:** RELAXATION — Lower-tier journal expands qualitative/historical acceptance beyond field-wide quantitative default.

### Emphasis on Critical Theory and Political Economy
IJCA's scope explicitly lists "political economy of accounting" and "social issues" (gender, class) as central topics. The field baseline does not emphasize critical/political positioning. Articles indicate stronger engagement with institutional critique and normative accounting alternatives than typical ABS 4* accounting journals.

**Classification:** PRACTICE — Observed in article topics and editorial positioning; not a formatting rule, but influences argument structure and framing.

### Abstract Structure
While the field baseline describes abstracts as "structured," it does not specify format details. IJCA follows Inderscience's strict 100-150 word limit (shorter than some ABS 4 journals' 250+ words). This tighter constraint shifts abstract compression.

**Classification:** ENFORCED — Inderscience hard constraint, applicable to IJCA.

- Results reporting: FIELD DEFAULT (tables primary, precise coefficient/significance reporting, parallel organization with hypothesis section)

- Discussion and conclusion: FIELD DEFAULT (merged common, contributions restated, explicit limitations, confident but not overreaching tone)

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Insufficient theoretical positioning for critical angle** — IJCA prioritizes explicit engagement with critical accounting theory or political economy framing. Purely technical empirical work without theoretical/critical context may be perceived as underdeveloped relative to IJCA's scope, even if methodologically sound by ABS 4 standards.

2. **Inadequate qualitative evidence integration** — If submitting interpretive/qualitative work, ensure extended thematic presentation of evidence (quotations, documentary evidence, or fieldwork detail) rather than brief evidence summaries. The field baseline norms (quantitative focus) do not apply to the breadth expected in IJCA's qualitative tradition.

3. **Vague positioning relative to "critical" accounting** — The journal's identity rests on critical perspectives. Articles should explicitly locate their contribution relative to critical accounting literature or normative alternatives, not just empirical findings.

4. **Exceeding abstract word limit** — The 100-150 word limit is strict by Inderscience standard and shorter than many peer journals. Compression required; do not rely on ABS 4 abstract length norms.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

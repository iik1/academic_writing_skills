# Journal Writing Skill: Review of Behavioral Finance

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** FINANCE
- **Publisher:** Emerald Group Publishing
- **Scope:** Behavioral finance research covering how behavioral attributes of decision makers influence financial decision-making, market functioning, portfolio construction, and financial markets. Welcomes empirical, experimental, and theoretical work from finance and adjacent disciplines (psychology, sociology, decision sciences).
- **Guidelines URL:** Author guidelines page access restricted; information derived from Emerald general publishing standards and published articles
- **Field baseline:** finance-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 3 (Blake & Pickles 2022; The Big 5 personality traits and investment biases 2024; Asymmetric and temporal effects of investor sentiment 2026)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
- Required sections follow field baseline (Introduction, Data/Methods, Results, Conclusion, References, Appendix as applicable)
- Section numbering: Arabic numerals (1, 2, 3; subsections 2.1, 2.2, etc.)
- Introduction always unnumbered or labeled "1. INTRODUCTION"
- No separate "Literature Review" section; position within Introduction

### Abstract
- **Structured format (mandatory):** Four to five subsections with bold labels:
  - **Purpose** — the research question or aim
  - **Design/methodology/approach** — methods and data approach
  - **Findings** — key results or discoveries
  - **Originality/value** — contribution to the field (mandatory)
  - *Optional:* Research limitations/implications (if applicable)
- **Length:** 150–250 words maximum
- **Format:** Prose, not bullet points; each subsection as paragraph
- **Placement:** Keywords on separate line(s) after abstract (not embedded in abstract)
- **Keywords:** 5–8 controlled vocabulary terms provided as simple list
- **JEL codes:** Include one or more JEL classification codes (e.g., "JEL: G4") on separate line after keywords

### Length
- Typical article: 5,000–8,000 words (15–25 pages)
- No strict word limit stated in guidelines; check specific call or contact editor

### Citations
- **Format:** Author-year parenthetical (field baseline)
- **Example:** "(Blake and Pickles, 2022)" or "Blake and Pickles (2022) show that…"
- **Direct quotation:** Avoided (paraphrase with citation)
- **Citation density:** Follow field baseline patterns

### Headings
- **Hierarchy:** Bold Arabic numerals with periods (1., 2., 2.1, 2.1.1)
- **Font:** Bold for primary headings; smaller font/non-bold for sub-subsections per Emerald template
- **Casing:** Title case (capitalize first and last words, all major words)
- **Examples from articles:**
  - "1. INTRODUCTION"
  - "2. PERSONAL VALUATION"
  - "2.1 Symmetric personal valuation"
  - "2.2.1 Psychological asymmetry"

### Tables and Figures
- **Placement:** Integrated in-text or grouped after References
- **Captions:** Descriptive titles; for tables, label as "TABLE 1: [Title]" (all caps); for figures, "CHART 1: [Title]" or "FIGURE 1: [Title]"
- **Notes:** Tables include source notes defining variables, samples, and statistical significance thresholds (e.g., "* p<0.10, ** p<0.05, *** p<0.01")
- **Graphics:** Charts may include arrows, labels, and legend; ensure readability in print and PDF

### Reference List
- **Placement:** After text (before appendix if present)
- **Format:** Alphabetical by author surname; follow APA or Emerald house style (check published examples)
- **Examples from Blake & Pickles (2022):**
  - Single author: "Ainslie, G., (2005). Précis of breakdown of will. Behavioral and Brain Sciences, Vol. 28, pp635-673."
  - Multiple authors: "Andersen, S., Harrison, G. W., Lau, M. I., and Rutstrom, E. E. (2008). Eliciting risk and time preferences. Econometrica, Vol. 76, No. 3, pp583-618."
  - Journal articles: Include volume, issue, page range

### Explicit Prohibitions
- **Generative AI restrictions:** Per Emerald policy, do not use generative AI to create or draft new material. Copy-editing for grammar and clarity is permitted; creation of study design, analysis, or text is not.
- **Anonymity:** Do not include information in the manuscript that identifies the author(s) during blind peer review.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates from finance baseline.

### Abstract Format
**ENFORCED DELTA — Structured abstract.** Review of Behavioral Finance mandates a labeled, multi-part abstract (Purpose, Design/methodology/approach, Findings, Originality/value) in prose form. FIELD_SKILL describes abstracts as "concise summary... typically 150–250 words" without prescribing subsection labels. This journal requires explicit labeling and partitioning.

### Keywords and JEL Classification
**ENFORCED DELTA — Keywords and JEL codes as separate elements.** Emerald house style requires keywords (5–8 terms) and JEL classification on separate lines after abstract. FIELD_SKILL does not mention keywords as a hard requirement (they appear "on the published page" but are not described as mandatory in submission).

### Section Numbering Convention
**GUIDELINE ONLY — Emerald prefers Arabic numerals with periods (1., 2., 2.1). FIELD_SKILL notes "Arabic numerals throughout... is the standard" but does not explicitly require periods after numerals. Observed practice in the Blake & Pickles article: "1. INTRODUCTION," "2. PERSONAL VALUATION" (with periods). This is consistent with Emerald house style.

## Common Pitfalls Specific to This Journal

1. **Unstructured abstract.** Submitting a narrative abstract without labeled subsections (Purpose, Design/methodology/approach, Findings, Originality/value) will trigger desk rejection or request for resubmission. The journal requires explicit structure.

2. **Missing keywords and JEL codes.** Ensure keywords appear on a separate line after the abstract and that at least one JEL classification code is included. Failure to provide these metadata fields delays processing.

3. **Inconsistent citation format.** While author-year is standard, ensure consistency in handling multiple authors, date format, and page ranges. Review recent issues for Emerald's house-style preferences on whether to include "Vol.", "No.", "pp" abbreviations.

4. **Overly casual or speculative tone on mechanisms.** While FIELD_SKILL permits measured language for mechanisms ("suggests," "consistent with"), do not drift into excessive speculation or hedging that undermines findings. Balance tentativeness with assertiveness appropriate to the evidence.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

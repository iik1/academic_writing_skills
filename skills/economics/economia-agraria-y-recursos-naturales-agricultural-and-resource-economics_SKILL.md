# Journal Writing Skill: Economia Agraria y Recursos Naturales – Agricultural and Resource Economics

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Universitat Politècnica de València, on behalf of the Spanish Association of Agricultural Economics (Asociación Española de Economía Agraria)
- **Scope:** Bilingual (Spanish/English) journal covering agricultural, food, and natural resource economics, with applied focus on rural development, environmental valuation, agri-food markets, and agricultural policy, primarily (but not exclusively) in Spain and Latin America.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://polipapers.upv.es/public/journals/34/Normas_de_formato.pdf (post-acceptance formatting norms); https://polipapers.upv.es/index.php/EARN/about/submissions (submissions page, partially accessible)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 19 abstracts/titles across 2003–2021 issues, sourced via DOAJ API (ISSN 2174-7350) and IDEAS/RePEc listing, plus one full first-page example from the journal's own formatting guide. Full-text access to polipapers.upv.es was blocked by a TLS certificate error on direct fetch; abstracts and metadata were retrieved successfully via DOAJ's article API and RePEc/IDEAS as a workaround. Full-text prose (body sections, discussion, hedging) could not be directly inspected — findings below are limited to title/abstract/keyword-level and the one full annotated example page provided in the journal's own formatting document.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
First page must contain, in this order: title (Spanish, then English), full author names (not initials), author affiliation as a lettered footnote, abstract in Spanish and English, keywords in Spanish and English, JEL classification (2-digit codes). Main body sections follow standard IMRaD-like structure but are not rigidly prescribed beyond this. A closing "Bibliografía" section is required. Supplementary material goes in an "Anexo" (not "Apéndice"/Appendix).

### Length
Articles: maximum ~25 pages at 1.5 line spacing (~8,000 words), including tables, graphs, annexes, and references. Short notes ("notas breves") up to 2,500 words are also accepted, as are book reviews and invited comments on topical issues. This is markedly shorter than typical FIELD_SKILL (ABS 4*/4) empirical papers.

### Abstract
Bilingual: Spanish abstract (RESUMEN) and English abstract (ABSTRACT), each capped at 100 words maximum — much shorter than typical field-level abstracts. Unstructured (no required subheadings/fields).

### Citations
Author-year in text, consistent with FIELD_SKILL, but with specific journal formatting rules: three or more authors cited as "Martínez et al. (2005)"; page-specific citations formatted as "Pérez (2009; pág. 33)"; parenthetical citations use "véase" (e.g., "(véase López et al., 2010)"). Reference list titled "Bibliografía", alphabetical by author. DOIs are mandatory in references when available — omission blocks indexing of the published article. Specific micro-formatting rules apply: no space between multiple author initials; lowercase initials for common nouns in titles; capitalize after a colon only if the title is in English.

### Headings
Section headings: lowercase, bold, numbered. Subsections: numbered, lowercase, bold italic. Explicit instruction to avoid proliferation of subsections.

### Tables and Figures
Journal-specific terminology is mandatory: use "Cuadros" (not "Tablas") for tables, and "Gráficos" (not "Figuras") for figures/charts; tables must be built as native Word tables. For each graphic, source must always be stated at the bottom (own elaboration marked as "Fuente: Elaboración propia."). A companion Excel file with one tab per graphic (named "Gráfico 1", "Gráfico 2", etc.) containing the underlying data must be submitted alongside the Word file for layout/reproduction. Decimals use commas, not periods; thousands use periods — reverse of Anglophone convention and worth flagging for authors used to English-language formatting.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Do not use "Tabla" for tables (use "Cuadro"); do not use "Figura" for charts (use "Gráfico"); do not use "Apéndice" for supplementary material (use "Anexo"); do not include congress/conference dates in "Comunicaciones en congresos" references; final manuscript must be submitted as a Word file, not PDF.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
The one full example available (journal's own template article) shows a notable split: the English-language abstract uses first-person plural ("we allow," "we estimate," "we account for"), matching FIELD_SKILL's "we" convention, but the parallel Spanish-language abstract for the same paper uses impersonal/passive constructions throughout ("se estiman funciones de costes," "se permite la existencia de," "se utiliza") rather than "we." This suggests Spanish-language submissions to this journal default to the impersonal register standard in Spanish-language academic prose, diverging from FIELD_SKILL's "we"-dominant norm — RELAXATION appropriate to a bilingual, non-Anglophone-core outlet. PRACTICE ONLY (observed in the one available full example; not stated in guidelines).

### Statistical Reporting
Abstracts in the spot-check sample are markedly shorter and less quantitatively specific than the FIELD_SKILL norm of announcing precise magnitudes ("we find that X increases Y by Z percent"). Most sampled EARN abstracts describe methodology and qualitative direction of findings (e.g., "results show a positive relation between intensification and efficiency") without leading with precise point estimates, likely a function of the strict 100-word abstract cap rather than a distinct reporting norm. PRACTICE ONLY / RELAXATION, plausibly driven by the length constraint rather than a discipline norm — cannot confirm full-text statistical reporting practices from abstracts alone.

### Structural Deviations
Journal accepts non-standard piece types beyond full research articles: brief notes (up to 2,500 words), book reviews, and invited "tribuna"/comment pieces on topical debates (confirmed by one sampled item, a "Comentario" on family farming, and a "Prólogo" tribute piece). This mix of formats is broader than the FIELD_SKILL scope, which addresses only standard research articles. GUIDELINE-CONFIRMED (stated in submissions material and observed in sample).

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Topical/geographic scope is narrower and more applied than FIELD_SKILL journals: nearly all sampled abstracts concern Spain, Latin America, or other regions' agricultural, water, land, or rural-development policy questions, often using choice experiments, contingent valuation, DEA, or hedonic pricing rather than structural or theoretical modeling. This is a scope observation, not a writing-style delta, and does not override FIELD_SKILL's structural/rhetorical guidance.

## Common Pitfalls
- Using "Tabla"/"Figura"/"Apéndice" instead of the required "Cuadro"/"Gráfico"/"Anexo" — this is enforced and distinctive to this journal.
- Exceeding the 100-word abstract cap (both languages) or omitting either the Spanish or English abstract/keyword set.
- Submitting the final Word file without the companion Excel workbook of graph data, or without DOIs in references where available — the guidelines state this blocks indexing and DOI assignment for the published article.
- Using period-decimals in tables/text instead of the required comma-decimal, period-thousands convention.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

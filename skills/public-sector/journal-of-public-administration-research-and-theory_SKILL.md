# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Public Administration Research and Theory

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 4
- **Field:** PUB SEC
- **Publisher:** Oxford University Press (for the Public Management Research Association)
- **Scope:** International, multidisciplinary journal publishing theoretical and empirical research on organizational, administrative, managerial, and policy-based aspects of the public sector.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://academic.oup.com/jpart/pages/Instructions_To_Authors
- **Field baseline:** public-sector-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 15 (abstract/front-matter level; full text paywalled). Vol 35 Iss 1 (Jan 2025): "Will trust move mountains?"; "Making administrative work matter"; "Does enforcement style influence citizen trust..."; "Gendered administrative burden" (Herd & Moynihan); "A reputational perspective on structural reforms"; "Procedural politicking for what?"; "The professional profile, competence, and responsiveness of senior bureaucrats" (Askim et al.). Vol 35 Iss 3 (Jul 2025): "How ensembling AI and public managers improves decision-making"; "Patterns of bureaucracy" (Jugl); "Do governance platforms achieve the aims of the platform sponsor?"; "Routines amid the unpredictable" (Wong); "Governance in layers... emotional labor" (Zhang); "Financial performance of state-owned enterprises... political ideology"; "Exploring the influence of administrative capacities on administrative burdens"; "Breaking the rules, but for whom?" Plus Vol 34 Iss 2 (2024): "The relationship between how agencies work together and coordinated outcomes."

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Title page submitted separately (anonymized main manuscript): full title, author names/affiliations, word count, funding sources, human-subjects permissions, acknowledgments. Main PDF order: Abstract → Keywords → Main text → References → Appendices → Data Availability Statement (required for every submission). No separate files for figures/tables/appendices — everything embeds in one PDF.

### Length
Maximum 12,000 words including abstract, tables, and references. 12-point font, double-spaced, page numbers on every page.

### Abstract
Unstructured (no labeled fields) but must explicitly cover theoretical approach, methodology, data, results, findings, and implications for theory in prose. Optional translated abstract (in authors' native language) permitted after the English version — observed in practice (Zhang 2025 includes a Chinese abstract).

### Citations
Author-date, e.g., "(Author date)"; reference list alphabetical. A valid DOI is required for every reference at submission (checked for accuracy) — stricter than typical FIELD_SKILL practice, which does not specify DOI verification. Data citations tagged "[dataset]" with authors, year, title, publisher, identifier.

### Headings
Not prescribed by name; observed practice varies widely by paper (thematic, case-first, or theory-first headings), consistent with FIELD_SKILL's flexibility. No universal "Hypothesis 1:" convention was visible in the spot-checked abstracts, though numbered/labeled hypotheses appear in some quantitative papers — treat as FIELD DEFAULT, not a JPART-specific requirement.

### Tables and Figures
Embed within text for review (not as separate files). Table notes must explain codes, model specifications, acronyms. Exact p-values or confidence intervals required (not just stars). Accepted-manuscript figure resolution: 1200 dpi (line art) or 300 dpi (color/halftone), .tif; color free online, grayscale in print; alt text required under each figure legend.

### Explicit Prohibitions
No simultaneous submission elsewhere. No separate submission of figures/appendices outside the single PDF. Manuscript must be fully anonymized during review: omit author names and funding info; cite own prior work in third person.

### Other
Minimum 3 / maximum 5 keywords, with a specific taxonomy: the first three must indicate theory, research theme, and method respectively; the remaining two may specify dataset, data source, or context (example given: "Principal-agent theory, local government, interviews, mayors, cities"). This keyword taxonomy is a JPART-specific structural requirement with no FIELD_SKILL analogue. Preregistration links (anonymized) required where applicable. Human-subjects approval note/ID required on the cover sheet.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Citation Density
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.

### Voice and Register
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm — hedged, first-person-plural framing observed consistently (e.g., "may alleviate ethnic biases," "practically indifferent," "suggests... rather than purely political motivations").

### Statistical Reporting
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm; DOI-verified citation requirement (see Hard Constraints) is the only reporting-adjacent delta, and it is a citation-mechanics rule, not a statistical-reporting one.

### Structural Deviations
None beyond the keyword taxonomy and single-PDF submission rule already captured under Hard Constraints. Section structure across the 15 spot-checked papers is heterogeneous (thematic headings for qualitative/conceptual work, theory-then-method-then-results for quantitative work) — this variability itself matches FIELD_SKILL's statement that no single structural orthodoxy holds.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Optional non-English abstract translation (confirmed in practice, not just stated) is a distinguishing feature not present in FIELD_SKILL.

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting keywords as a generic list rather than following the required theory/theme/method-first ordering (GUIDELINE ONLY — stated explicitly, easy to overlook).
- Exceeding the 12,000-word all-inclusive cap by treating tables/references as external to the count.
- Failing to anonymize third-person self-citation, or leaving funding acknowledgments in the main file rather than the separate title page.
- Omitting the Data Availability Statement, which is mandatory even for qualitative/interview-based work (confirmed as a fixed required section, not optional).

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

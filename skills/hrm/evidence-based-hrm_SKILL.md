# Journal Writing Skill: Evidence-based HRM

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** HRM&EMP
- **Publisher:** Emerald Group Publishing Limited
- **Scope:** A Global Forum for Empirical Scholarship promoting empirical evidence-based research in human resource management using econometric and statistical methods to fill the gap between conceptual arguments and observed data.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.emeraldgrouppublishing.com/products/journals/author_guidelines.htm?id=EBHRM (access restricted; information compiled from search results and publicly accessible documentation)
- **Field baseline:** hrm-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 3 (Lange 2013 editorial; 2 recent 2024–2025 articles from search results; limited access due to paywall)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows standard journal article structure (title, abstract, keywords, introduction, literature review/methods integration, results/findings, discussion, conclusion). All manuscripts must be submitted through ScholarOne editorial system via Emerald website only—email or third-party submission is not accepted.

### Length
Standard journal article length (specific word or page limits not publicly accessible; check journal homepage). Word count includes abstract, references, and text in tables and figures.

### Abstract
**Structured abstract format (mandatory).** Four mandatory subsections:
- **Purpose** (states the research question or objective)
- **Design/methodology/approach** (describes research design, methods, sample)
- **Findings** (presents key results)
- **Originality/value** (states contribution to theory or practice)

Three optional subsections (omit if not applicable):
- Research limitations/implications
- Practical implications  
- Social implications

**Maximum 250 words total** for the entire abstract across all subsections.

### Citations
APA citation format (inferred from Emerald publishing standards). References with three to five authors: cite all authors on first citation; at subsequent citations, use first author followed by et al. All parenthetical citations should be in alphabetical order.

### Headings
Not explicitly specified in publicly accessible guidelines. Assume FIELD_SKILL heading conventions apply (hierarchical numbering or bold/italics conventions typical in HRM journals).

### Tables and Figures
Must be integrated into manuscript; word count includes text in tables and figures. Standard academic presentation (check FIELD_SKILL for HRM conventions).

### Explicit Prohibitions and Requirements
- **Ethics and informed consent:** Mandatory for primary data collection. Statement must include institutional review board/ethics committee name and location, approval number, approval date, national/international guidelines followed, and confirmation of informed consent.
- **AI tool usage:** Must disclose any use of generative AI tools (name and version). Authors must describe content created or modified by AI and cite all sources. **Copywriting (including abstracts and literature reviews) using AI/LLM tools is not permissible.**
- **Originality:** All manuscripts must be original and not previously published.
- **Research and publishing ethics:** Manuscripts must comply with journal's research and publishing ethics guidelines.
- **Transparency and reproducibility:** Journal is a signatory of the Transparency and Openness Promotion (TOP) Guidelines. Reproducible research practices are expected.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structured Abstract Format
**ENFORCED.** Evidence-based HRM mandates a structured abstract with four mandatory subsections (Purpose, Design/methodology/approach, Findings, Originality/value) and up to three optional subsections. This is more prescriptive than FIELD_SKILL, which does not specify abstract format. The 250-word maximum and subsection structure are journal-specific hard requirements that override any abstract norms in FIELD_SKILL.

### Explicit Emphasis on Empirical Robustness and Analytical Rigour
**PRACTICE AND GUIDELINE.** The journal's founding statement emphasizes three pillars: "empirical robustness, analytical rigour and practical significance." While FIELD_SKILL mentions that quantitative papers report "robustness checks" and "endogeneity" discussions, Evidence-based HRM places particular emphasis on econometric and statistical methods as a core mission. Manuscripts using weaker or informal statistical approaches may receive more critical scrutiny than in a typical ABS 4/4* journal. This is a **relaxation** in that Evidence-based HRM (ABS 1) actively encourages rigorous quantitative work but does not enforce the sophistication level demanded by higher-tier journals; mixed-methods and qualitative work are published but may be less central.

### Ethical Approval and Reproducibility Requirements
**ENFORCED.** The journal requires explicit ethical approval statements for primary data collection (naming IRB/ethics committee, approval number, date, guidelines followed, informed consent confirmation). This is more explicit than FIELD_SKILL's reference to "ethical approval statements are a near-universal requirement." Additionally, as a TOP Guidelines signatory, the journal expects transparency in research methods and reproducibility practices. Authors should be prepared to justify methodological choices and, if applicable, commit to open data or code sharing.

### AI Disclosure
**ENFORCED (modern requirement).** Not mentioned in FIELD_SKILL. Evidence-based HRM requires disclosure of any AI/LLM tool usage with tool name and version, description of content created or modified, and citation of sources used by the AI. **Critical prohibition: copywriting abstracts or literature reviews using AI is not permissible.** This is a modern constraint reflecting journal policy on AI use in academic publishing.

### Submission and Revision Process
**ENFORCED.** All submissions must be made through ScholarOne only; email or third-party agents are not accepted. This is a procedural constraint not covered in FIELD_SKILL.

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Voice and register:** FIELD DEFAULT. Formal, impersonal academic register with first-person plural ("we") permitted in introduction, methods, discussion; passive constructions common in methods and literature summaries.
- **Sentence and paragraph style:** FIELD DEFAULT. Medium to long sentences with frequent subordination; paragraph-level organization around claims.
- **Argumentation and rhetorical moves:** FIELD DEFAULT. CARS-like introduction (establishing importance, identifying gap, stating contribution); contributions framed in relation to theory and practice.
- **Methods conventions:** FIELD DEFAULT. Quantitative papers report sample, measures, estimation strategy; robustness checks and endogeneity discussion expected. Qualitative papers report sampling, data collection, analytic approach, and reflexivity. (Note: Evidence-based HRM's emphasis on econometric rigour may tilt editorial preferences toward quantitative submissions, but conventions are not formally distinct.)
- **Results and reporting norms:** FIELD DEFAULT. Quantitative: findings narrated in prose with reference to tables; coefficients and statistics in tables. Qualitative: thematic organization with illustrative quotations and interpretive commentary.
- **Discussion and conclusion norms:** FIELD DEFAULT. Revisit contributions, distinguish theory from practice/policy implications; close with limitations and future research directions.
- **Citation and literature engagement:** FIELD DEFAULT. Paraphrase preferred over quotation; diplomatic framing of disagreement; citing seminal theoretical frameworks alongside recent empirical work.
- **Hedging and epistemic caution:** FIELD DEFAULT. Moderate to high hedging, especially in discussion and conclusion; causal language qualified in observational designs; generalisability caveated in qualitative work.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Submitting through email or third-party agents.** Only ScholarOne submissions via Emerald website are accepted. Manuscripts submitted outside the official system will be desk-rejected.

2. **AI-generated abstracts or literature reviews.** Submitting an abstract or literature review entirely or substantially written by generative AI/LLM will result in rejection. Disclosure alone does not permit copywriting of these sections.

3. **Missing or inadequate ethics statements for primary data.** If your study involves primary data collection, omitting or vaguely describing the ethics approval (IRB name, approval number, date, informed consent) will trigger revision requests or rejection. Be explicit and complete.

4. **Assuming weak statistical methods are acceptable.** Evidence-based HRM's founding mission emphasizes econometric and statistical rigour. Quantitative papers using only basic descriptive statistics or overly simple regression without robustness checks may face editorial skepticism. If you are using qualitative or mixed-methods approaches, ensure rigour through other means (reflexivity, triangulation, explicit analytic procedure).

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: International Entrepreneurship and Management Journal

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ENT-SBM
- **Publisher:** Springer Nature
- **Scope:** The journal publishes original research on entrepreneurship in its broadest sense and the management of entrepreneurial organizations, across diverse organizational contexts. Accepts Original Articles, Empirical Articles, Case Studies, and Theoretical Articles.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://link.springer.com/journal/11365/submission-guidelines (guidelines accessible to registered users only; public information extracted from secondary sources)
- **Field baseline:** entrepreneurship-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 (limited access; based on IDEAS/RePEc index, article titles from Springer, and abstract/structure information from secondary sources)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows Springer standard IMRaD (Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion) with adaptations. Articles may be structured as: Title, Abstract, Keywords, Introduction, Theory/Literature Review, Methods, Results, Discussion, Limitations and Future Research, Conclusion. Conceptual and review articles may substitute thematic structure but retain core sections.

### Length
No explicit word-count limit stated in publicly available guidelines. Springer journal norms generally range 7,000–12,000 words for empirical articles, though IEMJ does not enforce a strict maximum based on available information. Most published articles appear to be 15–40 pages in length.

### Abstract
Structured or unstructured abstract format acceptable (journal does not mandate structured format in publicly available guidelines). **Word limit: 150–250 words.** Must not contain undefined abbreviations or unspecified references.

### Keywords
**Requirement: 4–6 keywords** for indexing purposes. Keywords should be specific and represent the content of the manuscript.

### Citations
Author-date citation system with parenthetical in-text citations (e.g., "Marx 2015"; "Boal et al. 2000"). Full reference list at end in Harvard/Author-date format. References must include full details: author(s), year, title, journal/publisher, volume, issue, pages, and DOI where applicable.

### Headings
Follows standard Springer hierarchy; level 1 headings (Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion, Conclusion) typically centered or bold; lower-level headings (subsections within Methods or Results) formatted hierarchically. Manuscript templates on Overleaf follow Springer conventions.

### Tables and Figures
Tables should be created using built-in functions, not tabs or spreadsheets. Figures should be saved as EPS with embedded fonts or original Microsoft Office formats; photographs/scanned images require minimum 300 dpi (TIFF). All tables and figures must have captions and be referenced in text. Alt text for accessibility is encouraged.

### Statements and Declarations
**Required:** Manuscripts must include a section titled "Statements and Declarations" that discloses financial or non-financial interests directly or indirectly related to the work. Submissions lacking relevant declarations will be returned as incomplete.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Corresponding Author must ensure all listed authors have approved the manuscript before submission and must disclose material re-use and conflicts of interest. Adding/deleting authors during revision is generally not permitted (changes require justification and cannot occur post-acceptance). Substantial changes in content after online publication require editor approval; further changes only via Erratum.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Relaxation in Word Length
FIELD_SKILL does not specify word limits, noting that journals vary. IEMJ, as an ABS 1 journal, does not enforce a published strict maximum word count (in contrast to many ABS 4*/4 journals which may cap articles at 8,000–10,000 words). **RELAXATION:** This allows for longer methods appendices and more extensive supplementary analyses without strict space constraints, though conciseness is still valued.

### Citation Style Requirement
FIELD_SKILL describes citation density and practices (high density in intro/theory, moderate in discussion). IEMJ enforces a specific citation format: **author-date (Harvard) system with parenthetical in-text citations,** which is a FIELD_SKILL-aligned norm but worth stating explicitly to avoid using numbered citations or footnote styles common in some entrepreneurship venues.

### Explicit Compliance Statements
FIELD_SKILL does not specify required ethical/compliance statements. IEMJ mandates a "Statements and Declarations" section disclosing conflicts of interest and material re-use. This is **ENFORCED** and is a journal-specific procedural requirement not covered by FIELD_SKILL baseline.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Omitting or incomplete "Statements and Declarations" section:** Manuscripts returned as incomplete if ethical disclosures and conflict-of-interest statements are missing. Ensure all co-authors provide signed disclosures before submission.

2. **Exceeding abstract word limit or including undefined abbreviations/unspecified references in abstract:** The 150–250-word requirement is strictly enforced; abstracts with acronyms (e.g., "SME") without definition or citations without context are flagged for revision before peer review.

3. **Author list changes after initial submission:** The journal discourages adding or removing authors during revision. If changes are necessary, provide detailed justification; changes cannot occur after acceptance.

4. **Inconsistent or non-standard citation format:** Springer Link's system enforces author-date format. Numbered citations or footnote-based references will require reformatting, delaying processing.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

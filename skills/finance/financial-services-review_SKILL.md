# Journal Writing Skill: Financial Services Review

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** FINANCE
- **Publisher:** Academy of Financial Services (Diamond Open Access, hosted on University of Georgia OJS platform)
- **Scope:** "The Journal of Individual Financial Management." Focuses on the individual/household perspective on financial planning, financial counseling, financial literacy, banking services, employee benefits, estate and tax planning, insurance planning, investments, pension/retirement planning, and real estate. Explicitly welcomes practitioner-oriented and pedagogical contributions alongside empirical research.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://openjournals.libs.uga.edu/fsr/about/submissions (Author Guidelines section) — accessible
- **Field baseline:** finance-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 (abstract/landing-page level; full text behind non-extractable PDF rendering). Titles: "Perception Bias in the Financial Knowledge of American Adults" (Quadria & Lacombe); "Dissipation of Inheritance Windfalls and the Case for Time-Phased Transfers" (Thompson & James); "Using the Four-Quadrant Model of Think/Feel/Fast/Slow... Superannuation Fund Trustees" (Colbeck, D'Alessandro & Minas); "Financial Satisfaction and Anxiety: Unpacking Resilience Among Asian Americans" (Liu, White & Pearson); "Assessing the Impact of Rebalancing on Equal-weighted and Value-weighted Portfolios over Five Decades"; "The Changing Assessment of Risk for Young Investors."

**Access limitation:** Full-text PDFs on this platform did not render extractable body text via automated fetch (only metadata/abstract pages were retrievable), so heading-by-heading structural confirmation and in-text hedging/citation-density checks rely on abstract-page content and the guidelines page rather than full manuscripts. This is disclosed per the good-faith-sample allowance for ABS 1 journals.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Required sections, in order: Title; Abstract (max 250 words); Introduction (with explicit contribution statement); Background/Literature Review; Theoretical Framework (if applicable); Methodology (Data/Sample, Variables, Data Analysis); Results and Discussion; Implications (practical and theoretical contributions); Conclusions (must address limitations and future research); References (APA 7th edition). Results and Discussion are combined into one section rather than separated, and a distinct "Implications" section is required — both differences from the FIELD_SKILL pattern of a standalone Results section followed by a short Conclusion.

### Length
No explicit total word/page limit stated beyond the abstract cap. Abstract: maximum 250 words (FIELD_SKILL notes 150–250 as typical; this journal sets a hard ceiling at the top of that range, no lower bound given).

### Abstract
Single unstructured paragraph, max 250 words. Title page (separate from abstract) must include the title, at least one JEL classification code, and up to five keywords — JEL code is explicitly required at submission, though it was not visible on any of the six spot-checked public abstract landing pages (likely stripped/relocated on the OJS display, not omitted from the manuscript itself).

### Citations
APA 7th edition (author-date), consistent with FIELD_SKILL's author-year convention. No delta.

### Headings
No specific heading-level typography rules stated beyond following the required section order above.

### Tables and Figures
Numbered consecutively, embedded in text at the appropriate point (not collected at the end) — this is an explicit requirement, not merely a convention, at this journal. Clear titles and explanatory notes required. Equations/formulas numbered consecutively as (1), (2)... flush to the right margin.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- No previously published work; no simultaneous submission elsewhere without disclosure to the editor.
- Purely theoretical contributions lacking demonstrable practical implications are explicitly discouraged/unlikely to be accepted — a notable relaxation-in-reverse from FIELD_SKILL: this journal requires practical relevance even for theory-adjacent papers.
- Footnotes "should be kept to a minimum" — roughly one or fewer per two pages.
- An AI Disclosure Statement must be submitted with the manuscript.
- Double-blind review: no author-identifying information in the manuscript body; separate cover/title page required.

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting a purely theoretical or methodological paper without an explicit practical-implications discussion — this journal's guidelines flag such papers as unlikely to be accepted, more so than typical FIELD_SKILL-level finance journals.
- Placing tables/figures at the end of the manuscript rather than embedded in-text — explicitly against this journal's formatting rules.
- Omitting the required "Implications" section as a section distinct from Results and Discussion.
- Excess footnotes (journal enforces a "one or fewer per two pages" norm).

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

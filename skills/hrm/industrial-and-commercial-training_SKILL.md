# Journal Writing Skill: Industrial and Commercial Training

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** HRM&EMP
- **Publisher:** Emerald Publishing Limited
- **Scope:** Topical, evidence-based analysis of contemporary training and development affecting practice delivery in organizational settings. Emphasis on bridging theory and practice for HR professionals, practitioners, and organizational development specialists.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.emeraldgrouppublishing.com/products/journals/author_guidelines.htm?id=ict (access restricted; guidelines inferred from Emerald standard practices)
- **Field baseline:** hrm-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 (via Emerald abstracts: "Work engagement interventions linked to employee learning and improvement" 56(3); "Training needs assessment in the IT industry" 58(1); "Servant leadership and employee engagement" 58(2); "A practical analysis of blended training efficacy" 54(4); "Bridging the skills gap divide in manufacturing" 56(1); "The Kirkpatrick model for training evaluation" 54(1))

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
IMRaD macro-structure standard: introduction, methods/design, results/findings, discussion, conclusion. Acknowledgements and ethics statements as back matter.

### Length
Journal-specific word limit not publicly accessible; Emerald journals typically set 4,000–8,000 word parameters. Word count includes structured abstract, references, and all text in tables/figures (add 280 words per figure/table).

### Abstract
**Mandatory structured abstract, maximum 250 words total.** Sub-headings required:
- Purpose (mandatory)
- Design/methodology/approach (mandatory)
- Findings (mandatory)
- Research limitations/implications (if applicable)
- Practical implications (if applicable)
- Social implications (if applicable)
- Originality/value (mandatory)

Each section brief and declarative.

### Keywords
Not explicitly specified in accessible guidelines; Emerald standard is 5–6 keywords following abstract.

### Citations
**Harvard reference style (Emerald variant).** Author-date parenthetical citations in text. For three or more authors, use "et al" (italicized). Two authors: "and" not "&". Direct quotation avoided; paraphrase preferred. Emerald format example: Author, A. (Year), 'Title of article', *Publication Title*, Vol. X No. Y, pp. X–Y.

### Headings
Not explicitly specified in accessible guidelines; standard practice is numbered main headings (1. Introduction, 2. Methods, etc.) with subheadings as needed.

### Tables and Figures
Standard academic conventions; no journal-specific deviations accessible.

### Explicit Prohibitions and Requirements
- **Anonymity:** Manuscript must be anonymous; no author names or identifying information in text, figures, or captions. Use "previous research" not "our previous research."
- **Ethical Approval:** Must declare whether ethical approval was required and obtained if research involves human participants.
- **Generative AI:** Copywriting any part of the article (including abstract, literature review) using generative AI or LLMs is **not permissible.** Author(s) must be responsible for accuracy, integrity, and validity. Use of AI tools for analysis may be disclosed but must not replace authorial writing.
- **Open Access:** Articles published under Creative Commons CC BY 4.0 license.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Practitioner Emphasis and Anti-Theory Stance
**RELAXATION of field norm, enforced editorial policy.** FIELD_SKILL describes balanced practical/theoretical positioning. Industrial and Commercial Training explicitly deprioritizes abstract theory in favour of direct practitioner applicability. Editorial guidance states: "too much training theory takes too long to read and may not have immediate practical advantages." Practical implications section in structured abstract is weighted more heavily than conceptual contribution. Articles typically foreground application, implementation challenges, and immediate organizational relevance. Theory is framed instrumentally (as justification for practice recommendations) rather than as an end in itself. Contributions are expected to "spell out the relevance and implications of their studies for those involved in training."

**Classification:** ENFORCED (editorial policy evident in journal scope and article selection patterns).

### Structured Abstract Format
**FIELD DEFAULT with journal-specific requirement.** FIELD_SKILL specifies abstract conventions but does not mandate structured format. Industrial and Commercial Training requires the Emerald-standard 7-field structured abstract (Purpose, Design, Findings, Limitations, Practical implications, Social implications, Originality/value). This is a hard constraint deviating from field norm flexibility.

**Classification:** ENFORCED (stated in Emerald guidelines and mandatory).

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Overly theoretical framing without immediate practical application.** Reviewers and editors expect clear, early statement of how findings translate to practitioner action. Theoretical discussion should justify and support practice recommendations, not dominate.
2. **Weak practical implications section.** In structured abstract and discussion, "practical implications" must be concrete and actionable—e.g., "training design should prioritize X" not "more research is needed on implications."
3. **Identifying author information in manuscript.** Self-citation or author identification (even oblique—"as shown in our previous work") violates anonymity requirement and triggers desk rejection or round-trip revision.
4. **Using generative AI for abstract or literature review sections.** Emerald explicitly prohibits LLM-generated copywriting. Even partial use (e.g., LLM draft polished by hand) exposes authors to ethical concerns during review.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

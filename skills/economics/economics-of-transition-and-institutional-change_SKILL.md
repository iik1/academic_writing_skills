# Journal Writing Skill: Economics of Transition and Institutional Change

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** John Wiley & Sons
- **Scope:** Institutions and institutional change in transition and developing economies, with emphasis on formerly socialist countries, China, East/South-East Asia, Middle East/North Africa, and regions undergoing economic reform
- **Guidelines URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/page/journal/14680351/homepage/guide.htm (paywalled; guidelines extracted from search results)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 3 (titles from Vol 33, 2024-2025 issues: "Social trust and formal institutions" Bagchi & Majumder; "Price and welfare effects of tariffs" Moncarz; "Export product quality and inclusivity" Gnangnon)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows field default. Typical structure: Introduction, Background/Literature, Methods/Empirical Strategy, Results, Conclusion, with optional sections for Robustness and Extensions. A summary of article structure must be included at the end of the introduction; it should trace the argument's logic, not merely list sections.

### Length
**ENFORCED: Manuscripts must not exceed 10,000 words including tables, figures, and footnotes.** This is a strict hard limit and represents a significant contraction from top-tier journal norms. Budget carefully for empirical papers.

### Abstract
**ENFORCED: Approximately 100 words, structured format required.** Must include:
- Abstract text (~100 words)
- Keywords (3–6 terms)
- JEL classification codes (e.g., O16, P21)

Abstracts must be uploaded separately during submission. A standalone abstract page is expected.

### Citations
**Author-date (Harvard) style, parenthetical or in-text.** In-text: "Kornai (1990) argued...". Parenthetical: "...not found (see, for example, Kornai, 1990)". All references alphabetically by author in reference list. This aligns with FIELD_SKILL; no deviation.

### Headings
**ENFORCED: No more than three levels of headings.** This is a specific constraint not stated in FIELD_SKILL. Heading numbering (Roman or Arabic) is acceptable; style should match recent journal issues.

### Tables and Figures
**Tables:** Must fit comfortably on a single journal page (landscape orientation acceptable). Table notes should explain variable definitions, sample restrictions, and clustering of standard errors if applicable.

**Figures:** Originated in a drawing package, saved as EPS files. Must be produced in **black and white only**; tints and complex shading must be avoided. Captions should be concise and self-contained.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Figures must not use color or grayscale tints.
- Manuscripts exceeding 10,000 words will not be reviewed.
- Language must be clear and grammatically correct English, checked by a native speaker.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Word Limit (Relaxation)
FIELD_SKILL describes ABS 4*/4 practice without specifying an upper bound; top-tier outlets typically accommodate 12,000–15,000+ words. This journal enforces 10,000 words maximum. This is a **RELAXATION** (ABS 2 tier), not a deviation in voice or structure. Budget prose, robustness checks, and appendix material accordingly.

### Abstract Format (Tightened)
FIELD_SKILL does not specify abstract structure. This journal requires a **structured abstract** with keywords and JEL codes explicitly stated. The 100-word target is tighter than some field outlets. Keywords and JEL codes are not optional. **ENFORCED.**

### Heading Hierarchy (New Constraint)
FIELD_SKILL does not mandate a maximum heading depth. This journal explicitly limits headings to three levels. **ENFORCED.** Plan section architecture to avoid deep nesting.

### Figure Format (Technical)
FIELD_SKILL does not specify file format or color constraints. This journal requires **EPS format, black and white only**. Color figures will require conversion; plan accordingly. **ENFORCED.**

### Manuscript Submission Format
Accepted final manuscripts must be submitted in both original format (Microsoft Word or LaTeX) and as PDF. This is a **submission portal requirement**, not a style norm. Standard at Wiley journals.

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Voice and Register:** Follows FIELD_SKILL. First-person plural ("we find," "we argue") is standard; active voice is preferred. Formality and technical vocabulary density match field norms.
- **Sentence and Paragraph Style:** Follows FIELD_SKILL. Medium-to-long sentences, dense paragraphs (4–10 sentences typical), high technical vocabulary density are all consistent with FIELD_SKILL norms.
- **Argumentation Structure:** Follows FIELD_SKILL. Introduction includes motivating hook, research question, approach, preview of results, contributions, and roadmap. Positioning against prior work is required.
- **Methods and Identification:** Follows FIELD_SKILL. Identification strategy must be stated clearly; robustness checks are standard.
- **Results and Statistical Reporting:** Follows FIELD_SKILL. Integration of results and interpretation; quantitative specificity required (e.g., "increased by approximately X%").
- **Citation Density:** Follows FIELD_SKILL. High in Introduction, medium in Methods, low-to-medium in Results, low in Conclusion.
- **Hedging:** Follows FIELD_SKILL. Low overall; clear claims preferred; hedging used only for external validity caveats, local average treatment effects, and data limitations.
- **Conclusion:** Follows FIELD_SKILL. Restates findings, draws implications, acknowledges limitations, suggests future research. One to three pages typical; no new results introduced.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Exceeding 10,000 words:** With tables and footnotes counted, manuscripts frequently overshoot. Trim aggressively; move robustness checks and secondary analyses to an appendix. Editors enforce this limit strictly.

2. **Unstructured or oversized abstract:** Submitting an abstract without explicit keywords and JEL codes will trigger desk rejection or reviewer requests for revision. Keywords and codes are mandatory, not optional.

3. **Figures in color or with tints:** Black-and-white-only figures are non-negotiable for publication. If you submit color figures, they will be rejected at proofs and require resubmission, delaying publication.

4. **Excessive heading levels:** Nesting more than three heading levels confuses the page layout and is explicitly discouraged. Flatten hierarchy by combining subsections or moving nested content to an appendix.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

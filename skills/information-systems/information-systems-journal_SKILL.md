# Journal Writing Skill: Information Systems Journal

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 4
- **Field:** INFO MAN
- **Publisher:** Wiley-Blackwell
- **Scope:** International IS journal publishing a broad range of impactful
  information systems research spanning technological, social,
  organisational, and managerial dimensions; explicitly interdisciplinary.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/page/journal/13652575/homepage/forauthors.html
  (Wiley page returned HTTP 402 to automated fetch; guidance below is

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
ISJ publishes several distinct manuscript types, each with different
structural expectations — this is the journal's defining feature, not
present in FIELD_SKILL:
- **Research Article** — the standard empirical paper; must reference
  theory and specify epistemological/ontological stance explicitly.
- **Practitioner Paper (PP)** — same overall structure as a Research
  Article but written by/with non-academic practitioners; de-emphasises
  theoretical depth in favour of practical value, frameworks, and
  guidelines practitioners can act on.
- **Research Opinion (RO)** — authors take a position on a topic of
  broad significance to the IS discipline (methods, theory,
  epistemology, or a phenomenon); expected to be "trenchant and
  persuasive," explicitly prescriptive and provocative rather than
  hedged and balanced.
- **Research Methods Article (RMA)** — devoted to methodological issues
  and their enhancement.
- **Research Commentary** — short piece critically analysing one or
  more previously published ISJ articles; capped at 3,000 words; if
  accepted, the original authors are invited to a brief reply (max 500
  words).
- **Research Impact** — retrospective reflection on the real-world
  impact of an article the same authors published in ISJ more than five
  years earlier; less theoretical grounding expected than a standard
  paper; length flexible (brief reflection to full paper).

### Length
- Research Articles (standard): should not normally exceed 10,000
  words, excluding references and appendices.
- Practitioner Papers: should not normally exceed 8,000 words.
- Research Commentaries: maximum 3,000 words (plus a max-500-word
  author reply if invited).
- Research Impact pieces: no fixed cap; can range from short reflection
  to full-length paper.

### Abstract
No structured/labeled-field abstract format found in guidelines or
sampled articles; abstracts observed are single unstructured paragraphs,
consistent with FIELD_SKILL default.

### Citations
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.

### Headings
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm for Research Articles. Research Opinions and
Research Commentaries are shorter, argument-driven pieces and do not
follow the empirical-paper section sequence (no separate Methods/Results
sections observed in the Wessel "Quo Vadit?" opinion piece).

### Tables and Figures
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Manuscript files must not be security-protected and must be
  anonymised (author identity stripped from document properties, not
  just the manuscript text).
- No simultaneous submission elsewhere; if previously rejected at
  another journal, guidelines expect substantial revision before ISJ
  submission, not a resubmission of the same text.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
Research Opinion pieces (e.g., Wessel et al. "Digital Transformation:
Quo Vadit?"; Riemer et al. on GenAI methodology) adopt a more overtly
argumentative, first-person authorial voice than FIELD_SKILL's default
— explicitly framed by the journal as "prescriptive" and "provocative,"
challenging "current orthodoxy" rather than the more measured,
hedged register FIELD_SKILL describes for empirical papers. This
register shift is specific to the RO/Commentary manuscript types, not
the standard Research Article, which follows FIELD_SKILL norm.

### Structural Deviations
The multiplicity of manuscript types with materially different
structures (see Hard Constraints) is itself the primary journal-specific
delta. A generic empirical-paper template (Intro–Lit Review–Method–
Results–Discussion) applies only to Research Articles and Practitioner
Papers; Research Opinions, Commentaries, and Research Impact pieces
depart from it by design.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Research Articles are explicitly required to "specify epistemological
and ontological stance" — a more explicit and named requirement than
FIELD_SKILL's general note that methods sections should give enough
detail for assessment/replication. This appears to be stated policy
rather than something confirmed as universal practice across the
sampled articles (full methods sections were not accessible), so it is
classified GUIDELINE ONLY / PRACTICE UNCONFIRMED.

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting a standard empirical-paper structure for a Research
  Opinion or Commentary — these manuscript types are judged on
  argumentative force, not hypothesis-testing rigor.
- Exceeding the 3,000-word cap on Research Commentaries, or missing that
  Practitioner Papers have a tighter 8,000-word limit than the general
  10,000-word Research Article limit.
- Leaving author metadata in document properties — ISJ's anonymisation
  requirement extends beyond the manuscript text to file properties.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

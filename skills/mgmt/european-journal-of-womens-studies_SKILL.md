# Journal Writing Skill: European Journal of Women's Studies

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN (Gender Studies, Women's Studies, Feminist Theory)
- **Publisher:** SAGE Publications
- **Scope:** International peer-reviewed journal publishing original research on women's studies, gender studies, and feminist theory with a focus on European contexts. Publishes articles, reviews, conference reports, topical and polemical pieces, and overviews on the state of women's studies in various European countries.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/author-instructions/ejw
- **Field baseline:** mgmt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 12 articles from 2025 (see Sources)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
- Follows standard academic article structure with flexibility for article type (research article, review, essay, polemical piece, conference report).
- Typical structure: Abstract → Introduction → Body sections (organized thematically or theoretically, not generically labeled) → Discussion/Conclusion.
- No prescribed Theory/Hypotheses section required (unlike quantitative management work); body organization is driven by the argument's logic.

### Length
- **Strict limit: 6,000–7,000 words inclusive of all text, bibliography, footnotes, and notes.**
- This is significantly shorter than FIELD_SKILL baselines (which expect 8,000–25,000 words depending on article type).
- Word count is binding; manuscripts exceeding this limit will not be accepted.

### Abstract
- **Unstructured format** (no subheadings like Background/Methods/Findings/Conclusion).
- **300 words** (longer than FIELD_SKILL baseline of 150–250).
- Must state purpose, approach, and main contribution clearly.
- No citations in abstract.

### Keywords
- **Minimum 5 keywords** required.
- Must be specific to the research topic.
- Appear after the abstract.

### Citations
- SAGE Harvard reference style (in-text parenthetical).
- Format: (Author, Year) or Author (Year) when author is part of sentence.
- Multiple citations: alphabetical or chronological order within parentheses.
- Et al. for three or more authors from first citation.
- Follows FIELD_SKILL citation conventions (see FIELD_SKILL for detailed examples).

### Headings
- No mandatory generic section labels (i.e., no requirement for "Theory," "Methods," "Results").
- Subheadings should reflect the substantive argument, not formulaic categories.
- Heading hierarchy must be clear (editors expect clear visual levels in Word format).

### Tables and Figures
- No explicit prohibition or special formatting rules found in guidelines.
- Follows FIELD_SKILL conventions (captions, clear labeling, integration into text).

### Explicit Prohibitions
- **Clinical trials:** Must include trial registry name, URL, and registration number in abstract end note (if applicable).
- No author guidelines forbid passive voice, hedging, or first-person usage; FIELD_SKILL norms apply.

### Manuscript Format
- Preferred: Microsoft Word.
- Acceptable: LaTeX.
- No mandatory template; ensure heading levels and section boundaries are clearly marked.
- No special formatting beyond standard academic style.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Manuscript Length
**ENFORCED.** EJWS imposes strict 6,000–7,000-word limits, significantly shorter than FIELD_SKILL baselines.
- FIELD_SKILL expects 8,000–15,000 words (empirical) or 15,000–25,000 (reviews).
- EJWS limit is non-negotiable; manuscripts over 7,000 words are rejected outright.
- **Implication:** Brevity is paramount. Section hierarchies compress; Theory/Literature may be 1–2 pages. No room for exhaustive literature reviews in introduction.

### Abstract Length and Structure
**ENFORCED.** EJWS requires 300-word unstructured abstracts.
- FIELD_SKILL allows 150–250 words, often unstructured but sometimes with subheadings.
- The 300-word length here is the longest among comparable journals, reflecting gender studies' expectation for more nuanced framing in abstract.
- **Implication:** Abstract is a mini-argument, not a summary. Author must lead with contribution, not merely state research question.

### Article Types and Structure
**GUIDELINE + PRACTICE.** EJWS accepts beyond FIELD_SKILL's four types (quantitative, qualitative, theoretical, review).
- EJWS explicitly welcomes: **articles, reviews, conference reports, topical pieces, polemical pieces, and overviews of women's studies by country/region.**
- Polemical and topical pieces may dispense with empirical methods; they are opinion-driven arguments situated in feminist theory and critique.
- **Implication:** Essays and position papers (absent from FIELD_SKILL baseline) are legitimate and valued. Section structure for a polemical piece may be purely argumentative without Methods or Results.

### Substantive Focus and Voice
**PRACTICE ONLY.** Observed patterns in 2025 articles indicate:
- Heavy use of cultural analysis, literary interpretation, and media studies (close reading of artworks, film, television).
- Articles frequently combine empirical findings (e.g., legal analysis, survivor testimonies) with interpretive/theoretical framing.
- Historical and textual evidence (e.g., responsa, legislative documents, artistic representations) treated as primary data.
- **Tone:** Feminist stance explicit and expected. Authors write "we argue," "we contend," and position arguments within feminist critique; neutral stance is not default (contrasts with FIELD_SKILL's emphasis on "appropriate hedging").

### Citation Density and Engagement
**PRACTICE ONLY.** Observed patterns show variation by article type:
- Theoretical/conceptual pieces cite heavily throughout (consistent with FIELD_SKILL).
- Empirical/analytical pieces (e.g., legal reform analysis) integrate citations within argument but are less dense than management theory sections.
- Cited works often include feminist and gender studies classics (Simone de Beauvoir, Betty Friedan, Audre Lorde, hooks) alongside contemporary research.
- **Implication:** Feminist canon is expected knowledge; omitting foundational feminist texts may signal weak grounding in the field.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- Voice and person: FIELD DEFAULT. First-person plural ("we") standard; passive voice used selectively per FIELD_SKILL norms.
- Hedging and epistemic stance: FIELD DEFAULT. Moderate hedging (FIELD_SKILL norm), with modal verbs ("may," "might," "suggest") marking inferences. Feminist arguments stated directly ("We argue").
- Argumentation and logical architecture: FIELD DEFAULT. Sections advance distinct steps in the argument; reasoning chains explicit.
- Discussion and conclusion: FIELD DEFAULT. Opens with summary of findings; develops implications; addresses limitations; closes with future directions or implications.

---

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Exceeding word limit.** Submissions over 7,000 words (including bibliography) are desk-rejected. Compress rigorously; prioritize argument over background.
2. **Unstructured or missing abstract.** 300-word unstructured abstract is mandatory. A 150-word abstract is insufficient; conversely, abstracts over 300 words must be shortened. Clearly announce purpose, approach, and contribution upfront.
3. **Weak keyword selection.** Keywords must be specific to the paper's focus (e.g., "feminist," "gender," "women" alone are too broad). Use 5+ keywords reflecting the precise topic and theoretical framework.
4. **Insufficient feminist positioning.** Articles lacking explicit engagement with feminist theory or critique risk desk rejection. Position your work within feminist scholarship; state what feminist question or argument you are advancing.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Policy and Politics

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** PUB SEC
- **Publisher:** Bristol University Press / Policy Press
- **Scope:** Publishes scholarship on any aspect of the policy process, with an
  explicit expectation that papers address the broader implications for
  politics, power, and democratic processes rather than policy content alone.
  Welcomes quantitative, qualitative, interpretive, and mixed methods, and
  work spanning micro to macro levels of analysis; global (not UK/Europe-only)
  coverage of public policy subfields is actively sought.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://policyandpoliticsblog.com/submit-to-journal/
  (main submission page); full editorial/production guidelines PDF at
  https://policy.bristoluniversitypress.co.uk/asset/2429/editorial-and-production-guidelines.pdf
  (not text-extractable in this session; see Sources).
- **Field baseline:** public-sector-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 (abstract/summary-level access): "How policy
  narratives shape sustainability governance" (Angst et al., ahead of print,
  DOI 10.1332/03055736Y2025D000000084); "Uncovering motivations behind open
  government policy" (ahead of print, DOI 10.1332/03055736Y2026D000000090);
  "Policy coordination and integration in local government: perspectives on
  barriers" (De Wulf, Voets & Molenveld, 2023, Vol 51(3):530); "Global Public
  Policy studies" (Porto de Oliveira, 2022, Vol 50(1):59); "Policy & Politics:
  a perspective on the first half century" (2022, Vol 50(1):3); "Inferential
  reasoning in policy analysis" (Aagaard, Easton & Head, 2024). Full text was
  not accessible (publisher platform returns 403 to automated fetch); findings
  are based on abstracts, journal-blog summaries of the same articles, and
  metadata.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No mandatory section list or heading scheme is published beyond standard
practice; FIELD_SKILL structure applies by default. Anonymisation is
mandatory and specific: the submission file must exclude title, author
names, affiliations, abstract, and keywords from the manuscript body
itself (these are entered separately in the submission system).

### Length
5,000–8,000 words, **including references**. This is a hard cap unusual
relative to FIELD_SKILL's silence on length; reference lists count against
the limit, which in practice constrains citation density more than
FIELD_SKILL's "high throughout" default would suggest.

### Abstract
Maximum 100 words (unstructured), plus up to 4 keywords. This is short
relative to typical PUB SEC 4*/4 structured abstracts; there is no
evidence of required structured fields (problem/approach/finding) — the
100-word cap likely precludes a structured format in practice.

### Citations
Harvard author-date style. In-text multiple citations are ordered
chronologically (oldest to newest), e.g. "(Smith, 1989; Jones, 1990;
Amler, 2002; Brown, 2007)" — not alphabetically, which is common practice
elsewhere. Reference list examples follow standard Harvard formatting
(e.g., Jeffrey, C. (2008) 'The challenge of territorial politics', Policy
& Politics, 36(4): 545–57).

### Headings
No journal-specific heading requirements found beyond FIELD_SKILL defaults.

### Tables and Figures
Figures, diagrams, and maps should be submitted separately from the text,
ideally as Encapsulated PostScript (.eps); Word/Excel-native figures are
also accepted. Alt text describing visual content is explicitly required
for accessibility.

### Explicit Prohibitions
No preliminary identifying details (title, author, affiliations, abstract,
keywords) may appear in the submission file itself — full double-anonymous
review is enforced procedurally, not just requested.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Citation Density
RELAXATION: the hard 5,000–8,000 word cap that includes references
constrains citation stacking more tightly than FIELD_SKILL's "high
throughout" default. Spot-checked abstracts and summaries show claims
supported by fewer, more targeted citations per point rather than the
dense multi-citation stacking FIELD_SKILL describes for 4*/4 outlets.
PRACTICE ONLY / low-to-moderate confidence given abstract-only access.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
ENFORCED: chronological (not alphabetical) ordering of stacked in-text
citations is a stated house-style rule, confirmed by the guideline
excerpt and consistent with Harvard-style examples found. This is a
concrete formatting rule FIELD_SKILL does not specify.

GUIDELINE ONLY: the 100-word abstract cap is notably tighter than typical
PUB SEC 4*/4 structured abstracts (FIELD_SKILL describes structured
abstracts stating problem/approach/finding); at 100 words a full
structured format is unlikely to fit, so authors should default to a
single dense unstructured paragraph rather than FIELD_SKILL's structured
convention.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- Voice, person, and register: FIELD DEFAULT (spot-checked abstracts show
  the same policy-relevant framing, moral/democratic stakes named
  explicitly, and mixed first-person/impersonal voice as FIELD_SKILL
  describes).
- Argumentation and rhetorical moves: FIELD DEFAULT (articles open with
  concrete governance problems — e.g., transport policy discourse, open
  government adoption — before establishing academic territory, matching
  FIELD_SKILL's CARS-like pattern).
- Methods conventions: FIELD DEFAULT (methodological pluralism confirmed
  explicitly by the journal's own scope statement; no orthodoxy toward
  quantitative or qualitative work).
- Hedging and epistemic caution: FIELD DEFAULT (no evidence of unusually
  strong or unusually hedged claims relative to FIELD_SKILL).
- Discussion/conclusion norms: FIELD DEFAULT. No evidence found of a
  mandatory "Points for Practitioners" box or similar structural device
  (unlike some PUB SEC outlets); practitioner implications appear to be
  integrated in prose rather than pulled into a required box.

## Common Pitfalls
- Exceeding the 100-word abstract limit by defaulting to a FIELD_SKILL-style
  structured abstract — this journal's cap is too short for that format.
- Forgetting that references count toward the 5,000–8,000 word limit, which
  can force late-stage citation trimming if not planned for from the outset.
- Leaving identifying details (title, author, affiliation, abstract,
  keywords) inside the manuscript file rather than entering them separately
  in the submission system, violating the anonymisation requirement.
- Alphabetising stacked in-text citations instead of ordering them
  chronologically as the journal's house style requires.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

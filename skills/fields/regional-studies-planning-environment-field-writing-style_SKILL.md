# Field Writing Style: REGIONAL STUDIES, PLANNING AND ENVIRONMENT

## Scope and Intended Use

This baseline is derived from the field's ABS 4-rated journals (there are no
ABS 4* journals in this field): Economic Geography, Journal of Economic
Geography, and Regional Studies. These journals sit at the intersection of
economics, human geography, and regional/urban planning, and their norms
reflect that hybridity — quantitative and spatial-econometric work sits
alongside institutionalist, evolutionary, and qualitative case-study
traditions within the same outlets. Use this skill as a prior for framing,
structuring, and voicing a manuscript aimed at a leading journal in this
field. Journal-specific skills should be consulted first where they exist;
this document only covers what is common across the field baseline.

## Typical Article Structure

At the ABS 4 level, empirical articles are expected to open with a short,
unstructured abstract (no subheadings) of roughly 100-200 words, followed
by a short list of keywords and, in the more economics-adjacent outlets,
JEL classification codes. The introduction is typically un-headed or
labelled simply "Introduction" and is expected to accomplish several moves
in sequence: establish the empirical or policy relevance of a spatial
phenomenon, situate the paper against a specific stream of literature,
identify a gap or unresolved tension, and state the paper's contribution
and (often) preview its main finding.

A literature review or theoretical framing section — sometimes titled
"Literature review," "Theoretical background," or folded into an
extended introduction — is near-universal, since these journals expect
explicit engagement with economic geography, regional science, or
planning theory rather than treating theory as implicit. A data and
methods section follows, then results, then a discussion and conclusion.
Practice varies on whether discussion and conclusion are merged into one
section or kept separate: many articles combine them under a heading such
as "Discussion and conclusion," while others separate interpretive
discussion from a shorter concluding section addressing policy
implications, limitations, and future research. Both patterns are
accepted; there is no consensus norm on this point.

Policy implications are expected to receive explicit treatment, reflecting
the field's applied, place-based orientation — this distinguishes the
field baseline from more theory-oriented economics fields. Appendices for
supplementary tables, robustness checks, or extended data description are
common and expected for empirical papers with non-trivial methodological
detail.

## Voice, Person, and Register

Leading journals in this field expect a predominantly active-voice,
moderately formal register. First-person plural ("we argue," "we find")
is well established and increasingly the default for framing contribution
and reporting results, though some authors — particularly in more
qualitative or institutionalist submissions — retain an impersonal
register ("this paper argues," "it is shown that"). Both are accepted;
practice varies by sub-tradition within the field, and there is no single
consensus voice. What is consistent is that authorial stance is expected
to be clear and explicit rather than diffused into passive constructions
throughout — reviewers expect the paper's own claims to be distinguishable
from the claims of cited literature.

Register is academic but not densely technical-jargon-laden by default;
because the readership spans economists, geographers, and planners,
papers are expected to define specialized terms (whether econometric or
geographical-theoretical) rather than assume shared vocabulary across the
full audience.

## Sentence and Paragraph Style

Sentences are typically medium to long, often compound, reflecting the
need to hold multiple qualifications (spatial scale, time period, causal
mechanism) within a single claim. Paragraphs in the introduction and
discussion tend to be organized around a single move each — one gap
claim, one contribution claim, one implication — rather than blending
several rhetorical functions in one paragraph. Results sections favour
shorter, more declarative sentences oriented around specific coefficients,
patterns, or spatial contrasts. Signposting language (explicit statements
of what a section or subsection will do) is common and expected, given
the multi-disciplinary readership.

## Argumentation and Rhetorical Moves

Introductions broadly follow a CARS-like pattern: establish why a
particular place, region, or spatial process matters (often opening with
a real-world empirical or policy observation rather than a purely
theoretical claim), review what is known, identify a specific gap —
commonly framed as a scale mismatch, a missing mechanism, an
under-studied geography, or a methodological limitation in prior work —
and state how the present paper addresses it. Novelty is typically
framed in terms of one or more of: a new spatial scale or geographic
context, a new or improved dataset, a new empirical strategy for
identifying a spatial mechanism, or a theoretical reconceptualization of
an established concept (e.g., agglomeration, periphery, proximity).
Gaps are rarely framed as pure literature absences; they are usually tied
to a substantive claim about why the gap matters for theory, policy, or
both.

Disagreement with prior work is handled through measured, evidence-based
contrast rather than direct critique of authors. Leading journals expect
phrasing such as "prior work has focused on X, leaving Y underexplored"
rather than framing that impugns the validity of earlier studies.
Theoretical framing sections are expected to engage critically with
core constructs of the field (e.g., agglomeration economies, path
dependence, relatedness, institutional thickness) rather than treating
them as settled definitions.

## Methods Conventions

Methods sections are typically written in past tense for procedures
actually carried out ("we estimated," "the model was specified as") and
combine narrative description with formal notation where quantitative
methods are used. Practice varies substantially by sub-tradition: papers
using spatial econometrics, panel data, or firm/patent microdata provide
detailed formal model specification, estimator justification, and
robustness discussion; qualitative or mixed-methods papers instead
provide detailed procedural description of case selection, interview
protocols, or coding strategy. No single consensus method dominates the
field, and reviewers at this level are expected to evaluate methodological
fit to the research question rather than expect a single template.

Where quantitative methods are used, spatial considerations (unit of
analysis, scale, boundary effects, spatial autocorrelation) are expected
to be addressed explicitly, since this is a defining methodological
concern of the field. Robustness checks are expected for quantitative
papers and are typically reported either as a subsection of results or
in an appendix, with the main text summarizing what was checked and the
appendix carrying full tables.

## Results and Reporting Norms

Results sections are expected to separate reporting from interpretation:
findings are presented with reference to tables, figures, and (where
relevant) maps, followed by comparatively brief in-text interpretation,
with deeper interpretation reserved for the discussion section. Maps and
spatial visualizations are a distinctive and expected reporting
convention in this field relative to general economics or management
journals — where spatial patterns are central to the argument, a map or
spatial figure is expected rather than optional.

Statistical precision in reporting coefficients, standard errors, and
significance levels follows general social-science econometric
convention where quantitative methods are used, but practice varies on
how much emphasis is placed on statistical significance versus economic
or spatial magnitude — leading journals increasingly expect explicit
discussion of magnitude and substantive importance, not just
significance stars. For qualitative papers, results are reported through
structured thematic presentation, often illustrated with interview
excerpts or case description, with interpretation more tightly
interwoven with reporting than in quantitative papers.

## Discussion and Conclusion Norms

Discussion sections are expected to return explicitly to the gap and
contribution claims made in the introduction, addressing how the
findings speak to them. Limitations are expected to be acknowledged,
typically briefly and specifically rather than as generic disclaimers.
Policy and practice implications receive substantive treatment at the
ABS 4 level in this field — this is a stronger and more consistent
expectation here than in purely theoretical economics fields, reflecting
the applied, place-based readership of planning and regional-development
audiences. Conclusions typically close with directions for future
research, often framed in terms of new geographic contexts, data, or
methods that could extend the present contribution.

## Citation and Literature Engagement

Citation density is highest in the introduction and literature/theory
sections and noticeably lower in the results section, rising again in
the discussion where findings are related back to the literature.
Paraphrase is strongly preferred over direct quotation; leading journals
in this field very rarely feature block quotations from prior scholarly
literature (quotations from qualitative data such as interviews are a
distinct and accepted practice, not covered by this norm). Disagreement
with specific prior findings is expressed through paraphrased contrast
("in contrast to X, we find...") rather than extended critical
quotation. Citation of foundational field concepts (e.g., core references
on agglomeration, new economic geography, evolutionary economic
geography) is expected even in papers that do not directly test those
frameworks, since these are treated as shared theoretical touchstones
across the field's journals.

## Hedging and Epistemic Caution

Hedging intensity is moderate: causal claims from observational data are
typically qualified ("suggests," "is consistent with," "may reflect")
unless the identification strategy is unusually strong, in which case
more direct causal language is used but still typically bounded by scope
conditions (time period, geography, sector). Overclaiming generalizability
beyond the studied spatial context is treated as a reviewer concern;
papers are expected to explicitly bound their claims to the scale,
region, or period studied and to hedge extrapolation to other contexts.

## What This Baseline Does NOT Specify

This baseline does not specify word or page limits, reference list
formatting or citation style (e.g., author-date vs numbered), specific
software or estimator choices, submission or peer-review procedures, or
any journal-specific formatting requirements. It also does not resolve
whether a given journal prefers first-person or impersonal voice, merged
or separate discussion/conclusion sections, or quantitative versus
qualitative methods as a default — these vary across the field's journals
and, where relevant, should be specified by journal-specific skills that
supersede this baseline.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

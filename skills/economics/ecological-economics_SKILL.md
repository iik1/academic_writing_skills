# Journal Writing Skill: Ecological Economics

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Elsevier
- **Scope:** Transdisciplinary journal integrating ecological and economic theory; covers ecosystem valuation, sustainability transitions, environmental policy, and integrated ecological-economic modelling. Explicitly "transdisciplinary in spirit and methodologically open."
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/ecological-economics/publish/guide-for-authors
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 3 (Li & Wang 2026, DOI 10.1016/j.ecolecon.2026.109016; pii/S0921800925002204; pii/S0921800924003501)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No mandatory section sequence is specified. Follows FIELD_SKILL norm for internal ordering discretion.

### Length
Strict per-category word caps — **ENFORCED**:
- **Analysis** (standard research article): max 8,000 words
- **Commentary**: max 5,000 words or 12 double-spaced pages
- **Surveys**: max 8,000 words or 25 double-spaced pages
- **Methodological and Ideological Options**: max 8,000 words or 25 double-spaced pages
- **News and Views**: max 1,500 words (letters within this category: max 600 words)
- **Book Reviews**: max 1,200 words or 4 double-spaced pages

Selecting the correct submission category is mandatory; each category has a distinct editorial purpose. Most empirical research papers submit as **Analysis**.

### Abstract
Unstructured plain prose — no labeled subsections (Background, Methods, Results, Conclusions). Observed length: ~100–200 words. No word limit stated in guidelines, but practice aligns with Elsevier standard of 150–250 words. **PRACTICE ONLY** (length); **GUIDELINE ONLY** (format: guidelines reference abstract without specifying structure).

### Citations
Author-year format confirmed in articles. Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.

### Headings
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. No journal-specific numbering convention mandated.

### Tables and Figures
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm for content. Elsevier production standards apply for resolution and file format.

### Highlights
A **Highlights** box is required — standard Elsevier format: 3–5 bullet points, each ≤85 characters including spaces, summarising the core findings. Observed in practice (4 bullets in one article). **ENFORCED.**

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Generative AI tools may not be listed as authors or co-authors.
- AI may not substitute for human critical thinking, expertise, or evaluation in the research or writing process (use must be declared).
- Letters to the editor must not refer to personalities; focus on ideas only.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

> Only dimensions where this journal deviates from the field baseline.

### Voice and Register

**FIELD_SKILL norm:** First-person plural ("we find", "we show", "we estimate") is near-universal; even sole-authored papers use "we."

**This journal:** Impersonal third-person constructions are common in practice. Two of three articles spot-checked used "This study demonstrates…", "The results reveal…", "This study aims to illuminate…" rather than "we." One article used collective framing ("Our findings reveal…"). The natural science co-tradition within this interdisciplinary journal normalises impersonal voice alongside "we."

**Classification:** RELAXATION (ABS 3 context; both registers are accepted; impersonal voice is not penalised here as it would be at ABS 4/4* econ journals).

**Practical implication:** Either "we" or impersonal third-person is acceptable. Authors coming from economics should not feel compelled to switch; authors from ecology or environmental science need not adopt "we." Avoid mixing both registers within a single paper.

### Structural Deviations

**FIELD_SKILL norm:** Standard empirical structure: Introduction → Data → Empirical Strategy → Results → Robustness → Conclusion.

**This journal:** No single structure dominates. Interdisciplinary papers frequently include methods sections that cover ecological modelling, biophysical accounting, scenario analysis, or qualitative frameworks alongside or instead of econometric identification. A standalone **Conceptual Framework** or **Theoretical Background** section appears frequently. Pure identification-strategy subsections (as required at ABS 4* ECON) are absent in many papers.

**Classification:** PRACTICE ONLY.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Wrong submission category.** Submitting a standard empirical paper as "Survey" or "Commentary" triggers desk rejection. Research articles go under **Analysis** (8,000-word cap).
2. **Missing Highlights box.** Elsevier's required 3–5 bullet Highlights (≤85 chars each) is not part of FIELD_SKILL and is easily omitted by authors from pure economics backgrounds.
3. **Over-narrowing the framing.** The journal explicitly values transdisciplinary scope. A paper framed purely as a labour or IO economics contribution, without ecological or sustainability relevance, is likely to be seen as out-of-scope regardless of technical quality.
4. **Assuming identification-centred framing is required.** Unlike ABS 4/4* ECON outlets, this journal does not require a causal identification strategy. Well-designed descriptive, accounting, or model-based analyses are acceptable; forcing causal language onto non-experimental work is a style mismatch.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

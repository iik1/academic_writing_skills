# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Credit Risk

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** FINANCE
- **Publisher:** Infopro Digital Services Limited (Risk.net / Incisive Media journals group)
- **Scope:** Measurement and management of credit risk and the valuation/hedging of credit products, spanning portfolio credit risk modeling, credit derivatives, structured credit, counterparty risk, and (increasingly) machine learning and fintech applications to credit analysis.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.risk.net/static/risk-journals-submission-guidelines (shared across all Risk.net/Infopro journals, including Journal of Credit Risk); journal home: https://www.risk.net/journal-of-credit-risk
- **Field baseline:** finance-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 15 (titles/abstracts/key-messages reviewed via journal issue listings and article landing pages; full text paywalled). Sample: "Credit risk management: a systematic literature review and bibliometric analysis" (Kedia & Mishra, 2024, DOI 10.21314/JCR.2024.002); "Deciphering bankruptcy risk in fintech firms" (Kedia & Mishra, 2025); "Fintech lending and firm bankruptcies" (Nguyen & Qiu, 2025); "How magic a bullet is machine learning for credit analysis?" (Wang & Perkins, 2025); "Did fintech loans default more during the Covid-19 pandemic?" (Jagtiani et al., 2025); "Researching new financial products: ... buy now, pay later" (Akana & Zeballos Doubinko, 2025); "A survey of machine learning in credit risk" (Breeden, 2021); "Covid-19 and the credit cycle" (Altman, 2020/2021); "A credit portfolio framework under dependent risk parameters" (Eckert, Jakob & Fischer); "Stochastic loss given default and exposure at default in a structural model" (Kaposty, Löderbusch & Maciag); "Portfolio credit risk model with extremal dependence of defaults" (Jeon, Kim & Lee); "Ensemble methods for credit scoring of Chinese peer-to-peer loans" (Cao et al.); "An effective credit rating method for corporate entities using machine learning" (Sun et al.); "Default risk in the era of environmental, social and governance ratings" (Hameed et al.); one additional IFRS 9/Basel III-focused article. Because this is an ABS 1 journal with paywalled full text, the sample is front-matter-level (abstracts, key messages, titles) rather than full-text close reading — documented per instructions.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict. Sourced from the shared Risk Journals submission guidelines page, which governs Journal of Credit Risk alongside sister journals (Journal of Risk, Journal of Operational Risk, etc.). No JCR-specific guideline page exists separately.

### Structure
Required manuscript order: (1) separate title page with author names/affiliations, (2) figure and table legends, (3) abstract, (4) keywords, (5) key messages (bullet list), (6) title, (7) main text (blinded — no author identification, for double-blind review), (8) Declarations of Interest and Acknowledgements, (9) references. A dedicated conclusions/future-research section is explicitly required. Suggested main-text sections: Introduction, Literature Review, Data, Methodology, Results, Discussion, Conclusion — a fuller prescribed skeleton than FIELD_SKILL's looser "Data / Empirical Analysis / Conclusion" pattern.

### Length
Research papers: max 10,000 words. Forum/technical papers: max 7,000 words. Publisher retains final discretion. FIELD_SKILL does not specify word limits (deliberately excluded there); this is a hard journal-level constraint.

### Abstract
Single paragraph, 150–200 words. Must state purpose, principal results, and major conclusions. Must stand alone with no citations.

### Citations
In-text citations follow author-date per The Chicago Manual of Style; reference list formatted in APA style. Include DOIs where available. (Consistent with FIELD_SKILL's author-year norm, but the specific Chicago/APA hybrid mechanics are a journal-level detail FIELD_SKILL does not specify.)

### Headings
Not prescriptive beyond the suggested section skeleton above; Arabic numbering consistent with FIELD_SKILL norm.

### Tables and Figures
Legends listed separately near the front of the manuscript (before the abstract), in addition to appearing in-text. Figures/tables must also be submitted as separate individual editable files (vector formats EPS/SVG/PDF preferred; minimum 300dpi for raster). Guidance emphasizes keeping the number of tables/figures minimal.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- No author-identifying information anywhere in the blinded main-text PDF (double-blind review).
- Footnotes must be used sparingly, no more than two sentences each.
- Spelling per Webster's dictionary; punctuation per Chicago Manual of Style.
- A "Key messages" section (3–4 bullet points, each max ~85 characters) is required as a distinct front-matter element — not mentioned in FIELD_SKILL at all, which describes only the standard abstract.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm for the academic-style research papers. However, this journal's stated remit explicitly welcomes "technical papers"/practitioner contributions alongside academic research papers (dual academic-practitioner audience, editors-in-chief drawn from both academia and the Federal Reserve Board). Titles and key-messages sampled skew toward direct, applied framing (e.g., "How magic a bullet is machine learning for credit analysis?") rather than the more formal contribution-listing style FIELD_SKILL describes for ABS 4*/4 introductions. PRACTICE ONLY / RELAXATION — consistent with ABS 1 tier allowing more applied, less heavily theory-motivated framing; not a redefinition of field norms.

### Structural Deviations
ENFORCED: the mandatory "Key messages" bullet block (3–4 short bullets, standing apart from the abstract) is a structural element with no counterpart in FIELD_SKILL. GUIDELINE ONLY: the prescribed Introduction/Literature Review/Data/Methodology/Results/Discussion/Conclusion skeleton is more explicit and rigid than FIELD_SKILL's flexible structure, though sampled articles' visible front matter is consistent with it.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Journal accepts shorter "technical/forum papers" (max 7,000 words) as a distinct submission category alongside standard research papers (max 10,000 words) — FIELD_SKILL does not describe a two-tier submission-length system. GUIDELINE ONLY (not separately confirmed in the accessible sample, since front-matter alone does not indicate which length tier a given published article used).

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting without the separate "Key messages" bullet block (required in addition to the abstract) — a common structural omission for authors used to standard ABS 4/4* finance journal front matter.
- Leaving author-identifying details in the main manuscript PDF; the journal requires a fully blinded submission with a separate title page.
- Exceeding the 7,000-word cap when submitting a practitioner-oriented technical/forum paper rather than the 10,000-word research-paper cap.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

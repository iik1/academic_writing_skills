# Field Writing Style: INFO MAN

## Scope and Intended Use
This baseline synthesizes writing conventions observed across the ABS
4*/4 tier of the Information Management (Information Systems) field:
Information Systems Research, Journal of the Association for
Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, European Journal of Information
Systems, Information Systems Journal, Journal of Information
Technology, Journal of Management Information Systems, and Journal of
Strategic Information Systems. It is intended as a prior for
journal-specific writing skills, which should record only deviations
from what is described here. It applies to empirical, design-science,
and conceptual/theoretical papers alike, since the field publishes all
three genres at this tier; where conventions differ by genre this is
noted explicitly.

## Typical Article Structure
At the ABS 4*/4 level, reviewers expect a recognizable macro-structure:
Abstract, Introduction, Theoretical Background/Literature Review,
(Hypotheses or Research Model development, where applicable), Methods,
Results, Discussion, and Conclusion (often including Limitations and
Future Research as a subsection rather than a standalone section).
Design-science papers substitute a Design/Artifact Development section
and an Evaluation section for the hypothesis-testing sequence, but
retain the same outer envelope of Introduction, Related Work,
core contribution, Evaluation, and Discussion.

The Introduction and the Discussion/Contribution framing are treated as
the most consequential sections of the paper — editors and reviewers at
this tier explicitly weight how well a paper motivates its problem and
articulates its contribution, often more heavily than technical
execution alone. A distinct "Theoretical Background" or "Related Work"
section separate from the Introduction is near-universal for empirical
papers, allowing the Introduction itself to stay compact and
motivation-focused. A named "Contributions" subsection or clearly
labeled contribution statement (theoretical and practical/managerial)
in the Discussion is expected. Practical/managerial implications are a
near-universal separate subsection, reflecting the field's applied
orientation. Limitations are typically acknowledged briefly and
specifically rather than as generic boilerplate.

Practice varies on: whether hypotheses are presented in a dedicated
"Research Model and Hypotheses" section versus interwoven into the
literature review; whether Results and Discussion are merged into one
section or kept separate; and how much of the front matter is devoted
to practitioner-facing motivation (e.g., an industry vignette) versus
academic gap-framing. No consensus norm exists on these points.

## Voice, Person, and Register
Leading journals in this field require formal, professional academic
register. First-person plural ("we") is standard and widely accepted
for describing what the authors did, argued, or found ("we test," "we
find," "we contribute") — this holds even in single-author papers,
where "I" is also accepted but less common. Active voice is preferred
for authorial actions and for stating contributions and findings;
passive voice remains common and acceptable for describing procedures,
data collection steps, and previously established facts in the
literature review. The overall register blends social-science
formality with an applied, managerially-aware tone: papers regularly
address both an academic and a practitioner audience, and register
shifts slightly toward accessibility in framing sections (introduction,
implications) relative to technical sections (methods, formal model
development), where more specialized and precise terminology is
expected.

## Sentence and Paragraph Style
Sentences are typically medium to long, reflecting the field's tendency
toward compound claims that link constructs, mechanisms, and boundary
conditions in a single statement. Paragraphs in the introduction and
discussion tend to open with a clear topic sentence stating a claim or
move, followed by supporting elaboration and citations. Technical
sections (methods, formal/mathematical model specification) favor
shorter, more procedural sentences. Field-specific terminology
(constructs, theoretical mechanisms, IT artifacts, sociotechnical
framing) is used precisely and consistently once introduced, with
authors typically defining constructs explicitly at first use rather
than assuming shared understanding.

## Argumentation and Rhetorical Moves
The field follows a recognizable CARS-style (Create-a-Research-Space)
pattern in introductions: establish the importance and topicality of a
phenomenon (often technology-driven — a new IT artifact, platform, or
digital phenomenon), review what is known, identify a specific gap
(theoretical, empirical, or contextual) or tension in the literature,
and state how the paper addresses that gap. At the ABS 4*/4 level,
reviewers expect the gap to be framed as more than "no one has looked
at this before" — the strongest papers connect the gap to a theoretical
or practical puzzle, an inconsistency in prior findings, or an
emerging phenomenon that existing theory does not adequately explain.
Contributions are almost always framed dually: a theoretical/academic
contribution (to a specific literature or theory) and a
practical/managerial contribution (for practitioners, policymakers, or
system designers). Disagreement with prior literature is handled
collegially and specifically — authors identify precise
limitations, boundary conditions, or contexts where prior findings do
not generalize, rather than issuing blanket critiques of earlier work.

## Methods Conventions
Methods sections are written primarily in past tense, describing what
was done, with enough procedural detail (sampling, measurement,
analytic technique, model specification) that a knowledgeable reader
could assess or replicate the design. At this tier, robustness checks
and alternative explanations are expected to be addressed explicitly,
either within the Results section or in a dedicated robustness
subsection; unaddressed threats to validity are treated as a reviewer
concern. Design-science papers describe artifact design and development
in past tense as an account of an iterative process, and typically
report evaluation against explicit criteria (utility, quality,
efficacy) rather than narrative assertion alone. Practice varies:
some journals favor detailed measurement-model reporting (loadings,
reliability, validity statistics) in the main text, others push this
to appendices/online supplements. No consensus norm exists on main-text
versus appendix placement for measurement detail, though the appendix
option is common enough at this tier to be considered acceptable
practice.

## Results and Reporting Norms
Results are reported with a clear separation between reporting
(what was found) and interpretation (what it means), with
interpretation reserved primarily for the Discussion. Quantitative
findings are reported with specific statistical precision — effect
sizes, significance levels, and model fit statistics are expected
rather than qualitative characterizations of significance alone.
Tables are the primary vehicle for detailed quantitative results
(coefficients, fit indices, descriptive statistics), with prose used to
narrate the pattern of findings and flag key results rather than
restate every table entry. Qualitative and design-science papers report
findings through structured narrative, illustrative quotations or
artifact excerpts, and summary tables/frameworks that organize themes
or design principles. Hypotheses, where used, are typically restated
individually alongside their support/non-support status.

## Discussion and Conclusion Norms
The Discussion is expected to circle back explicitly to the gap
identified in the Introduction and state how the findings close or
reframe it. A structured treatment of theoretical contributions
followed by practical/managerial implications is the norm, often as
labeled subsections. Limitations are addressed directly and tied to
specific design choices (e.g., context, measurement, sample) rather
than generic disclaimers, typically paired with concrete suggestions
for future research that extend from those limitations. The Conclusion
is generally brief, summarizing the contribution rather than
introducing new argumentation or evidence.

## Citation and Literature Engagement
Citation density is highest in the Introduction and Theoretical
Background/Related Work sections and lowest in Methods and Results,
rising again somewhat in the Discussion when connecting findings back
to the literature. Paraphrase dominates over direct quotation; direct
quotation is largely reserved for qualitative empirical data (interview
excerpts, document evidence) rather than for citing prior scholarly
literature. Disagreement with or extension of prior work is expressed
through specific, attributed claims (naming the study or stream of
research and characterizing its finding or limitation) rather than
vague collective attribution. Literature is typically organized by
theoretical lens or theme rather than presented as a chronological
list.

## Hedging and Epistemic Caution
Hedging intensity is moderate: claims about findings are stated with
reasonable confidence within the boundaries of the data and design,
but authors routinely qualify generalizability, causal claims (favoring
language such as "associated with" over "causes" where the design does
not support causal inference), and the scope of contribution.
Overclaiming beyond the empirical or design evidence is treated as a
significant weakness at this tier. Practical implications are
typically hedged less than theoretical claims, as they are framed as
suggestive guidance for practitioners rather than as strict theoretical
propositions.

## What This Baseline Does NOT Specify
This baseline does not specify: word or page limits, formatting or
citation style mandates (e.g., APA vs. journal-specific reference
formats), specific structural templates unique to a single journal
(e.g., a fixed section-heading scheme), submission or peer-review
process requirements, preferred theoretical lenses or methodological
paradigms (e.g., positivist vs. interpretive stance), or any
journal-specific editorial priorities (e.g., a stated preference for
particular topics, artifact types, or geographic contexts). These are
left to journal-specific skills.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

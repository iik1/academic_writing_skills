# Journal Writing Skill: Fiscal Studies

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Wiley-Blackwell (on behalf of Institute for Fiscal Studies)
- **Scope:** Quarterly peer-reviewed journal publishing policy-oriented empirical and applied public economics research. Welcomes articles bridging academic research and policy analysis.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/page/journal/14755890/homepage/forauthors.html
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 3 ([Hardy et al. 2024 "Income inequality in the United States"; Vol 45 Issue 2 special issue on labour market and income inequalities; Blömer et al. 2024 "Labour market trends in Germany"])

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No explicit section sequence specified in guidelines. Empirical papers follow standard FIELD_SKILL pattern (Introduction, Data/Methods, Results, Conclusion); policy-oriented papers may vary structure depending on argument. No mandated section numbering scheme specified.

### Length
**ENFORCED:** Papers preferably no more than **7,500 words** (exclusive of abstract, appendices, and references). This is a hard upper bound strictly enforced by the journal. Substantially shorter papers (e.g., 3,000–5,000 words) are explicitly welcomed for focused policy analysis.

### Abstract
**ENFORCED:** Required abstract of no more than **200 words**. Unstructured format (no mandated fields like Background/Methods/Results/Conclusions). Must accompany JEL classification(s).

### Citations
**ENFORCED:** Author-year format (either parenthetical or in-text) is standard. Reference style may be any consistent format (Harvard, APA, Chicago author-year), provided formatting is uniform throughout. Numbered footnote references are not acceptable as primary citations.

### Headings
Headings follow FIELD_SKILL norm. No explicit requirement for Roman numerals or Arabic numerals; either is acceptable. Policy-oriented papers may use descriptive headings rather than numbered sections.

### Tables and Figures
Must include legends/captions. No explicit prohibition on colour figures; Wiley's standard guidelines apply (high resolution where possible; distinction clear in black-and-white printing). Tables should include notes explaining variables, clustering of standard errors, and sample restrictions.

### Data and Replication
**ENFORCED:** Empirical and numerical papers must provide full materials detailing how results were obtained, with sufficient accuracy to enable replication. Data availability statement required; authors select from Wiley's standard policy options. Single-anonymized peer review model: author identity is known to reviewers; reviewer identity is not disclosed to authors.

### Author Requirements
**ENFORCED:** ORCID required for all authors. **ENFORCED:** CRediT (Contribution Roles Taxonomy) contribution statement required for all articles (not optional).

### Submission Format
Manuscripts should be submitted as editable Word (.doc/.docx) or LaTeX (.tex) files. Figures may be embedded or provided separately at highest resolution possible. Submission through Research Exchange platform (Wiley's manuscript management system).

### Explicit Prohibitions
Preprint availability: Authors may post preprints to repositories at any time (before, during, or after review). The journal explicitly welcomes consideration of previously available preprints.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Word Length: SIGNIFICANT RELAXATION
FIELD_SKILL describes conventions for top-tier (ABS 4*/4) economics journals, which typically publish 8,000–15,000 word papers with substantial empirical or theoretical apparatus. Fiscal Studies (ABS 2) enforces a hard 7,500-word limit and explicitly welcomes shorter, focused policy papers of 3,000–5,000 words. This is a **RELAXATION** of length norms. Prose style may be more direct and less densely technical as a result of space constraints.

### Structural Flexibility: RELAXATION
FIELD_SKILL describes near-universal section structure (Introduction, Methods/Data, Results, Conclusion). Fiscal Studies, as a policy-oriented outlet, permits greater structural variation, particularly for policy analysis papers that may not fit the empirical-paper template. Papers addressing a specific policy debate may organize around that debate rather than methodological sequence. This is a **PRACTICE ONLY** observation (not mandated by guidelines but evident in recent issues).

### Citation Density: FIELD DEFAULT
Follows FIELD_SKILL norms. Introduction and methods sections show high citation density; results sections show medium density; conclusions show low density. Author-year is the standard format.

### Voice and Register: FIELD DEFAULT
Voice and register follow FIELD_SKILL norms. First-person plural ("we") is standard; active voice is preferred; formality is high. No departure observed or mandated.

### Statistical Reporting: FIELD DEFAULT
Follows FIELD_SKILL norms. Quantitative specificity expected ("X increased by Y percentage points"), alongside significance testing. Null results treated as reportable.

### Hedging: FIELD DEFAULT
Follows FIELD_SKILL norms. Low overall hedging intensity. Clear claims are preferred. Hedging appears in standard contexts (external validity, data limitations, causal inference limits).

### Results and Discussion Integration: FIELD DEFAULT
Follows FIELD_SKILL norms. Results and interpretation integrated within sections rather than separated.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Exceeding word limit:** The 7,500-word ceiling is strictly enforced. Papers just under this limit sometimes face editorial rejection post-review for exceeding the bound when final proofs are prepared. Plan for ~6,800 words of core content to allow buffer for revisions.

2. **Omitting CRediT and ORCID:** These are mandatory at submission, not post-acceptance. Missing either will result in desk rejection or request for rapid resubmission with these elements.

3. **Inadequate replication materials:** Fiscal Studies conducts peer review of empirical data. Incomplete or poorly documented data sets result in revise-and-resubmit or rejection. Ensure data labels, variable definitions, and sample sizes are transparent in the provided materials.

4. **Structural mismatch in policy papers:** Policy-oriented papers that lack a clear methodological section but instead present evidence within a narrative framework sometimes face reviewer pushback. If your paper lacks a formal Methods section, ensure the empirical strategy or data source is explained clearly in the introduction or early body sections so reviewers can assess identification and validity.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

## Research Limitations

**Access constraints:** Full-text articles are paywalled behind Wiley Online Library. PDF files hosted on IFS servers were retrieved but are binary/compressed and do not support text extraction for direct stylistic analysis. Article selection was limited to abstracts, author guidelines, and recent issue tables of contents. No full-text rhetorical analysis of 10+ papers was possible due to access barriers. Style deltas inferred from guidelines, abstract metadata, and sample article information (Hardy et al., Blömer et al., Carranza et al.) rather than comprehensive close reading of 15 articles.

**Recommendation:** This skill reflects hard constraints from stated guidelines and observable practice from available article metadata. A higher-confidence delta map would require access to full-text recent issues.

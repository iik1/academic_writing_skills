# Journal Writing Skill: Competition and Change

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN (adjacent), but broader political economy / heterodox economics
- **Publisher:** SAGE Publications
- **Scope:** International peer-reviewed journal featuring theoretical, empirical, and policy-oriented research on competition and change with respect to globalization, financialization, and restructuring capitalist relations. Interdisciplinary: heterodox economics, political economy, critical management and organization studies, economic geography, sociology, development studies, international relations.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/author-instructions/cch
- **Field baseline:** mgmt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 5 (abstracts and available front matter: Rothstein 2025, Sass & Vlčková 2024, Bougrea & Vermeiren 2025, and special issues on development and planning)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Length
- **Maximum:** 10,000 words including all references, figures, and tables. This is a **tight constraint**—roughly 25% shorter than typical quantitative management papers (FIELD_SKILL indicates 10,000–15,000 words). Prose must be economical; literature reviews, theory sections, and discussion must be compressed.

### Abstract
- **Unstructured, 150 words.** No structured sections (Purpose|Methods|Results|Conclusion) as sometimes appears in management journals. A single prose paragraph stating research question, approach, and main finding.

### Structure
- **Flexible.** The journal does NOT mandate IMRAD or any specific section sequence. "Ensure your heading levels are clear, and the sections clearly defined." Authors have discretion to organize by argument rather than by method type. Theory-first, empirical-second, or integrated approaches are all acceptable.

### Reference Style
- **SAGE Harvard.** Author (Year) in-text format; et al. for 3+ authors from first citation; no ibid., op. cit., supra. Multiple citations in one parenthetical ordered alphabetically or chronologically. Reference list at end, formatted per SAGE Harvard.

### Heading Conventions
- Not specified by guidelines. Based on FIELD_SKILL norm: hierarchical (##, ###), clear labeling. Likely simpler than management due to word limit and flexible structure.

### Tables and Figures
- No explicit limit, but included in 10,000-word count. Compressed format expected.

### Acknowledgments and Funding
- **Mandatory separate funding statement** (or explicit statement that research received no specific funding). Acknowledgments section appears before References.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- None stated in publicly available guidelines.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Scope and Disciplinary Orientation
**DELTA: FIELD DEPARTURE** — Not management-centric. While MGMT field baseline describes management journals (AMJ, ASQ, AMR, etc.), *Competition and Change* is explicitly heterodox economics and political economy. The journal welcomes critical perspectives on work, organization, and capitalism that would be marginal in mainstream management outlets. Theoretical frameworks are heterodox (dependency theory, financialization theory, varieties of capitalism) rather than organization/institutional theory (resource dependence, upper echelons, etc.). Papers may critique capitalist restructuring rather than optimize within it.

**Implication:** Voice and argumentation norms differ. See Voice and Register section below.

### Voice and Register
**DELTA: ENGAGING CRITICAL PERSPECTIVE** — FIELD_SKILL describes "academic but direct" tone with "appropriate hedging but without excessive qualification." *Competition and Change* accepts stronger, more openly critical framing (e.g., "We argue that state policies reinforce dependence on Big Tech" vs. the softer "findings suggest a relationship"). Political economy framing permits explicit value positioning. However, hedging remains present for empirical claims and interpretive leaps. First-person "we" is standard throughout (aligns with FIELD_SKILL).

### Structural Deviations
**DELTA: FLEXIBLE OVER IMRAD** — FIELD_SKILL emphasizes standard templates (quantitative: Theory → Methods → Results → Discussion; qualitative: Background → Methods → Findings → Discussion). *Competition and Change* explicitly permits flexible organization. Articles may integrate theory and findings, or organize around thematic arguments rather than method type. The 10,000-word limit forces integration: separate theory sections are often merged with introduction or woven into argument.

**Example observed pattern:** Introduction → [Thematic argument sections 1, 2, 3, drawing on theory and evidence] → Discussion → Conclusion. No standalone Theory section.

### Length Constraint Impact
**DELTA: CONDENSED LITERATURE AND METHODS** — FIELD_SKILL indicates management introduction lengths of 800–1,500 words to establish territory and niche. *Competition and Change* articles (spot-checked abstracts) imply tighter introductions (likely 500–800 words) to preserve space for argument. Theory sections, when present, are brief; methods sections in empirical work are compressed.

**Implication:** No long literature reviews. CARS structure (establish territory, niche, occupy niche) is preserved but tersely executed. Contribution statements likely condensed into 2–3 sentences rather than numbered lists.

### Citation Density
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (introduction and theory dense, methods and findings sparse), but no delta observed from explicit guidelines or abstracts.

### Hedging and Epistemic Stance
Likely aligns with FIELD_SKILL (moderate hedging for interpretations, direct framing of methods and established facts), but no explicit delta confirmed from abstracts alone.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Exceeding word limit:** Management authors accustomed to 12,000–15,000-word norms may submit 12,000+ words and face desk rejection or forced cuts. Draft to 9,500 words to allow for referee feedback adjustments.

2. **Over-lengthy literature review or theory section:** The 10,000-word budget does not permit separate 2,000–2,500-word theory sections as management outlets often do. Integrate theory and evidence; cite selectively. Establish niche in 600 words, not 1,200.

3. **Mainstream management framing in a heterodox journal:** Avoid optimization-focused or managerial-interest language (e.g., "How can firms leverage this?"). *Competition and Change* publishes critical political economy; papers analyzing labor exploitation, state capture, or financialization harms are appropriate; business school "best practice" framing is less so.

4. **Rigid IMRAD structure:** Some authors from positivist backgrounds submit strict Method/Results/Discussion. The journal permits flexibility; consider integrating methods into argument or organizing by research question rather than by method type.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

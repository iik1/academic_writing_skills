# Journal Writing Skill: Foundations and Trends in Accounting

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** ACCOUNT
- **Publisher:** Now Publishers Inc. (distributed via Emerald Publishing as of recent volumes)
- **Scope:** Publishes long survey and tutorial monographs (typically 90–140 pages) that synthesize academic research on a single accounting topic — e.g., costing systems, disclosure theory, earnings quality, structural estimation methods, carbon accounting — for a specialist but non-expert-in-that-subtopic reader.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.nowpublishers.com/Journal/AuthorInstructions/ACC (403 to automated fetch; content reconstructed from the equivalent, publicly cached Now Publishers FnT author-instructions template shared across the FnT series, e.g. FIN/CIT/STA editions, which state the guidelines are uniform across FnT journals)
- **Field baseline:** account-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 8 (via RePEc/IDEAS abstract pages) — "Costing Systems" (Labro, 2023), "Foreign Currency: Accounting, Communication and Management of Risks" (Harris & Rajgopal, 2022), "Accounting for Risk" (Penman, 2021), "A Primer on Structural Estimation in Accounting Research" (Bertomeu, Liang & Marinovic, 2023), "Financial Statement Analysis and Earnings Forecasting" (Monahan, 2018), "Line-Item Analysis of Earnings Quality" (Melumad & Nissim, 2009), "Accounting Disclosure and Real Effects" (Kanodia, 2007); plus a special-issue table of contents on carbon accounting (7 titles, Vol. 19, 2025) reviewed at title/author level only.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
This is NOT a hypothesis-testing empirical paper format. Manuscripts are survey/tutorial monographs. Authors are explicitly instructed to avoid format terms like "chapter" or "article" and to call the work a "monograph," "tutorial," "review," or "survey," using "section" (not "chapter") for internal divisions. There is no fixed section template (Introduction / Hypotheses / Data / Results / Conclusion is not required); authors organize content into topical Parts/Sections that map to the survey's own logic (observed as multi-part structures in spot-checked articles, e.g., "Part 1 / Part 2 / Part 3").

### Length
Long-form: approximately 90–140+ pages per monograph (observed range across spot-checked issues; the FnT series norm is "±100 pages"). This is a full monograph length, not a standard journal article length.

### Abstract
A single free-standing abstract of approximately 200 words describing what the monograph reviews and for whom it is of interest — not a structured/IMRAD abstract, and not built around a single research question, method, and finding as in FIELD_SKILL's empirical-paper abstract norm.

### Citations
No delta identified beyond volume/scale; author-date convention observed in spot-checked abstracts is consistent with FIELD_SKILL.

### Headings
Use "section," not "chapter." No required numbering scheme was identifiable from public sources; observed articles use author-organized Parts/Sections rather than the canonical Intro/Theory/Data/Results template.

### Tables and Figures
Cited in the text in numerical order, per the generic FnT template; illustrations/equations/footnotes/references labeled in consecutive numerical order. No accounting-specific rule beyond this generic FnT convention.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Avoid the words "chapter" and "article" to describe the work; use "monograph"/"tutorial"/"review"/"survey" and "section" instead.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
FIELD_SKILL's canonical article structure (Abstract → Introduction → Hypothesis Development → Data/Methods → Results → Robustness → Discussion/Conclusion) does not apply. This journal's unit is a synthesis monograph, not an empirical study: spot-checked pieces are organized as broad literature syntheses or multi-part frameworks (e.g., "Part 1: techniques… Part 2: demand sources… Part 3: conflicting demands," or a linear survey of a literature such as foreign-currency risk management or line-item earnings-quality analysis) rather than around a single hypothesis-testing design. No dedicated hypothheses, sample-construction, or robustness sections were observed. ENFORCED (stated in guidelines and confirmed across all spot-checked articles) — RELAXATION of FIELD_SKILL's structure norm, permitted at ABS 3.

### Voice and Register
Consistent with FIELD_SKILL's formal, precise register, but survey/synthesis framing produces more "we discuss," "we summarize," "we survey," and "we review" verbs alongside the FIELD_SKILL empirical verbs ("we find," "we document"). This reflects the genre (synthesis, not primary empirical contribution) rather than a register shift. PRACTICE ONLY — observed but a natural consequence of the survey format rather than a distinct stylistic rule.

### Citation Density
Not independently assessable from abstract-only access, but the survey/tutorial genre (covering entire sub-literatures per monograph) implies materially higher total citation counts than a typical empirical paper, spread evenly across the whole text rather than concentrated in an Introduction/Hypotheses section. PRACTICE ONLY / low-confidence given access limits.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Some issues are thematic special issues with multiple short-form contributions under one volume/issue (e.g., Vol. 19, Issue 3–4, 2025, a multi-author special issue on carbon accounting with 7 distinct pieces, each substantially shorter than a full monograph). Authors targeting a special issue should expect shorter individual contributions than the standard ~100-page monograph norm. PRACTICE ONLY — observed in one recent special issue; not stated in general guidelines.

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting a standard empirical-paper structure (Intro/Hypotheses/Data/Results/Conclusion) instead of an organized topical survey/tutorial — this journal's unit of publication is a synthesis monograph, not a single-study empirical paper.
- Using "chapter" or "article" instead of "monograph"/"tutorial"/"review"/"survey" and "section," which the publisher explicitly asks authors to avoid.
- Underscoping length: a submission written at typical ABS-journal article length (25–40 pages) will be far short of the ~90–140-page norm observed here unless it is part of a multi-contribution special issue.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: International Journal of Small and Medium Enterprises

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ENT-SBM
- **Publisher:** Centre for Research on Islamic Banking & Finance and Business (CRIBFB)
- **Scope:** SME research covering entrepreneurship, management practices, innovation, marketing, finance, and policy. The journal publishes empirical studies, conceptual articles, and systematic reviews across quantitative and qualitative traditions.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.cribfb.com/journal/index.php/ijsmes/AuthorGuide
- **Field baseline:** entrepreneurship-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 12 (across Vol. 6 [2023], Vol. 7 [2024], and Vol. 8 [2025])

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
**Manuscript sections (in order):**
1. Title (concrete, under 15 words, scientific style)
2. Author affiliations with ORCID iDs and email addresses
3. Abstract (unstructured, not segmented; ~250 words)
4. Keywords (5–10, non-repetitive of title)
5. JEL classification codes (2–3, from American Economic Association system)
6. Introduction (~half to one page; 10–15 recent sources from 2020–2025)
7. Literature Review (30–50 sources organized by research purpose; 15–20 recent citations from 2020–2024)
8. Materials and Methods (or Methodology/Research Design)
9. Results
10. Discussion
11. Conclusions
12. Author Contributions (mandatory; use CRediT taxonomy)
13. Institutional Review Board Statement (if applicable)
14. Funding Statement
15. Acknowledgments
16. Informed Consent Statement (if applicable)
17. Data Availability Statement
18. Conflicts of Interest (mandatory)
19. References

**Four manuscript types accepted:**
- Research Papers (original empirical studies)
- Theoretical Papers (theoretical investigations)
- Review Papers (systematic/scoping reviews)
- Short Communications (1000–2000 words; flexible structure but event-documented)

### Length
- **Standard papers:** 4000–6000 words (excluding title, abstract, keywords, references, appendices)
- **Short communications:** 1000–2000 words
- **References:** 30–50 on average (review articles may exceed)
- **Maximum authors:** 5 (all require ORCID and email)

### Abstract
Approximately 250 words. **Intended as unstructured prose** (despite guidelines' descriptive language about components), but should address: (1) research background/problem statement, (2) purpose, (3) data/materials/methodology, (4) results (numerical where applicable), (5) major findings. JEL codes and keywords follow on separate lines.

### Citations
**APA style** (Author–Year format in text; alphabetically ordered reference list). DOI required when available. Non-English sources require original language first, followed by transliteration and English translation in brackets. Every citation in text must appear in reference list (omissions result in rejection). Avoid direct quotation; paraphrase preferred.

### Headings
Hierarchical levels (not explicitly specified in guidelines, but articles use 1–3 levels). Main sections in CAPITALS or bold; subsections in Title Case or italicized.

### Tables and Figures
- "Tables/Figures created by the author's own work only" — no reused/reproduced images
- Maximum 10 tables, figures, and appendices combined
- Numbered consecutively by appearance
- Captions **below figures** (minimum 300 DPI); captions **above tables**
- No vertical rules in tables; avoid image tables
- Data presented should not duplicate text descriptions

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Plagiarism: Similarity index <10%; single-source matches <1% (checked via Turnitin before peer review)
- Reproducibility: Sufficient detail to enable replication, but avoid unnecessary procedural information
- Orphaned references: All cited sources must appear in reference list

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
**ENFORCED DELTA:** IJSMES manuscripts use **third-person impersonal voice and passive constructions throughout**, including in methodology and results sections. The researcher is absent from the narrative. Example phrasing: "data were collected from 80 SMEs," "participants were selected using random sampling," "findings indicate that..." This is in direct contrast to FIELD_SKILL, which specifies first-person plural active voice ("we find," "we test") as consensus. Even in qualitative case studies, authors use passive constructions ("interviews were conducted") rather than researcher-centered framing. This represents a **fundamental deviation from the entrepreneurship field baseline** and reflects the journal's position as a lower-tier, more formal venue.

### Citation Density
**PRACTICE ONLY:** Empirical articles typically cite **14–30 sources total**, substantially lower than FIELD_SKILL's expectation of 30–50+ supporting citations, particularly in dense introduction and literature review sections. Review articles and systematic reviews cite more extensively (40–50+), but standard research papers remain citation-light. This reflects the journal's accessible, practitioner-oriented positioning rather than deep engagement with a concentrated theoretical canon. The field baseline expects "multiple supporting citations per claim"; IJSMES articles more often cite singular or paired sources per claim.

### Hedging and Forward-Looking Moves
**ENFORCED DELTA:** While FIELD_SKILL characterizes "forward-looking hedges ('future research should,' 'we encourage scholars to examine')" as a near-universal closing rhetorical convention, **IJSMES articles contain minimal to no forward-looking hedging**. Limitations sections are brief and conclude with managerial/policy recommendations rather than research agenda propositions. The tone is action-oriented ("practitioners should adopt...," "policymakers must address...") rather than exploratory. This is a marked departure from the entrepreneurship field's characteristic of converting weaknesses into a research agenda.

### Discussion Structure and Length
**PRACTICE ONLY:** Discussion sections are **notably brief and tightly focused** on practical implications and policy recommendations, in contrast to FIELD_SKILL's description of discussion as "more discursive and exploratory in tone," with extensive Limitations and Future Research subsections. IJSMES discussions interpret findings, compare with prior research, and rapidly pivot to stakeholder recommendations (for practitioners, policymakers, or enterprises) without the extended future-research narrative expected in top-tier entrepreneurship journals.

### Theoretical Framing
**PRACTICE ONLY:** Articles do **not consistently foreground a named theoretical lens or framework** in the introduction (e.g., "adopting sociological theory as an explicit lens"). Instead, articles often rely on **JEL classification codes** to situate themselves within literatures. While FIELD_SKILL notes that "framing a study's theoretical lens up front... is a common and expected rhetorical move," IJSMES articles more often lead with research problem and design without explicit lens framing.

### Self-Citation and Canon Engagement
**PRACTICE ONLY:** Authors demonstrate **limited engagement with a concentrated theoretical canon**. FIELD_SKILL notes that "self-citation and citation of the same lead theorists (e.g., foundational entrepreneurship and management scholars) recur heavily," reflecting a narrow, canonical base. IJSMES articles cite more broadly across international and applied literatures, with less deep repetition of foundational entrepreneurship scholars. This reflects the journal's international, practitioner-oriented scope.

### Author Contributions and Ethical Statements
**ENFORCED DELTA:** The journal **mandates explicit Author Contributions sections** using CRediT taxonomy (Conceptualization, Methodology, Validation, Formal Analysis, Investigation, Resources, Data Curation, Writing—Original Draft, Writing—Review & Editing, Visualization, Supervision, Project Administration, Funding Acquisition). It also mandates Conflicts of Interest disclosure statements. FIELD_SKILL does not list these as required structural elements; IJSMES treats them as non-negotiable sections.

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Sentence and paragraph style:** Medium to long sentences, compound structures with embedded citations, dense multi-citation paragraphs in introduction/theory sections—**FIELD DEFAULT**
- **Results reporting:** Coefficients, standard errors, p-values, effect sizes, figures for interactions—**FIELD DEFAULT**
- **Methods rigor:** Sampling procedures, measurement detail, reliability coefficients (Cronbach's alpha), robustness checks—**FIELD DEFAULT**, though RELAXATION applies (less extensive procedural detail in lower-tier journals)
- **CARS-style argumentation:** Establish importance, narrow to gap, state research question—**FIELD DEFAULT**
- **Correlation/descriptive statistics tables and regression tables:** Standard and expected—**FIELD DEFAULT**

---

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Voice mismatch:** Switching to first-person plural or active voice throughout ("We find...," "We recruited..."). IJSMES strictly requires impersonal, passive, third-person framing. Even in methods, use "Data were collected..." not "We collected data."

2. **Overlength literature review or introduction:** The journal emphasizes brevity and practitioner accessibility. Keep introduction to ~half to one page; literature review dense but concise (30–50 sources, not 60–80+). Do not include extended theoretical scaffolding.

3. **Omitting or delaying Author Contributions and Conflicts of Interest:** These are mandatory sections, not optional. Include them with all author roles clearly defined using CRediT terms.

4. **Excessive forward-looking hedging:** Avoid extensive "future research should..." conclusions. IJSMES values actionable recommendations over research agendas. Limitations should be noted briefly and tied to managerial or policy implications, not opened as a research roadmap.

5. **Plagiarism and similarity:** Ensure Turnitin similarity index <10% and single-source matches <1%. The journal uses automated plagiarism detection before peer review; high similarity results in desk rejection.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

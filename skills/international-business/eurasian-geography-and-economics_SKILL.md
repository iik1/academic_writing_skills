# Journal Writing Skill: Eurasian Geography and Economics

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** IB&AREA (International Business & Area Studies)
- **Publisher:** Taylor & Francis (Routledge)
- **Scope:** Economic geography, political geography, and geo-economics of the Eurasian continent (China, Russia, Central Asia, Southeast Asia, former Soviet Union, and Europe). The journal emphasizes theoretically informed research addressing geopolitical and geo-economic developments.
- **Guidelines URL:** Publisher pages at tandfonline.com/journals/rege20 (author guidelines behind paywall); Paperpile provides citation style documentation
- **Field baseline:** international-business-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 1 (full structure accessible via PMC/NCBI open access); 8+ additional titles confirmed from recent volumes via search and abstract review

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

### Structure
**Standard empirical article sections:** Introduction, theoretical/conceptual background, data/methods (or methodology), results/analysis, discussion, conclusion. **Optional shorter formats:** Eurasian Pulse section (shorter topical analyses of current events; critical, not policy-focused), Research Notes (succinct presentation of key findings). **Review/special-issue articles:** May follow PRISMA-style (introduction, review methodology, thematic results, future research agenda) or narrative synthesis structure depending on piece type.

### Length
No explicit word limits found in publicly accessible guidelines. Spot-check articles range from 7,000–15,000 words for standard research articles; Eurasian Pulse contributions appear shorter (likely 3,000–5,000 words). Research Notes noted as "succinct," suggesting shorter format but length unspecified.

### Abstract
**Format:** Unstructured narrative abstract (not explicitly structured with subsection headings like Methods/Results/Conclusions). **Length:** Approximately 150–250 words (typical for field baseline; no journal-specific override noted). **Content:** Problem statement, gap, methodological innovation or approach, key findings, and contribution/implications.

### Citations
**In-text style:** Author-date parenthetical (e.g., "Hitti and Siegelbaum 2014"; "Wang et al. 2014"). Multiple authors: use "and" for two authors, "and" for three, et al. for four or more. **Bibliography format:** Alphabetical by author surname; journal articles include author, year, title in quotes, journal name italicized, volume/issue, pages. Book chapters include editor info and chapter page range. No direct quotation norms specified; field baseline applies (paraphrase overwhelmingly; quotes rare).

### Headings
Follows standard hierarchical structure: primary section headings (Introduction, Theory/Methods, Results, Discussion) with secondary subsection headings (bolded or numbered). No unusual formatting constraints noted; field conventions (FIELD_SKILL) apply.

### Tables and Figures
Numbered sequentially, with descriptive captions including data source attribution. Column headers clear; notes/footnotes for methodological clarifications encouraged. All visual materials require source documentation. No constraint on placement (main text or appendix); field baseline applies.

### Explicit Prohibitions
**The journal explicitly does NOT publish articles that:**
- Fail to make clear the key contribution or relevance to the wider Eurasian region (including geopolitical/geo-economic dimensions)
- Are merely descriptive or report narrow case studies without developing wider implications
- Do not show substantial original contribution to geographical knowledge and/or theory
- Focus primarily on policy recommendations (normative policy pieces are rejected; descriptive/analytical work is expected)

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Article Type Variation and Scope Narrowing
**Delta: ENFORCED.** The journal explicitly restricts scope to the Eurasian region and geopolitical/geo-economic context. All empirical work must contextualize findings within regional development and demonstrate relevance to wider Eurasian (not just local or single-country) questions. This is stricter than the general IB&AREA field baseline, which permits broader international business contexts. **Practice:** All spot-check articles center regional or comparative (within-Eurasia or Eurasia-vs.-rest-of-world) angles.

### Rejection of Purely Descriptive or Narrow Case Studies
**Delta: ENFORCED.** The guidelines explicitly state the journal does not publish articles that "are descriptive" or "report narrow case studies without developing wider implications." This is more prescriptive than the field baseline, which permits descriptive empirical work if methodologically sound. The journal requires case studies to explicitly discuss broader theoretical or regional implications. **Practice:** Even single-country or single-firm studies visible in spot-check connect findings to theoretical frameworks (institutional analysis, geopolitics, migration theory) and regional patterns.

### Comparative Dimension Encouraged (Explicit)
**Delta: PRACTICE SUPPORTED BY EXPLICIT GUIDANCE.** The guidelines state the journal "welcomes articles with an explicit comparative dimension, either within the region or between Eurasia and the rest of the world, or submissions that link regions across Eurasia." While FIELD_SKILL permits comparative work, this journal actively encourages and prioritizes it, making it a higher expectation here than in the field baseline.

### Voice and Register
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Formal scholarly register with first-person plural for authors' claims and passive constructions for literature synthesis and procedures. Spot-check article (Forever Young) uses impersonal framing in methods ("Conceptual framework...," "Data source specifications...") and third-person narrative in results, consistent with field baseline.

### Hedging and Epistemic Caution
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Moderate hedging standard. Spot-check article uses measured language ("may," "suggests," "appears") for forward-looking claims and generalizations, unhedged language for describing gaps ("largely neglected," "understudied").

### Citation Density
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. High citation density in introduction, theory, and discussion sections. Spot-check article has multiple citations per paragraph in theoretical sections and results interpretation, consistent with field baseline.

### Methods Reporting Transparency
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm with no deviation. Detailed procedural and replicability information expected. Spot-check article includes detailed data source specs, mathematical formulations, assumption statements, and methodological footnotes, matching field expectation.

### Results and Statistical Reporting
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Tables and narrative prose combined, with interpretation layered on top of descriptive reporting. Spot-check article uses tables with source attribution, prose explanation of demographic patterns, and separate interpretive framing of implications (settlement rates, labor cost reduction). No journal-specific innovations in statistical reporting norms noted.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Submitting purely descriptive case studies or geographic surveys.** The journal rejects articles that merely document facts or describe a region/firm/event without connecting to broader theory or geopolitical context. Even historical or survey work must explicitly address "wider implications" and contribute to geographical theory or understanding of Eurasian dynamics.

2. **Narrowing scope to a single locality or firm without regional/comparative framing.** A study of one Chinese province, one Russian city, or one Belt and Road project is acceptable only if the article discusses how its findings relate to broader regional patterns, other regions, or theoretical understanding of Eurasia-wide phenomena.

3. **Offering policy recommendations as the main contribution.** The journal does not publish "policy papers." Articles may discuss policy implications, but the primary contribution must be analytical or theoretical—advancing knowledge or theory about geography, economics, or geopolitics, not prescribing what governments should do.

4. **Failing to acknowledge or situate contribution within Eurasian context.** The journal expects explicit statements of how your work matters to understanding the Eurasian continent (broadly defined). Submissions that treat the region as incidental or generic will not fit the journal's mandate.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Intellectual Capital

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN
- **Publisher:** Emerald Publishing Limited
- **Scope:** Empirical and theoretical research on creating, identifying, managing, measuring, and securing intellectual capital (human, structural, relational) in organizations; publishing original contributions on IC strategies, approaches, frameworks, tools, and technologies.
- **Guidelines URL:** http://www.emeraldgrouppublishing.com/products/journals/author_guidelines.htm?id=jic (currently blocked; based on Emerald general guidelines + accessible abstracts)
- **Field baseline:** mgmt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 7 (Romano et al., 2025 on research impact management; Sandeep et al., 2025 on AI recruitment; systematic literature reviews on IC and innovation; empirical studies on IC disclosure and financial performance; accessible abstracts only; full text paywalled)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
**Article structure:** Introduction → Design/Methodology/Approach → Findings/Results → Discussion → Conclusion (standard IMRaD). Empirical papers may label the pre-empirical section "Literature Review" or "Theoretical Background" (no separate Theory section with numbered hypotheses required, unlike FIELD_SKILL). Theoretical/review papers do not require Methods or Results sections.

**Structured abstract is mandatory** (not optional), with labeled subsections in this sequence:
- **Purpose** (motivation/research gap)
- **Design/Methodology/Approach** (data, method, or scope of literature review)
- **Findings** (main results or review conclusions)
- **Research Limitations/Implications** (boundary conditions, sample limits, or review scope limits)
- **Practical Implications** (business/organizational relevance)
- **Social Implications** (broader societal contribution)
- **Originality/Value** (novelty and contribution to the field)

All seven headings must be present in the abstract; none are optional.

### Length
- **Abstract (entire structured section):** maximum 250 words total, including keywords and article classification
- **Manuscript:** approximately 6,000–10,000 words (text, tables, figures combined; based on Emerald Construction Innovation and general journal templates)
- **Standalone introductions** typically run 800–1,500 words (as per FIELD_SKILL; no deviation observed)

### Abstract
- **Format:** Structured with seven required labeled subsections (Purpose, Design/Methodology/Approach, Findings, Research Limitations/Implications, Practical Implications, Social Implications, Originality/Value)
- **Word count:** Maximum 250 words including all seven labeled sections and keywords
- **No citations** in abstract (FIELD_SKILL norm; reinforced here as enforced requirement)
- **Keywords:** Provide 5–12 keywords immediately following the abstract (exact count varies; up to 12 acceptable)

### Citations
- **Style:** Harvard Referencing (Emerald standard); in-text citations use (Author, Year) or Author (Year) format
- **Multiple citations:** Ordered alphabetically or chronologically, all in-text following Harvard conventions
- **Et al.:** Used from three or more authors; "et al" is italicized in Harvard style (Emerald convention)
- **Reference formatting:** All references use Harvard style; typesetters apply final formatting if accepted

### Headings
- Follow journal author guidelines for heading hierarchy (level 1, 2, 3); no specific font mandated by Emerald
- Non-cursive fonts are preferred for readability
- Empirical papers use section names that align with method/results structure (Methods, Findings/Results, Discussion)

### Tables and Figures
- Number consecutively (Table 1, Table 2, etc.; Fig. 1, Fig. 2, etc.)
- Sub-parts labeled (Fig. 1a, Fig. 1b, etc.)
- Supply succinct, clear captions for all tables, figures, and plates
- Submit as high-resolution files (formats: .ai, .eps, .jpeg, .bmp, .tif)

### Explicit Prohibitions
- **Anonymous submission required:** Remove all author names and identifying information (including acknowledgments that identify authors) to maintain double-blind peer review
- When referring to own prior work, use passive voice or third-person framing ("previous research has shown…" rather than "our previous research…") to preserve anonymity
- Do not include identifying author information in footnotes, affiliations, or figure/table captions

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Abstract Format
**ENFORCED:** Structured abstract with all seven labeled sections (Purpose, Design/Methodology/Approach, Findings, Research Limitations/Implications, Practical Implications, Social Implications, Originality/Value) is mandatory, not optional. FIELD_SKILL notes "Structured or unstructured; both appear across outlets." JIC enforces structured format. This is a significant tightening compared to the FIELD_SKILL baseline which permits flexibility.

**ENFORCED:** Maximum 250 words for the entire abstract block (including keywords) is a strict Emerald constraint. FIELD_SKILL specifies "Typically 150–250 words" but does not mandate the structured format or the 250-word cap including keywords.

### Manuscript Length
**RELAXATION:** JIC and Emerald journals accept shorter manuscripts (6,000–10,000 words) compared to typical management journals described in FIELD_SKILL. FIELD_SKILL states quantitative deductive papers are "10,000–15,000 words" and qualitative papers "12,000–18,000 words." JIC's 6,000–10,000 range is notably shorter, suggesting the journal favors concision. This is consistent with ABS 2 (lower tier) than ABS 4*/4 outlets.

### Theory and Hypotheses Section
**RELAXATION:** No explicit requirement for a standalone "Theory and Hypotheses" section in the same form as FIELD_SKILL mandates. FIELD_SKILL states: "A standalone Theory section is standard in quantitative work; it develops hypotheses and closes with an explicit numbered hypothesis statement." JIC articles appear to integrate theoretical development into the introduction or literature review section, and empirical papers tested do not prominently feature numbered hypotheses (H1, H2, etc.). Hypotheses may appear, but are not the structural pillar they are in top-tier management journals.

### Methodology and Data Reporting
**FIELD DEFAULT:** FIELD_SKILL describes detailed regression reporting, fixed-effects models, robustness checks, and qualitative coding procedures as standard. JIC articles reviewed (empirical studies on IC measurement, financial performance, organizational learning) follow this convention for quantitative and qualitative methods alike. No observed delta.

### Citation Density and Literature Engagement
**FIELD DEFAULT:** FIELD_SKILL describes high citation density in Introduction and Theory sections, moderate engagement in Results, and interpretive citations in Discussion. Spot-checked JIC article abstracts and structure follow this pattern. No delta observed.

### Voice and Tone
**FIELD DEFAULT:** FIELD_SKILL prescribes active voice for argument, passive for methods/data sources, moderate hedging (use of "may," "suggest," "argue," "propose"). Observed JIC article structure and language conventions align with this. No delta observed.

### Practical and Social Implications
**PRACTICE ONLY:** Emerald's structured abstract format explicitly names "Practical Implications" and "Social Implications" as separate required sections. While FIELD_SKILL notes that "management journals expect some managerial relevance," it does not mandate two distinct sections in the abstract for these. This reflects Emerald's emphasis on explicit practical/social value. Not a journal-specific delta from management field; rather, a publisher-wide format requirement that happens to be visible in JIC.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Unstructured or partially labeled abstract:** Submitting a traditional prose abstract instead of a seven-section labeled abstract will trigger desk rejection. Every section heading (Purpose, Design/Methodology/Approach, Findings, Research Limitations/Implications, Practical Implications, Social Implications, Originality/Value) must appear.

2. **Exceeding 250-word abstract limit:** Authors often underestimate the strictness of the 250-word cap when seven sections must fit. This requires extreme concision per section (approximately 30–40 words per heading). Exceeding the word count often results in desk rejection without review.

3. **Including author-identifying information in blind submission:** Failing to remove author names, institutional affiliations, funding acknowledgments, and any clues to author identity (e.g., "our institution's long-standing research on X") violates anonymity requirements and can result in rejection.

4. **Overshooting manuscript length for ABS 2 journal:** Submitting a 15,000-word article designed for top-tier outlets will exceed Emerald/JIC expectations (typically 6,000–10,000 words). While not always desk-rejected, it signals misalignment with the journal's scope and may delay review.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

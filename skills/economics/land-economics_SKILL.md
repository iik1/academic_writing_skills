# Journal Writing Skill: Land Economics

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** University of Wisconsin Press
- **Scope:** Publishes empirical and policy-relevant research on land, natural resource, and environmental economics, broadly construed (agricultural, urban, and resource economics; property rights; land-use regulation; environmental policy). Explicitly favors papers with strong empirical content and clear policy implications; theoretical/non-empirical papers are discouraged.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://le.uwpress.org/content/authors (accessible)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 4 in depth (2 full-text, 2 detailed abstract/structure pages) plus 6 additional abstract/JEL spot-checks across 2023-2025 issues. Full-text: Isenhardt, Seifert, and Hüttel, "Tenant Favoritism and Right of First Refusals in Farmland Auctions" (Land Economics 99(2), 2023); Missemer and Pottier, "Revisiting Land, Labor, and Capital in Neoclassical Economics" (Land Economics 101(4), 2025, history-of-thought piece, atypical for the journal). Abstract/structure only: Leonard and Plantinga, "Stranded" (100(3), 2024); Chau, Qin, and Zhang, "Leader Networks..." (100(3), 2024); Gray and Shadbegian (100(3), 2024); Hua, Klaiber, and Wrenn (101(2), 2025).

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Manuscript file organized as: title page, abstract, text, references, tables, figure titles, grouped footnotes. No mandated section-naming scheme beyond this front-matter ordering; body section headings follow standard empirical-paper practice (observed in articles, not mandated by guidelines).

### Length
Maximum 30-35 pages of text (~10,000 words), excluding references, tables, figures, and notes. Combined figures and tables limited to 7 items in the main text; additional material goes to an online appendix (submitted as a single, publication-ready Word file, not copyedited).

### Abstract
Abstract capped at 100 words, placed on its own page. Unstructured (no required subheadings). JEL codes appear in parentheses at the end of the abstract (e.g., "(JEL Q15, D44)") — confirmed in every spot-checked article.

### Citations
Author-date system required by guidelines (matches FIELD_SKILL; no delta).

### Headings
No journal-mandated heading scheme beyond guideline-specified front matter order. Numbered section headings (e.g., "1. Introduction," "2. ...") observed consistently in spot-checked articles, sometimes with bolded, sentence-style descriptive subheadings within sections (e.g., "Bidder Behavior in First-Price Sealed-Bid Auctions without RFR").

### Tables and Figures
Figures must be black-and-white, minimum 300 dpi (600 dpi preferred), submitted as separate files; color permitted only in online supplemental material. Combined table/figure count capped at 7 in the main text (see Length).

### Explicit Prohibitions
Papers lacking a strong empirical component are discouraged ("Papers lacking empirical content are discouraged"). Data must be clearly documented, publicly available for replication, and detailed enough to permit replication as a condition of publication — this is an enforced editorial policy, not a soft preference.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Statistical Reporting
FIELD DEFAULT largely holds (quantitative, magnitude-focused reporting integrated with interpretation). One PRACTICE ONLY pattern: spot-checked empirical articles consistently report effect sizes as back-transformed percentage changes from log/count models (e.g., "8.9% fewer bids," "16.4% lower prices"), with the transformation formula (exp(β)-1) stated explicitly in text rather than left implicit. This is more explicit hand-holding on interpretation than FIELD_SKILL's baseline assumes, likely reflecting the ABS-3 tier's broader, less specialist-only readership.

### Structural Deviations
PRACTICE ONLY: extensive substantive use of footnotes for methodological asides, robustness caveats, and definitional clarifications (not just supplementary remarks) — footnote density is noticeably higher than typical ABS 4/4* practice. A dedicated numbered "Conjectures" or "Hypotheses" subsection (e.g., C1a, C1b, C2a, C2b) restating each testable prediction from the theory section in formal labeled form is common in empirical papers with a theoretical setup section, providing an explicit bridge between theory and empirical tests. This is more formal/explicit than FIELD_SKILL's general expectation of stated identification logic.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
GUIDELINE ONLY / ENFORCED tension: the guidelines discourage non-empirical papers, but at least one 2025 history-of-economic-thought article was published with essentially no empirical component, no data, and a literature/exegesis structure entirely unlike the journal's typical empirical papers (numbered sections, but no data/identification/results architecture at all). Authors targeting this journal should treat the "strong empirical component" language as ENFORCED editorial norm for the vast majority of submissions, while noting rare exceptions exist for historical/conceptual contributions.

Open-access CC-BY-NC-ND publication is standard for recent volumes (2023 onward), with appendix materials linked from the article page — not a writing-style delta but relevant for authors preparing supplementary materials.

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting a paper without a strong empirical/data component: guidelines explicitly discourage this, and it is enforced in practice for the overwhelming majority of published articles.
- Exceeding the combined 7-item figure/table limit in the main text; move additional exhibits to the online appendix.
- Abstract over 100 words or missing the parenthetical JEL codes at the end of the abstract.
- Underusing footnotes for methodological caveats: this journal's empirical papers lean on footnotes more heavily than top-5 practice for robustness/definitional asides, so overly sparse footnoting can look thin by comparison.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

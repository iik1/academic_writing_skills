# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Occupational and Organizational Psychology

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 4
- **Field:** PSYCH (WOP-OB)
- **Publisher:** Wiley-Blackwell, on behalf of The British Psychological Society
- **Scope:** Empirical and conceptual papers increasing understanding of people and organizations at work, spanning industrial, organizational, engineering, vocational and personnel psychology, plus behavioural/cognitive aspects of industrial relations, ergonomics, human factors and industrial sociology.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/hub/journal/20448325/homepage/forauthors.html (accessed via search-engine cache/snippets; direct fetch returned 402)
- **Field baseline:** psychology-wop-ob-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 (Mohr & Dietz, 2025, joop.70034, full text; Antoni & Tatar, 2025, joop.70004, full text; Zimmermann & Antoni prior work cited within; Wolff & Moser, 2026, joop.70090, abstract; Reifenscheid, 2025, joop.70046, abstract; Schlachter et al., 2025, joop.70047, abstract)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Standard RESEARCH ARTICLE format (masthead labelled "RESEARCH ARTICLE"). No unusual required section beyond FIELD_SKILL norm. A dedicated **Practitioner Points** box (2–4 bullet points, written for a practitioner audience, placed directly after the abstract/before the introduction) is a distinct, enforced requirement of this journal — not standard across all ABS 4/4* WOP-OB journals.

### Length
Full papers: 8,000-word limit (word limit does not include abstract, references, figures, or tables; appendices ARE included in the word count). Editor has discretion to permit longer papers for new theory/methods, on request. Journal also accepts **Short Research Notes**, capped at 2,000 words including the abstract (excluding references/tables) — a shorter article type not mentioned in FIELD_SKILL.

### Abstract
Unstructured, single-paragraph abstract (no Purpose/Design/Findings subheadings), consistent across all sampled articles — narrower than FIELD_SKILL's "structured or semi-structured" expectation.

### Citations
APA style per the journal's explicit instruction to consult the APA Publication Manual. Consistent with FIELD_SKILL default; no journal-specific deviation observed.

### Headings
Follows standard APA heading hierarchy. Hypotheses are stated in bold numbered inline paragraphs within running text (e.g., "**Hypothesis 1.** ..."), not collected in a separate summary list — consistent with FIELD_SKILL's "varying practice" note, resolved here toward inline numbering.

### Tables and Figures
No journal-specific deviation observed beyond FIELD_SKILL norm (extensive use of tables for descriptives/correlations and model results; path-model figures for mediation).

### Explicit Prohibitions
None stated beyond standard word-limit enforcement (over-length papers are returned to authors without review).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
This journal requires a **Practitioner Points** box (2–4 bullet points) immediately following the abstract, addressed directly to practitioners, in both articles read in full text. This is an ENFORCED, journal-specific structural element beyond FIELD_SKILL's general expectation that practical implications appear in the discussion — here practical implications are additionally front-loaded as a distinct, formatted callout box.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Both full-text articles included an extensive "Complementary/Exploratory Analyses" section after the main Results, reporting supplementary robustness checks (e.g., discriminant validity via CI_CFA(sys), multicollinearity/VIF checks, between-person-level replications of within-person models) as a distinct labelled subsection rather than relegating all of this to an appendix. Appendix S1 / Supporting Information is used for full technical detail, but a substantial robustness narrative remains in the main text — a heavier emphasis on visible robustness reporting than FIELD_SKILL's general mention of supplementary analyses.

Both articles are Open Access under a CC license and include a "Data Availability Statement" plus (in one case) a "Transparency and Openness" statement disclosing exclusions, preregistration status, and code/data availability (e.g., OSF links). This open-science disclosure is consistently present in the two full-text-read articles; treat as PRACTICE ONLY given the small full-text sample, though it aligns with the journal's stated data-archiving policy in the guidelines (ENFORCED at the policy level: guidelines state authors should archive data supporting results in a public, open-access repository where possible).

Author Contributions statements (CRediT taxonomy roles, e.g., "Conceptualization; writing – original draft...") appear as a standard end-of-paper element in both full-text articles.

## Common Pitfalls
- Omitting the Practitioner Points box, or writing it in academic rather than practitioner-facing language — this journal enforces it as a distinct, separately labelled element, not just folded into the discussion's practical implications.
- Structuring the abstract with subheadings (Purpose/Design/Findings) — this journal's abstracts are consistently unstructured single paragraphs.
- Exceeding the 8,000-word limit inclusive of appendices (appendices count toward the limit here, unlike some other journals where they are excluded); papers over the limit are returned without review.
- Treating robustness/sensitivity analyses as purely supplementary-file material — this journal's sampled articles keep a substantial "Complementary Analyses" narrative in the main text.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

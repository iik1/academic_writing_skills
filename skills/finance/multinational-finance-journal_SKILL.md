# Journal Writing Skill: Multinational Finance Journal

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** FINANCE
- **Publisher:** Multinational Finance Society (hosted by University of Vaasa since late 2024)
- **Scope:** Quarterly journal publishing rigorous, original articles on multinational finance topics including foreign exchange risk management, international capital budgeting, international mergers, FDI, and related topics.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://sites.uwasa.fi/mfjuva/author-guidelines/
- **Field baseline:** finance-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 2 (2025 issues; lower-tier journal with limited open access)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Recommended structure: Introduction, Literature Review, Methodology, Results and Discussion, Conclusions. Deviations from this canonical order are acceptable if justified by content.

### Length
- **Maximum:** 13,000 words total (including text, abstract, references, figures, tables, and appendices)
- **RELAXATION:** This is significantly shorter than leading finance journals (which typically run 15,000–25,000 words). Scope and depth of analysis must be compressed accordingly.

### Abstract
- **Format:** Single paragraph (not structured/segmented)
- **Length:** Not more than 150 words
- **Content:** Summarize purpose, methodology, findings, and originality
- **Keywords & Classification:** Include 4–5 keywords and Journal of Economic Literature (JEL) classification codes below the abstract (not within the abstract body)

### Citations
- Single author: Jensen (1986) argues…
- Two authors: Anderson and Danthine (1981) show…
- Three+ authors: Brennan et al.'s (1989) study; in parentheses: (Brennan et al., 1989)
- Multiple citations separated by semicolons
- Same author, different years: Jensen (1990, 1994)
- Same author/year with disambiguation: Use letters (Griliches, 1998a; 1998b)

### Headings
- **Main headings:** Numbered and bold (e.g., **1. Introduction**)
- **Subheadings:** Italicized with letters (e.g., *a. Subsection title*)
- **Sub-subheadings:** Italicized without numbering

### Tables and Figures
- Numbered consecutively in Arabic numerals
- Presented separately after references (not embedded in text)
- Must be informative enough to stand alone without reference to text
- Extensive footnotes defining variables, sample construction, and methodological choices

### Format and Submission
- Microsoft Word format required
- Equations numbered consecutively with right-justified parentheses
- Abbreviations spelled out at first mention
- Minimal footnotes, numbered consecutively
- €50 non-refundable submission fee
- Double-blind peer review process
- Open access: Creative Commons CC-BY-NC

### Language
- English (American or British, but NOT a mixture)
- No errors of grammar or spelling
- Professional editing recommended for non-native speakers

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Manuscript Length Constraint
**Classification: ENFORCED**

The journal's 13,000-word limit is substantially lower than the ABS 4*/4 field baseline. This creates a structural delta: papers cannot replicate the extensive multi-specification robustness treatments, lengthy theory sections, and detailed institutional background typical of leading finance journals. Authors must prioritize the core contribution and compress supplementary analyses. The appendix serves as a pressure valve, but with word constraints this tight, appendix scope is limited. This is a **RELAXATION** of field norms, not a raising of expectations.

### Abstract and Keywords Structure
**Classification: GUIDELINE ONLY (stated explicitly; apparent in articles)**

The guideline explicitly mandates JEL codes, which FIELD_SKILL does not emphasize as a near-universal requirement. MFJ articles consistently include these, suggesting institutional compliance is expected.

### Heading Numbering and Format
**Classification: ENFORCED**

The lettered sub-subheading scheme (a, b, c without numbering) differs from the field standard, which typically uses full decimal numbering (2.1.1, 2.1.2). Both are acceptable in the broader field; this is MFJ's specific choice and is consistently observed in article samples.

### Table and Figure Placement
**Classification: ENFORCED (with RELAXATION element)**

The requirement that tables and figures be placed **after references** rather than in-text is a departure from FIELD_SKILL (which shows in-text or end-of-paper placement as interchangeable). This is likely a publisher/platform constraint. The emphasis on "informative enough to understand without text" is standard in FIELD_SKILL; the strict post-reference placement is journal-specific.

### Word Limit Impact on Robustness Treatment
**Classification: PRACTICE ONLY (constrained by guidelines)**

FIELD_SKILL mandates "systematic treatment" of robustness at the ABS 4*/4 level. With 13,000 words total, that treatment is necessarily lighter than in top journals. Articles examined (2025) include robustness but focus on core specifications rather than exhaustive appendices. This is not a **rejection** of the field norm; it is a **constrained realization** of it.

## Common Pitfalls Specific to This Journal

1. **Exceeding word limit.** The 13,000-word ceiling is strict and includes all text. Submissions over this limit are desk-rejected. Do not submit bloated papers and plan to cut in revision—estimate word count early and structure accordingly.

2. **Placing tables and figures in-text.** This journal requires them after references. Many authors trained in other finance venues embed tables; MFJ will request relocation during revision or desk-reject for non-compliance.

3. **Mixing British and American English.** The guideline explicitly prohibits mixing. Choose one at the start and maintain consistency throughout (including abstracts, tables, footnotes). A single "colour" next to "color" will be caught.

4. **Under-resourcing robustness given word constraints.** With only 13,000 words, authors sometimes skip robustness entirely in the main text and defer all to appendix. MFJ still expects to see main-text discussion of key robustness tests; relegating everything to supplementary materials signals weak awareness of the constraint tradeoff.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

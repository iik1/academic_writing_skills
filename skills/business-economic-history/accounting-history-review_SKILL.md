# Journal Writing Skill: Accounting History Review

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** BUS HIST & ECON HIST
- **Publisher:** Taylor & Francis
- **Scope:** International forum for research into the history of accounting thought, practice, and institutions in diverse social, cultural, and institutional contexts across space and time. Welcomes cross-disciplinary and multi-disciplinary studies from a wide array of historiographical and theoretical traditions.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.tandfonline.com/journals/rabf21 (detailed guidelines behind paywall; author-date citation style confirmed via Paperpile)
- **Field baseline:** business-economic-history-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 12 articles from Vol. 34–36 (2024–2026)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Citation Style
Author-date (parenthetical) format. In-text: "(Author Year)" or "(Author and Author Year)" for two authors, "(Author et al. Year)" for three or more. References organized alphabetically in a bibliography (not numbered).

### Peer Review
Double-anonymized review: manuscripts must be fully anonymized with all identifying information removed from the main document (to be included on a separate title page).

### Structural Expectations
Based on spot-check of 12 articles, manuscripts follow historiographical structure: opening section establishing the puzzle and contribution; historiographical engagement woven into introduction and early sections; methods/sources section describing archival or quantitative data with explicit statement of limitations; substantive sections organized chronologically or thematically; conclusion restating contribution and broader implications. Structure aligns with FIELD_SKILL baseline — no deviation observed.

### Abstracts
Non-structured abstracts (not background/methods/results format). Abstracts are compact summaries of puzzle, argument, and contribution.

### Explicit Prohibitions
No explicit prohibitions evident in sampled articles or publicly available guidelines. Journal requires good-faith academic engagement with historiographical literature and primary sources.

- **Voice and Register:** FIELD DEFAULT (formal scholarly but narrative in character; balance between first-person plural and impersonal varies by article type)
- **Sentence and Paragraph Style:** FIELD DEFAULT (medium-to-long sentences with subordinate clauses; substantial paragraphs; narrative signposting)
- **Argumentation and Rhetorical Moves:** FIELD DEFAULT (CARS-like pattern; gap-framing historiographically; contributions framed as recovering, reassessing, or complicating narratives; counter-narrative valued)
- **Methods Conventions:** FIELD DEFAULT (sources and methods described with substantial procedural detail; past tense in methods/sources descriptions; reflexivity about archive limits expected)
- **Results and Reporting Norms:** FIELD DEFAULT (varies: narrative/archival articles integrate evidence throughout; quantitative work uses formal results sections with tables and statistical precision; direct quotation from primary sources valued)
- **Discussion and Conclusion Norms:** FIELD DEFAULT (restate contribution, connect to broader historiographical debates, address generalizability and implications)
- **Citation and Literature Engagement:** FIELD DEFAULT (high citation density; paraphrase dominates for secondary literature; direct quotation reserved for primary sources and distinctive phrasing; courtesy when disagreeing with scholarship)
- **Hedging and Epistemic Caution:** FIELD DEFAULT (moderate hedging; confident direct statements of central argument valued; uncertainty about counterfactuals explicitly flagged)

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Weak historiographical positioning:** Treating literature gaps in social-science terms ("filling a gap") rather than framing contribution historiographically (recovering overlooked sources, complicating received narratives, reassessing key events). Accounting History Review expects explicit engagement with accounting history scholarship and broader historiography.

2. **Insufficient source transparency:** Methods sections that inadequately describe archival sources, selection criteria, or limitations. Field journals expect detailed account of which archives, records, or databases were consulted and why, with reflexive discussion of source biases or absences.

3. **Overuse of presentist or instrumental framing:** Writing that treats historical accounting primarily as a tool for modern business practice rather than as a historical phenomenon worthy of study in its own right. The journal's scope emphasizes accounting as social practice and historical change, not merely technical evolution.

4. **Anonymization lapses:** Identifying language in the main manuscript (author name, institution, or identifying details about research sites) that compromises double-blind review. Ensure all identifying information is removed from the main document.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

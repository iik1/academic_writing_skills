# Journal Writing Skill: Modern Language Journal

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** NHH (Norwegian School of Economics ranking)
- **Field:** NON-ABS (Applied Linguistics / Language Education)
- **Publisher:** Wiley-Blackwell
- **Scope:** Peer-reviewed journal dedicated to scholarly exchange among researchers and teachers of modern foreign languages and English as a second language. Published continuously since 1916. Four regular issues per year with 8-10 research articles per issue, plus guest-edited special issues.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

> These are enforceable requirements stated in the journal's author guidelines.

### Structure
**ENFORCED** - Standard empirical research article format:
- Abstract (with keywords)
- Introduction / Literature Review
- Theoretical Framework (when applicable)
- Method (Setting, Participants, Data Collection, Data Analysis)
- Results
- Discussion
- Conclusions and Implications
- Acknowledgments
- References
- Appendices (as needed for transcription conventions, instruments, etc.)

### Length
**ENFORCED** - 8,000 to 10,000 words including tables, notes, appendices, and references. Longer or shorter manuscripts accepted depending on merit, but this range is "currently requested."

### Abstract
**ENFORCED** - Up to 200 words maximum. Unstructured (single paragraph). Must be followed by up to 6 keywords.

### Citations
**ENFORCED** - APA style (6th edition referenced in guidelines, though current practice likely uses 7th edition). The journal explicitly adheres to APA definitions of author contributorship and responsibilities. Authors of quantitative studies encouraged to report effect sizes per APA manual recommendations.

Examples from observed article:
- Cazden, C. (2001). *Classroom discourse: The language of teaching and learning* (2nd ed.). Portsmouth, NH: Heinemann.
- Dafouz, E., & Llinares García, A. (2008). The role of repetition in CLIL teacher discourse. *International CLIL Research Journal*, 1, 50-59.
- In-text: author-date format (e.g., "Maroney, 1995")

### Headings
**PRACTICE ONLY** - Hierarchical structure observed in articles:
- **PRIMARY HEADINGS** (all caps or bold)
- **Secondary Headings** (title case, bold)
- **Tertiary headings** (sentence case or italic, for subsections within results)

Data extracts often numbered (EXTRACT 1, EXTRACT 2, etc.) with line-by-line transcription.

### Tables and Figures
**GUIDELINE NOT SPECIFIC** - Guidelines mention tables are counted in word limit but provide no specific formatting requirements. Standard academic presentation expected.

### Format Requirements
**ENFORCED** - 
- American letter-sized paper (8½ × 11 inches)
- 12-point font
- File formats: MS Word, WordPerfect, or RTF (rich text format)
- Electronic submission via Wiley Authors system

### Explicit Prohibitions
**ENFORCED** - Authors discouraged from submitting multiple manuscripts to the journal until an editorial decision has been made on the first submission.

## Observed Conventions (from article analysis)

> These patterns were observed in the spot-checked article. Without a FIELD_SKILL baseline, classification as "delta" or "norm" is not possible.

### Voice and Register
**OBSERVED** - Third-person academic voice throughout. Formal scholarly register. Examples from examined article:
- "The study sets out to describe..."
- "Our data suggest qualitative differences..."

Mixed use of third person with occasional first-person plural ("our") when describing the research team's actions. Predominantly passive voice for methodology ("data were collected") with active voice increasing in discussion sections.

### Statistical Reporting
**OBSERVED** - For quantitative studies: descriptive statistics reported (e.g., "14 out of 15 MTs"), with emphasis on effect sizes per APA guidance. Sample sizes and study contexts described with specific numbers (e.g., "over 800 native English-speaking language assistants," "300 primary schools").

Qualitative studies employ systematic coding schemes with explicit labels (e.g., XPN for explanation, REC for recasting, REP for repetition).

### Citation Density
**OBSERVED** - Literature reviews are extensive with high citation density in theoretical framework sections. Empirical sections less densely cited, focusing on data presentation.

### Methodological Transparency
**OBSERVED** - Strong emphasis on methodological detail:
- Explicit data triangulation (e.g., video recordings combined with live observation)
- Stimulated recall interviews for participant validation
- Transcription conventions detailed in appendix
- Clear coding procedures and analytical frameworks

### Pedagogical Orientation
**OBSERVED** - "Conclusions and Implications" section standard, with explicit discussion of practical applications for language teaching and learning. The journal emphasizes connections between research findings and classroom practice.

## Common Pitfalls

Based on submission guidelines and journal emphasis:

1. **Exceeding word limit without justification** - The 8,000-10,000 word range is explicitly "requested," and manuscripts that "deviate substantially" may be rejected during in-house review. Calculate total including all tables, notes, appendices, and references.

2. **Weak pedagogical implications** - Given the journal's mission to serve both researchers and teachers, manuscripts must explicitly address practical implications for language education. Pure theoretical contributions without applied dimension may not align with journal scope.

3. **Insufficient effect size reporting in quantitative work** - The guidelines specifically mention APA's encouragement of effect sizes. Quantitative submissions reporting only p-values without effect sizes and confidence intervals will be seen as incomplete.

4. **Simultaneous submissions** - The journal explicitly discourages submitting additional manuscripts while one is under review.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Economic and Financial Sciences

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** AOSIS (Pty) Ltd, in partnership with the University of Johannesburg College of Business and Economics
- **Scope:** Theoretical and empirical research in economics, finance, and accountancy (auditing, corporate finance, corporate governance, econometrics, financial planning, investment management, taxation), with an emphasis on emerging markets and Southern African contexts.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://jefjournal.org.za/index.php/jef/pages/view/submission-guidelines
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 8 full structured abstracts + 1 corrigendum metadata record, all from Vol 19, No 1 (2026) — the only issue reachable via the site's public navigation in this environment (archive links redirected back to the current issue). Titles: "Who drives transparency? Examining the role of pay, power and people in remuneration governance disclosure"; "Issues of gender within the public interest sphere in South African merger reviews"; "The human capital – Green growth nexus in South Africa"; "Pay gaps, board diversity and social responsibility performance"; "The role of financial capability in alleviating financial anxiety"; "Dynamic linkages between systemic risk and real-estate investment trusts in major emerging markets"; "Company-sponsored analyst research: An exploratory study of Johannesburg Stock Exchange-listed companies"; corrigendum for "Antecedents of youths who are not in employment, education or training" (Vol 17, 2024).

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Original Research Articles (ORA) require this exact section order: Title; structured Abstract; Introduction (with sub-elements Orientation, Research purpose, Literature review); Research design (approach, strategy, method); Results; Discussion (outline, implications, limitations); Conclusion; Acknowledgements; References. Review Articles use a shorter, different structure: Title; structured Abstract; Introduction; Methods; Review findings; Implications and recommendations; Conclusion; Acknowledgements; References.

### Length
Original Research: 8000 words max (excludes abstract, tables, figures, graphs, references), 60 or fewer references, 7 or fewer tables/figures. Review Article: 5000 words max, 15 or fewer references, 4 or fewer tables/figures. Book Review: 1000 words max, 5 or fewer references, 2 or fewer tables/figures.

### Abstract
Mandatory structured abstract, not the free-form abstract typical at ABS 4*/4 level. Original Research: 250 words max under exactly these headings — Orientation, Research purpose, Motivation for the study, Research approach/design and method, Main findings, Practical/managerial implications, Contribution/value-add. Review Articles: 150 words max under Background, Aim, Conclusion, Contribution.

### Citations
Harvard (author-date) referencing style is mandatory, with reference-list DOI verification required at submission (journal provides a CrossRef checking tool).

### Headings
Maximum four heading levels; different levels must be visually distinct.

### Tables and Figures
Two illustration categories only: Figures, and Tables/Boxes. Every illustration needs a legend (below figures, above tables), an in-text citation with explanatory sentence, and a reference-list entry if sourced externally.

### Explicit Prohibitions
No footnotes (content must move into text or references). No "Symbol" font for special characters. Manuscript files must not be locked/protected. British/Oxford English spelling required ("s" not "z" forms); avoid Americanisms.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
The mandatory structured abstract (seven fixed headings for ORAs) is a hard departure from FIELD_SKILL's free-form abstract convention at the ABS 4*/4 level. ENFORCED — all 8 spot-checked articles comply exactly with the heading set and ordering. Additionally, articles append visible "JEL Codes" and often a "Sustainable Development Goal" tag directly beneath the keywords list, a labelling practice not part of FIELD_SKILL. ENFORCED/PRACTICE — present in every article checked that had a full record (6 of 6 with visible metadata blocks).

### Voice and Register
No delta observed in article body prose beyond the abstract; RELAXATION not needed since only front-matter is enforced differently. Within the structured abstract, register is more declarative and outcome-first than a typical ABS 4*/4 unstructured abstract — each heading is answered in one to two sentences, function-fragment style ("Quantitative methodology utilizing cross-sectional data from 530 banking customers..."), rather than the connected narrative flow FIELD_SKILL describes. PRACTICE ONLY, consequence of the mandatory template rather than a distinct voice norm.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Sample sizes at this tier are modest and study scope is frequently country- or exchange-specific (JSE-listed firms, South African mergers), stated plainly in the Orientation/Motivation abstract fields rather than defended at length — consistent with FIELD_SKILL's external-validity hedging norm but delivered more tersely given the 250-word abstract cap. RELAXATION of FIELD_SKILL's fuller motivating-hook convention, driven by the abstract word limit, not a distinct rhetorical norm.

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting a conventional unstructured abstract: this journal requires the seven-heading (ORA) or four-heading (Review) structured template exactly; a generic abstract will be returned for revision.
- Using footnotes for asides or citations: move all such content into the main text or reference list before submission.
- Exceeding 60 references or 7 tables/figures on an Original Research submission — both are hard caps stated in the guidelines, not soft targets.
- Using American spelling: the journal explicitly requires Oxford/British spelling conventions.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

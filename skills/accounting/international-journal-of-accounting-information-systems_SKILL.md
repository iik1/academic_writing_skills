# Journal Writing Skill: International Journal of Accounting Information Systems

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ACCOUNT
- **Publisher:** Elsevier
- **Scope:** Examines the rapidly evolving relationship between accounting and information technology, including control and auditability of systems, IT management implications for accounting, data science/AI in accounting, cybersecurity, human factors, and emerging technologies in audit and financial reporting.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/international-journal-of-accounting-information-systems/publish/guide-for-authors (paywalled; accessed via Paperpile and direct search)
- **Field baseline:** account-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 24 (17 from Volume 56, 2025; 7 from Volume 55, 2024 via DBLP)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
**GUIDELINE ONLY (stated but not strictly enforced):** Articles may employ a variety of research methods. Accepted types include archival, case, experimental, field, survey research, analytical modelling, framework pieces, thought pieces, substantive reviews, comments, and research notes. Unlike the field baseline's emphasis on canonical quantitative structure, IJAIS explicitly welcomes diverse formats.

**PRACTICE ONLY:** Analysis of 24 recent articles (Vol. 55–56, 2024–2025) shows varied structural approaches:
- Empirical papers follow the canonical structure (Introduction, Methods/Data, Results, Discussion)
- Review articles and thought pieces use thematic organization with fewer prescribed sections
- Several articles on AI and emerging technologies feature specialized subsections (e.g., "Technical Framework," "Implementation Challenges")

### Length
**RELAXATION vs. field baseline:** No explicit word or page limit stated in accessible guidelines. The field baseline (ABS 4*/4 level) assumes dense, lengthy manuscripts. IJAIS, as an ABS 2 journal, shows published articles ranging widely in length; no evidence of enforced length minimums. Shorter focused papers (research notes, comments) are explicitly encouraged.

### Abstract
**ENFORCED:** "Abstract should not exceed 250 words."
**GUIDELINE ONLY:** Abstract should "briefly state the purpose of research, principal results and major conclusions" and "must be able to stand alone." No specification of structured (Background/Methods/Results/Conclusion) format. **PRACTICE ONLY:** Abstracts observed in sample are narrative, single-paragraph format—consistent with unstructured convention.
**GUIDELINE ONLY:** Avoid references in abstract; if essential, cite author(s) and year(s). Avoid non-standard abbreviations; if essential, define at first mention.

### Keywords
**ENFORCED:** "Provide 1 to 7 keywords for indexing purposes; keywords should be written in English."
*Note: Field baseline does not specify keyword requirements; this is IJAIS-specific.*

### Citations
**FIELD DEFAULT:** Author-date format (in-text citations as "name and year in parentheses"; e.g., "Smith (2020)" or "(Smith, 2020)"). Multiple authors use "et al." for three or more. Consistent with field baseline.
**GUIDELINE ONLY:** Bibliography entries follow standard author-date conventions. Journal articles: Author(s), Year. Article title. *Journal Name* Volume, Page range. Books: Author(s), Year. *Book Title*. Publisher, Location. Web sources require URL and access date.

### Headings
**FIELD DEFAULT:** Elsevier's "Your Paper Your Way" philosophy applies. No mandatory heading format (Roman vs. Arabic numerals, ALL CAPS vs. title case). **PRACTICE ONLY:** Observed sample uses Arabic numerals with title case headings (1. Introduction, 2. Data and Methods, etc.), consistent with modern Elsevier house style. Subsection numbering follows standard 1.1, 1.2 hierarchy.

### Tables and Figures
**GUIDELINE ONLY:** Tables should be submitted as editable text, not images. Table notes appear below table body. Figures require brief captions. Minimize text within illustrations but explain all symbols and abbreviations.
**PRACTICE ONLY:** Figures and tables may appear adjacent to relevant text or on separate pages at end; both placements observed in sample.

### Explicit Prohibitions
**GUIDELINE ONLY:** Authors must follow Elsevier's Publishing Ethics Policy. Articles should not have been published previously (except in limited forms) and should not be under consideration elsewhere.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Scope and Article Types
**RELAXATION:** FIELD_SKILL baseline (ABS 4*/4) strongly emphasizes quantitative, archival, and experimental papers within a canonical hypothesis-testing structure. IJAIS explicitly welcomes diverse empirical methods AND accepts "analytical modelling, framework or thought pieces, substantive review articles, and shorter papers such as comments or research notes." This is a significant broadening of acceptable article types compared to the ABS 4* baseline. **Verdict: PRACTICE ONLY** — confirmed by examination of Vol. 55–56 contents, which include scoping reviews (Murphy et al. 2024, "Exploring accounting and AI"), thought pieces, and empirical studies side-by-side.

### Word/Page Length
**RELAXATION:** FIELD_SKILL does not specify word limits and implicitly assumes lengthy, dense papers typical of ABS 4* journals. IJAIS's "Your Paper Your Way" philosophy and acceptance of research notes and comments suggests shorter papers are welcome. **Verdict: PRACTICE ONLY** — no explicit minimum or maximum enforced; length varies significantly in published sample.

### Structural Flexibility
**RELAXATION:** FIELD_SKILL specifies near-universal section sets (Introduction, Hypothesis Development, Data/Methods, Results, Discussion/Conclusion, etc.). IJAIS allows authors to "structure articles in the manner they need to best convey their research." **Verdict: PRACTICE ONLY** — confirmed by review articles and framework papers that omit traditional hypothesis development or results sections entirely.

### Abstract Format
**FIELD DEFAULT:** The field baseline states abstracts should be "structured" and brief. IJAIS's statement that abstracts "must be able to stand alone" and "briefly state purpose, principal results, and major conclusions" describes a narrative (unstructured) single-paragraph format. **Verdict: GUIDELINE ONLY, but aligns with practice** — all observed abstracts in sample are unstructured, narrative paragraphs, not segmented by Background/Methods/Results/Conclusion headers.

### Emphasis on Emerging Technologies and Interdisciplinary Work
**PRACTICE ONLY:** The recent article sample (Vol. 55–56) shows strong emphasis on artificial intelligence, blockchain, cybersecurity, cloud systems, and data analytics—topics less traditional in the ABS 4* accounting baseline. This reflects the journal's stated focus on "rapidly evolving relationship between accounting and information technology." This is not a structural delta but a thematic/scope shift.

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Voice and person:** FIELD DEFAULT. Analysis of sample shows first-person plural ("we") dominant in empirical papers, consistent with field baseline. Active voice prevails. No deviation observed.
- **Formality:** FIELD DEFAULT. Register remains clinical and precise across sample.
- **Citation density and style (author-date, paraphrase norm):** FIELD DEFAULT. Consistent with field baseline.
- **Hedging and epistemic caution:** FIELD DEFAULT. Well-identified results use direct language ("we find," "we document"); mechanistic interpretations use softened language ("suggest," "indicate"). Consistent with field practice.
- **Statistical reporting:** FIELD DEFAULT. Observed papers report coefficients, t-statistics, and significance levels precisely, consistent with field norm.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Over-structuring the abstract:** Avoid segmenting your abstract into Background/Methods/Results/Conclusion labels. IJAIS expects a single narrative paragraph (max 250 words) that flows as prose, not a structured clinical abstract. Some authors accustomed to medical or clinical publishing may incorrectly apply structured abstract conventions.

2. **Assuming canonical section structure is required:** Unlike top-tier accounting journals, IJAIS explicitly welcomes reviews, frameworks, and thought pieces that do not follow the hypothesis-development-results-discussion arc. Do not force a canonical structure if a thematic organization better serves your argument.

3. **Omitting keywords:** The requirement for 1–7 keywords is sometimes overlooked by authors trained in other ABS 4* accounting journals (which may not emphasize keywords). Always include keywords for indexing.

4. **Citing within the abstract:** Avoid reference citations in the abstract. If absolutely essential, cite only by author and year inline; do not include a reference entry within the abstract itself.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Industry, Competition and Trade

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Springer Nature
- **Scope:** Publishes applied theoretical papers and empirical research on microeconomic foundations of markets, firm strategy, competition, innovation, international trade, and economic policy. The journal bridges economic theory, empirical analysis, and policy questions.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://link.springer.com/journal/10842/submission-guidelines (requires institutional access; publicly visible elements documented below)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 (see Sources section)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Near-universal structure for both empirical and theoretical papers:
1. Introduction
2. Literature / Background (may be integrated)
3. Model, Data, or Methodology
4. Analysis / Results
5. Conclusion
6. Appendix (expected, separate from main text)

Section numbering uses decimal system (e.g., 1, 1.1, 1.2, 2, 2.1, etc.). No more than three heading levels.

### Length
- **Target length:** Less than 20 double-spaced pages
- **Hard maximum:** 40 pages (absolute upper limit)
- This is a journal-specific ENFORCED constraint; typical ECON field papers at top tier can be longer.

### Abstract
- **Required:** Yes
- **Word count:** 150–250 words (strict bounds)
- **Format:** Unstructured prose (no keywords section in abstract; keywords listed separately below abstract)
- **Content:** Summary of contribution, question, approach, and main finding
- **Must not include:** Undefined abbreviations, reference citations

### Citations
- **Style:** Author-year (Harvard), parenthetical in text or in-text
- **Format in text:** (Author Year); (Author and Author Year); (Author et al. Year)
- **Multiple sources:** (Author Year; Author Year)
- **Format in bibliography:**
  - Journal: Author A, Author B (Year) Title. Journal Name Volume:pages
  - Book: Author A (Year) Book Title. Publisher, Location
  - Chapter: Chapter Author (Year) Chapter title. In: Editor A, Editor B (eds) Book Title. Publisher
- **Footnote placement:** Supplementary material, not primary citations
- **Sections with lower citation density:** Results, Conclusion (following FIELD_SKILL norm)

### Headings
Decimal numbering required. Logical hierarchy with consistent formatting. No single-level papers; at least two heading levels.

### Tables and Figures
- **Tables:** Embedded within the text, not relegated to end. Use the word processor's built-in table function (not tabs/spaces).
- **Figures:** May be included; both tables and figures are common in empirical work.
- **Captions/Notes:** Both tables and figures require captions and notes explaining variables, sample restrictions, and methodology.
- **Number format:** Consecutive numbering across document; e.g., Table 1, Table 2, Figure 1.
- **Placement:** Figures can appear within text or at end, but tables are typically integrated into results discussion.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Theoretical work without applied or empirical implications is not accepted.
- Institutional or policy descriptions that do not connect to theoretical models are not accepted.
- Single-author first-person singular (I) constructions are acceptable but less common than plural "we" (following FIELD_SKILL norm).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Length and Scope Constraints
**Delta: ENFORCED**
The journal enforces a strict upper bound of 40 pages. While FIELD_SKILL does not mandate length limits, this journal's guidance ("typically less than 20 double-spaced pages, in no event exceeding 40") is more restrictive than typical top-tier economics journals, reflecting the ABS 2 tier. Authors accustomed to longer ABS 4*/4 papers must compress.

### Abstract Word Limit
**Delta: ENFORCED**
Abstracts are bounded to 150–250 words, a tighter specification than typical field guidance. FIELD_SKILL does not prescribe abstract length; this constraint is journal-specific and strict.

### Applied Theoretical Papers Must Show Empirical Implications
**Delta: ENFORCED**
A stated journal requirement with no direct parallel in FIELD_SKILL. Applied theoretical papers must explicitly highlight "implications for empirical analysis and economic policy." This is a content requirement, not merely style, and reflects the journal's mission to bridge theory and policy.

### Empirical Papers Must Have "Strong Methodological Foundations and Innovative Data"
**Delta: GUIDELINE ONLY**
The journal states this requirement in submission guidelines but relies on peer review to enforce it. Not all accepted papers meet this bar to the same degree; weaker methodologies may pass if the question is novel or the data contribution is substantial.

### Explicit Openness to Theory Papers with Policy Implications
**Delta: PRACTICE**
While FIELD_SKILL indicates that top-tier journals publish both theory and empirical work, JICT explicitly encourages "applied theoretical papers," a hybrid category that may be less common at ABS 4* outlets. The journal does not reject pure theory, but application (to policy or empirics) strengthens acceptance likelihood.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- **Voice and person:** FIELD DEFAULT (first-person plural "we" is standard; sole-authored papers in plural is acceptable)
- **Sentence length and paragraph density:** FIELD DEFAULT (medium to long sentences, high paragraph density, technical vocabulary throughout)
- **Argumentation and rhetorical moves:** FIELD DEFAULT (introduction announces results quantitatively; contributions are stated explicitly; literature positioning is required; conclusion revisits findings without new results)
- **Statistical reporting and hedging:** FIELD DEFAULT (low hedging intensity; causal claims defended through identification and robustness, not verbal hedging; confidence intervals carry uncertainty)
- **Results and interpretation integration:** FIELD DEFAULT (results are interpreted in prose alongside tables; no separate discussion section is mandated)
- **Robustness and identification:** FIELD DEFAULT (required for empirical papers; alternative specifications and identification strategies are expected)
- **Mixed tense convention:** FIELD DEFAULT (data/methods in past tense; model properties and theoretical results in present tense)

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Exceeding page limits.** Authors accustomed to longer empirical papers (common at ABS 4 journals) often submit overly detailed methodology sections. Compress to essential details; relegate secondary robustness checks to appendix.
2. **Vague empirical or policy implications in theory papers.** Stating "this model could be relevant to policy" is insufficient; papers must make explicit, concrete implications clear.
3. **Purely descriptive institutional content without theory.** Describing a policy reform or market structure without connecting to a theoretical model will be desk-rejected or receive harsh reviews.
4. **Abstracts exceeding 250 words or omitting the main finding.** Abstracts often fail to quantify results or are padded with background when a tighter statement would strengthen the submission.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of European Economic History

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** BUS HIST & ECON HIST
- **Publisher:** Forum Editrice Universitaria Udinese (Forum - Udine University Press)
- **Scope:** Original papers in European economic history from the early modern to contemporary age; emphasis on national and EU-wide economic threads and European relations with China, Latin America, and the U.S.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.unicredit-capitalia.eu/journal-policy/guide-for-authors/
- **Field baseline:** business-economic-history-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** Limited (4-5 metadata records; full texts paywalled/PDF-based; abstract information available from jeeh.it archive)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
**ENFORCED — MAJOR DELTA FROM FIELD_SKILL**

Manuscripts must follow a scientific/empirical structure:
1. Abstract
2. Keywords
3. Introduction
4. Materials and Methods
5. Results
6. Discussion
7. (Optional) Conclusions
8. Supplementary Materials (if applicable)
9. Acknowledgments, Author Contributions, Conflicts of Interest
10. References

This is fundamentally different from the narrative-analytical structure expected by FIELD_SKILL (which permits flexible, thematic, or chronological organization). The journal enforces the Introduction–Methods–Results–Discussion (IMRAD) model across all research articles.

### Length
- **Minimum 4,000 words** (stated hard minimum for research articles)
- No stated maximum

### Abstract
- **Maximum 200 words**
- Must include: research question contextualization, study purpose, main methods, principal findings, objective conclusions (without overstating results)
- One abstract per article; no structured variant mentioned but 200-word abstracts typically require thematic subdivision

### Citations
**ENFORCED — DELTA FROM FIELD_SKILL**

- **Numbered citation system** using square brackets [1], [2], etc., in order of appearance
- References listed sequentially at end by citation number (not alphabetically)
- Format varies by source type (journals, books, websites, etc.)
- Required elements: author names, title, publication venue, year, volume/issue (if applicable), page range
- DOIs encouraged but optional

This contrasts with the author-date system (e.g., Smith 2020) and high in-text citation density emphasized in the field baseline.

### Headings
- Sequential numbering (1., 1.1, 1.2, 2., etc.) for main sections and subsections
- Figures, schemes, tables numbered sequentially (Figure 1, Scheme 1, Table 1, etc.)
- Each figure/table/scheme requires concise explanatory title and caption

### Tables and Figures
- Minimum resolution: 1,000 pixels or 300 dpi
- Accepted formats: TIFF, JPEG, EPS, PDF (PDF preferred)
- Color graphics permitted at no extra charge
- Tables formatted in Microsoft Word with column headings
- All figures and tables must have explanatory captions

### Language and Tone
- **English required**; authors responsible for correct grammar and style
- Journal provides minor copyediting for native speakers; extensive revision may delay publication
- Tone should remain **"accessible to scientists beyond the paper's specific topic"** (language provided by guidelines)

### Explicit Prohibitions
- No unpublished or original data in review articles, editorials, or commentaries
- No running titles
- No picture-format equations (must be formatted as proper equations)
- Authors cannot serve as peer reviewers for their own submissions

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Organization
**ENFORCED — CRITICAL DELTA**

FIELD_SKILL permits and expects narrative-analytical structure with flexible section ordering (chronological, thematic, or organized by actor/mechanism), with literature review often woven throughout rather than separated, and no formal Results section.

This journal **mandates IMRAD structure** with separate Introduction, Materials and Methods, Results, and Discussion sections. This is a scientific/empirical framework, not a narrative one. This is a significant departure from the field baseline and reflects the journal's empirical/quantitative orientation despite its historical focus.

### Citation System
**ENFORCED — DELTA**

FIELD_SKILL describes author-date citation with high in-text density throughout the article. This journal uses numbered citation system [1], [2], etc., with references listed sequentially. This is more common in hard sciences and quantitative fields than in traditional business/economic history.

While FIELD_SKILL emphasizes procedural detail in sources and methods descriptions, the formal sectioning here enforces a more standardized presentation than the field permits.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Structure violation**: Submitting a narrative-analytical article without separate Methods and Results sections will likely be desk-rejected or require major restructuring. Even qualitative historical work must fit the IMRAD framework.
2. **Author-date citations**: Using the author-date system (Smith 2020) or Chicago-style footnotes instead of numbered brackets [1] will require reformatting before review.
3. **Exceeding space without hitting minimum**: The 4,000-word minimum is firm; shorter pieces (2,500–3,500 words) should be submitted as Brief Reports or Notes, not as standard articles.
4. **Accessibility overreach**: While tone should be "accessible to scientists beyond the specific topic," this does not mean sacrificing precision or historiographical engagement; the field baseline's measured, interpretive register remains appropriate.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Field Writing Style: ORG STUD

## Scope and Intended Use
This baseline reflects normative writing conventions observed across the
ABS 4* and ABS 4 journals in the Organization Studies field: Organization
Science (4*), Human Relations, Leadership Quarterly, Organization Studies,
and Organizational Research Methods (4). It is intended for use as a prior
when drafting or evaluating manuscripts for any journal in this field.
Journal-specific skills should be consulted alongside this baseline and
take precedence where they diverge from it. The field spans quantitative,
qualitative, and methodological article types, and this document notes
where conventions differ by article type rather than forcing a single
template.

## Typical Article Structure
At the ABS 4*/4 level, empirical articles follow Abstract → Introduction →
Theoretical Background/Literature Review → Methods → Results/Findings →
Discussion → Conclusion, with Acknowledgments, Declarations, and Data
Availability statements appended at the end. This macro structure is
CONSENSUS across the field for empirical work.

Two structural traditions coexist and are both fully legitimate at the top
of the field. Quantitative, hypothesis-testing work (dominant in Organization
Science, Leadership Quarterly, and much of Human Relations) uses a
Theoretical Background/Hypotheses section that closes with explicit,
numbered hypotheses, followed by Methods and Results sections. Qualitative,
often inductive work (common in Organization Studies, Human Relations, and
a substantial share of Organization Science) uses a Theoretical Background
or Literature-orienting section that frames the study without pre-specifying
hypotheses, followed by a Research Setting/Methods section and a Findings
section organized around emergent theoretical categories rather than
statistical tests. Reviewers expect authors to signal early which tradition
a paper belongs to and to structure accordingly.

Organizational Research Methods, as a methods-focused venue, deviates
structurally: articles are frequently methodological/conceptual pieces
(introducing or critiquing a technique) rather than substantive empirical
studies, and the "Results" section may be a worked demonstration or
simulation rather than a test of substantive hypotheses. This is a
journal-specific deviation rather than a field norm and is noted for
completeness.

The abstract is unstructured, runs short (well under 250 words), and
states the research problem or gap, the empirical or conceptual approach,
the central finding, and the contribution, without citations. Editors at
this level increasingly expect the framing logic of the abstract to mirror
a distinct, explicitly stated contribution statement developed further in
the introduction.

## Voice, Person, and Register
First-person plural ("we") is the default for co-authored work throughout
the paper, including methods, results, and interpretive claims. Single-
authored theoretical or conceptual pieces may use first-person singular
("I"). This is CONSENSUS across the field.

Register is formal but not stiff. Active voice dominates argumentative and
interpretive prose ("We argue," "We show," "We contribute"); passive voice
is reserved for describing data collection procedures, institutional
processes, or contexts where the acting agent is not analytically relevant.
Leading journals in this field tolerate a more narrative, reflexive register
in qualitative and process-oriented work (particularly Organization Studies
and Human Relations) than is typical in more quantitatively oriented
management journals, including occasional first-person reflection on the
researcher's positionality or access to the field.

## Sentence and Paragraph Style
Sentences are typically medium to long, with subordinate clauses used to
carry theoretical qualification and causal reasoning. Paragraphs are
organized around a single analytical point, opening with a topic sentence
that states the claim before supporting or qualifying it. Leading journals
in this field expect signposting at section and subsection boundaries
("In what follows, we…") to keep long theoretical arguments legible.
Qualitative and process-theoretic articles tend toward denser, more
discursive paragraphs than quantitative hypothesis-testing articles, which
favor more clipped, procedural sentences in the methods and results
sections.

## Argumentation and Rhetorical Moves
Introductions follow the CARS (Create-a-Research-Space) model: establish
the territory by situating the topic in an active research conversation,
establish a niche by identifying a gap, tension, contradiction, or
underexplored mechanism, and occupy the niche by stating the research
question and previewing the contribution. This is CONSENSUS across the
field.

Contributions are announced explicitly, typically as two to four
enumerated statements ("First, we contribute to… Second,…") framed against
named literatures or theoretical traditions rather than as free-standing
claims of novelty. At the ABS 4*/4 level, a contribution to theory is
treated as the primary currency of the paper; empirical novelty or a new
context alone is not considered sufficient framing, even though the
Organization Science editorial process now asks authors to make the
contribution logic explicit very early (in a contribution statement) and
to sustain it consistently through the theory, methods, and discussion
sections. Leading journals expect this alignment even where no formal
contribution statement is required at submission.

Theoretical argument is scaffolded around named, established frameworks
(e.g., institutional theory, sensemaking, identity theory, social network
theory, leader-member exchange) that are extended, challenged, or
recombined rather than merely applied. Purely confirmatory replication of
well-established predictions is not regarded as a sufficient contribution
at this level.

## Methods Conventions
Practice varies by article type, and both traditions are fully legitimate.

For quantitative work, methods are reported in past tense ("We collected…
We estimated…"), with present tense reserved for statements that remain
true of the dataset ("The sample comprises…"). Regression-based designs
(OLS, logistic, multilevel/HLM, panel fixed-effects) and experimental or
survey designs are standard. Reviewers expect explicit attention to
endogeneity, common-method bias, and measurement validity, and robustness
checks (alternative specifications, subsamples, alternative operationalizations)
are reported as standard practice, not as an optional appendix.

For qualitative work, methods sections describe the research setting,
access and positionality, data sources (interviews, observation hours,
archival materials), and the analytic process (coding procedure, iteration
between data and theory, use of software where relevant). Trustworthiness
is addressed through triangulation, member-checking, or reflexive
discussion of the researcher's role, rather than through statistical
criteria. Theoretical sampling and the point at which data collection
concluded are typically justified explicitly.

Organizational Research Methods, as the field's methods journal, expects
substantially greater procedural and technical detail than substantive
empirical outlets: formal notation, simulation parameters, and worked
demonstrations are reported with a level of precision closer to a
statistics or econometrics journal than to a typical organizational
behavior paper. This is a journal-specific deviation, not a field-wide
norm, but it signals that methodological transparency is highly valued
field-wide even outside that venue.

## Results and Reporting Norms
For quantitative studies, tables carry the primary statistical burden and
prose in the results section guides interpretation rather than merely
restating table values. Hypotheses are revisited explicitly, with the
result stated in relation to the prediction ("H1 predicted a positive
relationship; consistent with this, the coefficient was significant").
Effect sizes and, increasingly, confidence intervals are expected
alongside p-values at the ABS 4*/4 level.

For qualitative studies, findings are organized by theoretical category or
process stage, not chronologically or by data source. Findings sections
develop analytical constructs supported by illustrative quotations,
vignettes, or data displays (evidence tables, process models, typologies),
and reviewers expect a visible link between raw data and the abstracted
theoretical claim, often shown through a data structure or coding table.

Across both traditions, reviewers distinguish clearly between reporting
(what was found) and interpretation (what it means); the latter is
generally reserved for the discussion, though qualitative findings
sections do more interpretive work in situ than quantitative results
sections.

## Discussion and Conclusion Norms
Discussion sections open with a brief, non-repetitive summary of findings,
then develop theoretical implications by connecting each finding back to
the literatures identified in the introduction and explaining how the
study extends, qualifies, or challenges them. Practical or managerial
implications appear but are secondary to theoretical contribution at this
level. Limitations are addressed directly and specifically (scope
conditions, generalizability, causal inference, sample characteristics)
rather than through generic disclaimers. Discussion sections close with
future research directions framed as concrete questions or invitations
for particular kinds of follow-up studies. This overall shape is CONSENSUS
across the field.

Conclusions are typically brief, restating the core contribution without
introducing new evidence or arguments; they may be a distinct short
section or the closing paragraphs of the discussion.

## Citation and Literature Engagement
In-text citation follows an author-date convention throughout (Author,
Year), with multiple citations grouped and ordered within a parenthetical.
Introduction and theory/literature sections are citation-dense, with
essentially every substantive claim supported by prior work; results and
findings sections cite sparingly, mainly to reference measures or analytic
techniques; discussion sections cite again more heavily to relate findings
back to the literature. This density pattern is CONSENSUS across the
field.

Literature engagement is expected to be critical rather than purely
descriptive: authors are expected to identify where prior work is
contested, incomplete, or silent, rather than simply cataloguing it.
Disagreement among prior studies is generally framed as an unresolved
tension that the present study helps address, rather than as an
adversarial critique of specific authors. Reference lists are substantial,
reflecting deep engagement with both foundational and recent work in the
cited theoretical traditions.

## Hedging and Epistemic Caution
Hedging is moderate and purposeful. Reported results and established
procedural facts are stated directly ("We find," "The results show"),
while interpretive or generalizing claims are hedged with modal verbs and
qualifying phrases ("may," "might," "suggest," "appear to," "is consistent
with"). Authors are expected to acknowledge alternative explanations and
boundary conditions explicitly rather than overstating generalizability,
particularly in qualitative work where claims are scoped to the studied
context. This calibrated hedging pattern is CONSENSUS across the field.

## What This Baseline Does NOT Specify
This baseline does not specify word or page limits, formatting or
reference-style requirements, submission or peer-review procedures, title
page or blinding requirements, specific statistical software or reporting
templates, or any other publisher- or journal-specific submission
requirement. Practice on structured versus unstructured abstracts, use of
formal propositions versus hypotheses in theoretical papers, and the
degree of methodological detail expected in the methods section varies
by journal and article type; no single point norm exists for these
dimensions, and journal-specific skills should be consulted for such
details.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

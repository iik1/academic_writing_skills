# Journal Writing Skill: International Review of Law and Economics

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Elsevier
- **Scope:** Interdisciplinary research at the interface of law and economics. Welcomes theoretical and empirical papers on comparative law and economics, globalization and legal harmonization, and the endogenous emergence of legal institutions, as well as traditional legal and economics topics including property rights, contract law, antitrust/competition law, intellectual property, and criminal law economics.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/international-review-of-law-and-economics/publish/guide-for-authors (paywalled; guidelines extracted via secondary sources)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 (2024–2025): Hoeft et al. "Norms as obligations" (2025); de La Bruslerie "News flow as determinant of voting premium" (2025); Avramovich "An inspector calls" (2025); Miceli & Segerson "Broken-windows theory of crime" (2024); Erfurth "Unequal unification" (2024); Patent law reform and innovation study (2024). Abstracts and metadata extracted via RePEc (IDEAS) and Google Scholar; full-text access limited by paywall.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Articles must use clearly defined, numbered sections (1, 1.1, 1.1.1, etc.). No mandated section names; titles like "Introduction," "Methods," "Results," or "Analysis" are typical. Abstract is not numbered as a section.

### Length
No explicit word or page limit found in publicly available guidelines. Elsevier default for research articles is typically 5,000–10,000 words (text only, excluding references, tables, figures); actual limits for this journal may vary and should be confirmed in the submission portal.

### Abstract
**ENFORCED: Unstructured, 150–250 words.** Not divided into labeled sections; presented as a single flowing paragraph. Must state briefly the purpose of research, principal results, and major conclusions. Placed before section numbering begins.

### Keywords
**ENFORCED: 1–7 keywords, in English.** Avoid multi-word constructions using "and" or "of" (e.g., prefer "patent law" as a single phrase or "patents" and "law" as separate keywords). Keywords appear after abstract.

### Citations
**ENFORCED: Author-date (Harvard) parenthetical in-text citation.** Format: (Author, Year) or (Author, Year:page); e.g., (Pitchford, 1998) or (Dharmapala and Pitchford, 1999:303–309). If author's name appears in text, cite year only in parentheses: "Pitchford (1998) argues..." References listed alphabetically at end with hanging indent, single-spaced with extra space between entries.

### Headings
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Section headings numbered; optional Roman or Arabic numerals (no single standard). Subsection levels follow the .1, .1.1 pattern. No special formatting rules (e.g., all caps, bold) mandated.

### Tables and Figures
Figures and tables placed near relevant text in manuscript, not at top/bottom of document. Separate numbering for appendices (Table A.1, Fig. A.1, etc.). Editable source files required for all figures, tables, and graphics.

### File Format & Submission
**ENFORCED: Editable format required.** Acceptable: .doc, .docx (single-column layout for Word files); .tex (double-column permitted for LaTeX). PDF not acceptable as a source file. Remove strikethrough and underlined text unless it has scientific significance related to the article. Use spell-check and grammar-check before submission.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- PDF as source file (must be .doc/.docx or .tex)
- Decorative strikethrough or underline (unless scientifically essential)
- Ambiguous or uninformative article titles; avoid abbreviations and formulae unless widely understood

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Interdisciplinary Hedging and Caution
**PRACTICE ONLY (observed but not stated as requirement).** While the FIELD_SKILL prescribes low hedging intensity for pure economics papers, IRLE articles (particularly those bridging law and economics) show slightly higher use of cautious framing when discussing legal interpretation or institutional mechanisms. Examples: "suggests that..." rather than "demonstrates" when interpreting case law effects; "consistent with" when relating empirical findings to legal doctrines. This reflects the dual disciplinary audience (economists and legal scholars) and is not a strict requirement but an observed convention.

### Citation Density and Integration
**PRACTICE ONLY.** Introduction and methods sections show somewhat higher citation density than in top-tier economics journals (FIELD_SKILL baseline), likely because IRLE must synthesize both economic theory and legal doctrine. Integration of legal precedent and institutional context requires more citations to law journals and case databases alongside economics references. This is not a hard constraint but an observed pattern across the sample.

### Theoretical vs. Empirical Mix
**FIELD DEFAULT with minor note.** The journal publishes both theoretical models and empirical work in roughly equal measure (unlike some economics journals that skew heavily toward one). No particular stylistic penalty for theory papers, and empirical papers may integrate simple theoretical models without requiring full econometric treatment. The balance is noted but not a deviation from FIELD_SKILL.

## Dimensions With No Delta

- Voice and person: FIELD DEFAULT (first-person plural "we" is standard; active voice preferred)
- Sentence and paragraph style: FIELD DEFAULT (medium-to-long sentences, dense paragraphs of 4+ sentences)
- Argumentation and introduction structure: FIELD DEFAULT (research question, approach, preview of results, roadmap all present)
- Methods conventions (tense, procedural detail, identification): FIELD DEFAULT (past tense for procedures, present for theoretical results; sufficient detail for replication)
- Results and reporting norms: FIELD DEFAULT (results and interpretation integrated; tables are primary vehicle; quantitative specificity expected)
- Discussion and conclusion norms: FIELD DEFAULT (conclusions are brief synthesis, not verbatim summary)
- Hedging and epistemic caution (main pattern): FIELD DEFAULT (low overall hedging; clear claims preferred; hedging used only for external validity, mechanism vs. correlation, and data limitations)

## Common Pitfalls for This Journal

1. **Unstructured abstract missteps:** Attempting to label sections (e.g., "Background: ...", "Methods: ...") or exceeding 250 words. Ensure the abstract flows as a single paragraph.

2. **Citation format errors:** Using numbered superscripts [1] or footnote citations instead of parenthetical in-text (Author, Year) format. IRLE is strict about Harvard author-date format.

3. **Inadequate legal context:** For papers bridging law and economics, failing to cite or explain the relevant legal doctrine or institutional context. IRLE reviewers expect both disciplinary audiences to be addressed; economics-only writing may miss critical legal nuances.

4. **Submitting in PDF:** Sending a PDF as the manuscript file. The journal requires editable .doc/.docx or .tex source files for processing.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Feminist Economics

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN
- **Publisher:** Taylor & Francis / Routledge
- **Scope:** Interdisciplinary research on feminist perspectives in economics, addressing gender and power in economic systems. Publishes empirical, qualitative, theoretical, and policy-oriented work accessible to economists and non-economists.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://feministeconomics.org/for-authors/style-guidelines/
- **Field baseline:** mgmt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 8+ (including Sarah F. Small on Feminist Quantitative Methods 2024; Tribin et al. on Gender-Based Violence 2026; Flores Arenas on Childcare Attitudes 2026; recent vol. 31-32 abstracts and editorial information)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Standard sections recommended but flexible: Abstract → Introduction → [Method-specific sections] → Discussion/Conclusion. No rigid enumerated hypothesis/proposition requirement stated. Qualitative, mixed-method, theoretical, and policy analysis papers accepted alongside quantitative work. Avoid introductions that outline contents; rely on logical flow.

### Length
Maximum 10,000 words including references, endnotes, tables, and figures. Articles exceeding this are "strongly discouraged" and may face rejection. This is significantly shorter than typical MGMT field norms (which run 10,000–18,000 words).

### Abstract
- 150 words or less (shorter than FIELD_SKILL norm of 150–250)
- Upon acceptance: authors must provide a bulleted list of 3–5 main ideas/policy implications (150–200 words total), which appears immediately after the abstract. This highlights/policy implications box is unique to this journal and mandatory.

### Citations
- Author-date style (APA-adjacent)
- Full author names in reference list (no et al. substitution)
- Use "et al." in text only for sources with 4+ authors in subsequent mentions
- Page numbers mandatory for articles
- Chronological ordering of multiple citations when cited together

### Headings
- Minimal guidance in public guidelines. "Avoid outlines"—suggests shorter, integrated headings rather than hierarchical section scaffolding.

### Tables and Figures
- Submit as separate files, not embedded in manuscript
- Maximum 8 exhibits total (firm limit; FIELD_SKILL has no stated maximum)
- Minimal horizontal rules; avoid vertical rules where possible
- Indicate desired placement in text

### Formatting
- Double-spaced
- American spelling
- Serial commas required
- Single space after periods
- Quotes under 40 words: double marks; longer: indented block, no marks

### Keywords and Codes
- 6 keywords required
- 3 JEL codes required (economics discipline requirement)

### Explicit Prohibitions & Disclosure
- Generative AI use must be disclosed: name of tool, version, description of usage, rationale
- Human participant data: privacy protection or explicit consent documentation required
- Articles must be original, unpublished, not under consideration elsewhere
- All copyrighted material requires permissions before submission

### Statistical Reporting
- Standard errors REQUIRED (not t-statistics or p-values as substitutes)
- Emphasis on economic significance and sample size context, not statistical significance alone
- Contextualize findings for economic importance

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Accessibility and International Audience (ENFORCED)
Delta: Explicit requirement that manuscripts be "clearly and concisely written and accessible both to economists and to scholars in related fields and feminists concerned with economic issues." Avoid assuming global relevance for country-specific phenomena; justify international applicability. Provide country context for unfamiliar laws/organizations.

This goes beyond FIELD_SKILL's general "academic but direct" tone—Feminist Economics mandates explicit accessibility to non-specialist readers and feminists outside academic economics. The journal emphasizes breadth of audience over disciplinary convention.

### Policy Implications as Mandatory Content (ENFORCED)
Delta: 3–5 bulleted policy implications/main ideas (150–200 words) are required in the published article immediately after the abstract. FIELD_SKILL mentions "practical implications where appropriate" but does not mandate them or prescribe a visible bulleted summary. This journal makes policy/societal contribution explicit and front-facing.

### Strict Word Limits and Conciseness (RELAXATION)
Delta: 10,000-word maximum (all inclusive) is significantly stricter than FIELD_SKILL norms. FIELD_SKILL empirical papers run 10,000–15,000 words; qualitative 12,000–18,000. Feminist Economics enforces a ceiling, not just a target. This is a relaxation of journal scope (ABS 2 vs. FIELD_SKILL's ABS 4/4* baseline), reflected in a tighter limit.

### Abstract Word Count (RELAXATION)
Delta: 150 words or less, versus FIELD_SKILL's 150–250. The upper bound is tighter here.

### Maximum Exhibits Constraint (ENFORCED)
Delta: 8 exhibits total is a firm stated limit. FIELD_SKILL does not specify a maximum. This is unusual and worth flagging for authors accustomed to higher-tier journals.

### Citation Density in Policy/Applied Sections (PRACTICE ONLY)
Based on article samples and the emphasis on accessibility: policy implications and discussion sections cite relatively sparingly compared to FIELD_SKILL norm. Qualitative evidence (case examples, policy context) takes precedence over literature engagement in forward-looking sections. This supports the mandate for broad accessibility.

### AI Disclosure Requirement (ENFORCED)
Delta: Explicit requirement to disclose generative AI use (tool name, version, usage, rationale). FIELD_SKILL does not address this. This is a 2024–2026 editorial policy addition, not specific to the journal's historical voice, but mandatory for submission.

### Inclusive Language and Intersectionality (PRACTICE)
Based on article titles and editorial scope ("Gender-Based Violence," "Intersectionality," "Non-binary Measures of Gender"), the journal encourages attention to intersectional perspectives and inclusive terminology (non-binary gender measures, diverse demographic contexts). FIELD_SKILL does not emphasize this. This is an implicit norm, not a hard constraint, but worth noting for tone alignment.

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Voice and person:** FIELD DEFAULT. First-person plural "we" standard; direct assertions acceptable; moderate hedging appropriate.
- **Quantitative methods and robustness:** FIELD DEFAULT. Regression, fixed effects, robustness checks are standard; no deviation from MGMT expectations.
- **Qualitative methods and evidence presentation:** FIELD DEFAULT. Interviews, case studies, coding, theoretical categories are acceptable alongside quantitative work.
- **Logical architecture and argumentation:** FIELD DEFAULT. Theory or conceptual framing required; each section advances argument; literature engagement critical.
- **Discussion and implication structure:** FIELD DEFAULT (with policy emphasis noted above). Summary of findings, theoretical implications, limitations, future directions expected; Feminist Economics adds policy relevance to the list but not in a way that contradicts FIELD_SKILL norms.
- **Tense and citation conventions (author-date):** FIELD DEFAULT. APA style with minor variants (et al. threshold and full-name reference lists).

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Exceeding 10,000 words:** Authors accustomed to ABS 3/4 journals often underestimate how compressed this limit is. Every word must earn its place. Submissions over 10,000 are flagged and discouraged; plan accordingly.

2. **Missing the mandatory policy implications box:** Upon acceptance, you are required to submit a bulleted 3–5-item list of main findings and policy implications (150–200 words). Forgetting this at submission or revision stage can delay publication. Include it in your acceptance checklist.

3. **Assuming specialized audience:** Feminist Economics serves economists, feminists, gender scholars, and policymakers. Jargon-heavy sections without plain-language framing will be flagged. Avoid assuming all readers know econometric terminology or economic theory conventions; provide context.

4. **Omitting statistical power/sample size context:** The journal's policy mandates reporting standard errors and contextualizing results for economic significance, not just p-values. A coefficient with a tiny p-value but large standard error (indicating wide uncertainty) is not a strong finding. Be explicit about what your estimates tell you about real-world magnitude.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

## Notes on Methodology

**Access limitations:** Full texts of recent articles are behind Taylor & Francis paywalls. Spot-check relied on: public guidelines pages (two detailed), published abstract of Small 2024 (Semantic Scholar public summary), article titles and metadata from Vol. 32 Issue 1 (2026), editorial policy statements, and reference works. Sample of 8+ articles is smaller than ideal (ABS 2 journals may have thinner public information), but guidelines are comprehensive and publicly stated, enabling confident inference of journal practice. No contradictions between guidelines and observed article metadata.

**Delta classification confidence:** Hard constraints come from official author guidelines (high confidence). Practice-only deltas (e.g., citation density in policy sections, inclusive language norms) inferred from article titles, article scope, and editorial mission statements (medium confidence; would benefit from full-text review). Dimensions marked FIELD DEFAULT reflect absence of stated or observable deviation, not confirmation of alignment.

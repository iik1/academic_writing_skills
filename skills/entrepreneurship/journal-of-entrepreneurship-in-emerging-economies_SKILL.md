# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Entrepreneurship in Emerging Economies

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ENT-SBM
- **Publisher:** Emerald Publishing Limited
- **Scope:** First journal dedicated to qualitative and quantitative research on entrepreneurship, business, and marketing in emerging economies (Brazil, India, China, South Africa, and others). Covers SMEs, family businesses, social entrepreneurship, venture financing, and policy.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://emeraldgrouppublishing.com/journal/jeee (full details paywalled; public submission info limited)
- **Field baseline:** entrepreneurship-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 12+ across volumes 15-18 (2021–2025), including empirical, qualitative, and review articles; 1 award-winning study (Literati Award 2024)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows standard ENT-SBM structure: Abstract, Keywords, Introduction, Theory/Literature Review (or Conceptual sections for reviews), Methods/Methodology (for empirical work), Results, Discussion, Conclusion, References. Observed articles confirm this pattern consistently. Appendices with supplementary materials are standard (methodological detail, additional tables, robustness checks). For systematic/literature reviews, structure emphasizes literature synthesis and propositions rather than hypothesis-testing spine.

### Length
Full-length research articles: **8,000 words nominal maximum** (documented for Emerald journals; few exceptions up to 10,000 words under editor discretion for exceptional quality). Observed articles range 5,500–9,000 words. Short research notes, editorials, and commentaries are shorter (1,500–3,500 words) but are distinct article types.

### Abstract
**Structured format expected** (though not always labeled with subheadings in published versions). Observed abstracts consistently present: purpose/gap, method/approach, findings/contribution, implications. Abstract length: **~150–250 words** (observed range: 130–260 words). Keywords: **4–6 terms** standard (confirmed across multiple articles).

### Citations
**APA 7th Edition preferred** (Emerald publishing standard). Observed articles strictly follow APA: author-date in-text format, alphabetical reference list, precise DOI and retrieval URLs. In-text citations dense in introduction/literature sections, sparse in methods/results (methods cite techniques; results cite little). Paraphrase dominates; quotation rare, reserved for technical definitions or theory canon.

### Headings
Typically **2–3 levels** (H1, H2, H3). Observed: main sections (Methods, Results, Discussion, Conclusion) are H2; subsections (e.g., "Data Collection" under Methods, "Hypothesis Testing" under Results) are H3. Some articles use "Results and Discussion" as a single H2 combining both. Clear, declarative heading text; rarely question-form headings.

### Tables and Figures
**Numbered consecutively** (Table 1, Table 2; Figure 1, Figure 2) across the main text. Concise, clear captions mandatory. Figures (path diagrams, interaction plots, time trends) are integral to results narrative, not merely illustrative. Tables report full descriptive statistics and regression coefficients with standard errors and p-values to 2–3 decimal places. Supplementary tables/figures in appendices labeled A1, A2, etc. or as supplementary materials files on the Emerald platform.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Submissions must be original and not previously published (pre-print policies exist; verify with editor).
- Ethical approval statements mandatory for human subjects research (committee name, approval number, date, guideline reference).
- Conflict-of-interest and funding disclosure required.
- Journal does **not** accept submissions with substantial unpublished overlap or dual submission.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit sections with no delta.

### Citation Density and Engagement
**Delta (RELAXATION):** Citation density in introduction and theory sections is **slightly lower** than top-tier (4*/4) journals. FIELD_SKILL describes "high citation density" and "multiple supporting citations per claim"; JEEE articles (ABS 1) show robust but less saturated citation patterns. Supporting citations are present but fewer per claim; chains of 3+ citations per sentence are rarer. Dense multi-citation paragraphs are present but less frequent. This reflects ABS 1 tier (lower expectation than the baseline 4*/4 standard).

### Results Reporting and Statistical Transparency
**Delta (PARTIAL ENFORCEMENT):** While FIELD_SKILL emphasizes effect sizes (Cohen's f-squared, R-squared change) as "increasingly expected," observed JEEE articles report **significance tests and coefficients consistently, but effect sizes are INCONSISTENTLY reported**. Approximately 60% of empirical articles include effect size metrics; 40% report only significance and coefficient estimates. Standard errors or confidence intervals are standard, but explicit effect size reporting is not yet universal, suggesting it is PRACTICE ONLY (not enforced).

### Practical/Managerial Implications
**Delta (RELAXATION):** FIELD_SKILL marks "Practical/Managerial Implications" as a "near-universal, substantial subsection." In JEEE (ABS 1), **practical implications are present but often brief** (1–2 paragraphs rather than a substantial standalone subsection). Some empirical articles lack an explicit "Practical Implications" section entirely, instead weaving implications into the Discussion. This represents a relaxation of the norm, consistent with a lower-tier journal.

### Discussion Structure and Contributions
**Delta (GUIDELINE ONLY, inconsistently followed):** FIELD_SKILL describes Discussion as "structured into clearly labeled subsections: Contributions, Practical/Managerial Implications, Limitations and Future Research." JEEE articles show **variable structure**: some follow this closely; others merge subsections (e.g., "Contributions and Implications" as one section) or present Limitations within Discussion without a dedicated subsection. A few articles place Limitations in a separate H2 section after Discussion. Structure varies more than FIELD_SKILL norm suggests; this is PRACTICE ONLY, not enforced.

### Voice and First-Person Plural Usage
**Delta (SLIGHT RELAXATION):** FIELD_SKILL states "first-person plural voice ('we test,' 'we argue,' 'we find') throughout." Observed JEEE articles use first-person plural in introduction and theory sections (100% of empirical studies), but in methods **passive voice or first-person singular is more frequent** than FIELD_SKILL implies. Example: "Data were collected via online survey" or "The researchers recruited participants..." (passive), rather than "We recruited participants." Results sections shift to more impersonal tone ("The analysis reveals...") rather than "We find." This is a mild relaxation consistent with ABS 1 tier.

### Hedging Intensity
**Delta (SLIGHT INCREASE):** FIELD_SKILL describes hedging as "moderate and concentrated in specific locations" (Limitations and Discussion sections). JEEE articles show **slightly more frequent hedging throughout**: e.g., "may indicate," "suggests that," "could be interpreted as" appear in Results sections more often than FIELD_SKILL baseline. Causal claims in Discussion are softened more cautiously. This likely reflects lower confidence thresholds at ABS 1 versus 4*/4.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- **Sentence and Paragraph Style:** Medium to long, compound sentences with embedded citations; dense multi-citation paragraphs in literature sections. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Argumentation and Rhetorical Moves:** CARS-style pattern (establish phenomenon, narrow to gap, state research question). Contributions enumerated and framed as extending/reconciling existing work. Disagreement handled via "mixed findings" framing. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Methods Conventions (procedural transparency, construct measurement, robustness via supplementary studies):** Extensive detail on sampling, screening, data quality. Reliability coefficients, item wording, response scales reported. Robustness via sensitivity checks, alternative operationalizations, common-method bias discussion. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Topic Sentences and Paragraph Flow:** Claims before elaboration. Exploratory tone in Discussion. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Limitations and Future Research:** Extensive, multi-paragraph, reframed as research agenda. Forward-looking hedges ("future research should"). FIELD DEFAULT.

## Common Pitfalls
> Submission mistakes specific to THIS journal, evidenced by observed patterns:

1. **Insufficient ethical approval detail:** Authors omit specific approval number, committee name, or guideline reference. JEEE strictly enforces this for human subjects research; provide exact documentation.

2. **Overstated practical implications relative to findings:** ABS 1 editors show less tolerance for implications presented as more actionable than data support. Ensure implications are explicitly bounded to sample, context, and limitations.

3. **Excessive appendices:** While supplementary materials are encouraged, submitting 30+ pages of appendices (common in top-tier work) signals overscope for ABS 1. Prioritize the most essential robustness checks; defer minor sensitivity analyses.

4. **Under-reporting of missing data and handling:** JEEE articles consistently report listwise vs. pairwise deletion choice and missing data percentages in methods. Omitting this signals lower methodological transparency standards.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Canadian Journal of Administrative Sciences

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN
- **Publisher:** Wiley-Blackwell
- **Scope:** International quarterly fostering interdisciplinary and multidisciplinary dialogue. Publishes articles in all key disciplines of business (management, organizational behavior, public administration, marketing, finance, accounting, strategy, ethics/CSR) that advance theory and practice in administrative sciences. Publishes in English and French.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/page/journal/19364490/homepage/forauthors.html (paywalled; guidelines extracted from search results and journal metadata)
- **Field baseline:** mgmt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 5 (Good et al. 2025; David et al. 2025; Song et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025; Bujaki et al. 2024)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Standard quantitative/qualitative templates apply. Abstract → Introduction → [Theory/Methods/Findings] → Discussion → Conclusion. No deviations observed.

### Length
**ENFORCED CONSTRAINT - tighter than FIELD_SKILL.** Manuscript should not exceed **40 pages total** (including abstract, text, references, appendices, tables, figures), or alternatively **10,000 words maximum**. This is a strict page/word limit; FIELD_SKILL baseline (10,000–18,000 words for qualitative work) is more permissive. Authors should budget conservatively.

### Abstract
**ENFORCED CONSTRAINT - shorter than FIELD_SKILL.** Abstract must not exceed **120 words** (FIELD_SKILL baseline is 150–250 words). Unstructured or structured; both acceptable. Must be followed by **5–7 keywords** (FIELD_SKILL does not specify keyword count). No citations in abstract.

### Citations
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. APA in-text citations (Author, Year) required. Multiple citations ordered alphabetically. Et al. for 3+ authors from first citation. Reference list alphabetical, full APA format.

### Headings
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. APA-style hierarchical headings. No deviations observed in spot-check.

### Tables and Figures
At initial submission, figures may be embedded in manuscript or submitted separately. **ENFORCED CONSTRAINT for revision stage:** If manuscript reaches revision, all tables and figures must be submitted as separate files. This is more prescriptive than FIELD_SKILL.

### Formatting
**ENFORCED CONSTRAINT.** Manuscript must follow APA format, 12-point Times New Roman font, double-spaced (manuscript, reference list, and abstract). Use **endnotes instead of footnotes** (FIELD_SKILL does not specify this distinction). Free-format submission acceptable for initial review.

### Explicit Prohibitions
ORCID iD required at submission. No other explicit prohibitions found in accessible guidelines.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Abstract Length
**RELAXATION.** FIELD_SKILL specifies 150–250 words; this journal caps abstracts at 120 words. This is a tighter constraint, not a relaxation, but is journal-specific and must be observed.

### Keyword Specification
**ENFORCED (PRACTICE ONLY).** FIELD_SKILL does not specify keywords or their count. CJAS requires 5–7 keywords following the abstract. Observed in all spot-checked articles.

### Manuscript Length
**RELAXATION.** FIELD_SKILL indicates articles typically run 10,000–18,000 words (longer for reviews); CJAS enforces a 40-page or 10,000-word ceiling. This is a lower-tier journal constraint. Authors must condense relative to AMJ/ASQ expectations.

### Endnotes vs. Footnotes
**ENFORCED.** Guidelines specify endnotes instead of footnotes. FIELD_SKILL does not distinguish. Observed in article structure documentation.

### Figure/Table Submission Rules
**GUIDELINE ONLY.** Guidelines explicitly require separate figure/table files at revision stage, more prescriptive than FIELD_SKILL general practice.

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Voice and Person:** FIELD DEFAULT (first-person "we" standard for co-authored work; active voice preferred; no journal-specific deviation)
- **Macro Structure:** FIELD DEFAULT (CARS introduction, Theory/Methods/Findings/Discussion structure standard; no deviation)
- **Hypotheses vs. Propositions:** FIELD DEFAULT (Hypotheses for quantitative work, propositions for theoretical; no deviation)
- **Citation Density:** FIELD DEFAULT (Introduction and Theory citation-dense; Results/Findings cite minimally; Discussion engages citations; standard management pattern)
- **Hedging:** FIELD DEFAULT (Moderate hedging; "we find," "we argue," "suggest" used appropriately; no deviation)

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Exceeding abstract word limit.** 120 words is strict; authors often draft 180–200 and must severely compress. Prioritize research question and main finding; defer methods detail to introduction.
2. **Footnotes instead of endnotes.** Guidelines explicitly forbid footnotes. Use endnotes only; some authors miss this formatting detail and incur revision requests.
3. **Figures embedded in text at revision.** Authors sometimes fail to extract figures into separate files at revision stage, delaying publication. Plan for this early.
4. **Omitting keywords.** While 5–7 keywords are required, some authors forget or provide fewer than 5, triggering revision requests. Always supply exactly 5–7.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

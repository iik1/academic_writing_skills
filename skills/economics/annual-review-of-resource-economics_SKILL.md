# Journal Writing Skill: Annual Review of Resource Economics

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Annual Reviews Inc.
- **Scope:** Authoritative critical reviews of significant research developments in resource economics, covering agricultural economics, environmental economics, renewable resources, and exhaustible resources.
- **Guidelines URL:** Not publicly accessible (authors invited by committee only)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 8 (titles and abstracts from Volume 16–17, 2024–2025; full-text access paywalled; abstract and metadata publicly available)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Submission Model
**CRITICAL:** Unsolicited manuscripts are not accepted. The editorial committee determines topics, solicits reviews from qualified authors by invitation only. Authors cannot propose unsolicited papers for this journal. (ENFORCED)

### Structure
Review articles typically follow Introduction → substantive topic-specific sections → Conclusion. Section numbering and naming vary by topic; no rigid formula. Standalone Related Literature sections within the introduction are common. (ENFORCED from observed article structure)

### Length
Manuscripts typically span 20–40+ pages (printed format). Specific word limits not publicly stated; production editor communicates word count targets upon commission. (PRACTICE ONLY)

### Abstract
- **Length:** 150 words maximum. (ENFORCED)
- **Format:** Unstructured prose (no labeled subsections required). (ENFORCED)
- **Required elements:** Summary of question, main findings, and key implications. (PRACTICE ONLY)

### Keywords
Maximum 6 keywords required. (ENFORCED)

### Optional Mandatory Elements
Authors must include the following unless waived by production editor:

- **Summary Points:** Up to 8 central points in complete sentences, positioned above Literature Cited. (ENFORCED)
- **Future Issues:** Up to 8 forward-looking research directions in complete sentences, positioned above Literature Cited. (ENFORCED)
- **Reference Annotations:** Brief explanations of key citations, 15 words maximum per annotation. (ENFORCED)
- **Related Resources:** Optional list of up to 10 references not cited in Literature Cited, for reader interest. (OPTIONAL)
- **Sidebars:** Optional 50–200 word adjacent topics, clearly labeled and separated from main text. (OPTIONAL)

### Citations
- **Format:** Author-year, parenthetical in-text (e.g., "Smith 2020" or "Smith & Jones 2019"); author-et-al. for three or more authors. (ENFORCED)
- **Section title:** "Literature Cited" (not "References"). (ENFORCED)
- **Style:** Alphabetical by author, follows Harvard author-year conventions. Examples and full format details in Annual Reviews author handbook. (ENFORCED)

### Headings
No mandatory heading hierarchy specified. Authors have flexibility in section naming and numbering (Roman numerals, Arabic numerals, or no numerals). Match existing journal issues for consistency. (FIELD DEFAULT)

### Tables and Figures
Standard academic conventions. Must obtain permission to reproduce figures from other sources; source attribution required in caption. Submitted as separate files with clear captions. (FIELD DEFAULT with ENFORCED attribution requirement)

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Unsolicited submissions will not be reviewed.
- No primary research articles; this is a review journal only.
- No new data collection or original empirical studies (unless explicitly part of commissioned review).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Submission and Invitation Model
**ENFORCEMENT:** This journal is invitation-only. FIELD_SKILL assumes open-submission journals. The journal's editorial committee selects all topics and authors. A researcher targeting this journal cannot submit independently; they must wait for or cultivate an invitation. This is a structural delta, not a writing style delta, but it is critical: no amount of stylistic mastery will overcome unsolicited status. (ENFORCED structural constraint)

### Abstract Length
FIELD_SKILL does not specify abstract length limits. Annual Review of Resource Economics enforces a 150-word maximum. This is significantly shorter than many ABS 4*/4 journals and reflects the review article format (wide scope, brief synopsis). (ENFORCED, delta from field baseline)

### Mandatory Supplementary Elements
FIELD_SKILL does not mention Summary Points, Future Issues, Reference Annotations, or Sidebar formats. These are specific to Annual Reviews and mandatory (or near-mandatory) components of the manuscript. (ENFORCED, journal-specific additions)

### Citation Density by Section
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (high in introduction, medium in methods/analysis, lower in conclusion). No observed deviation. (FIELD DEFAULT)

### Voice and Register
Articles observed are substantively pitched to a professional resource-economics audience, not to broad academic economics. Formality remains high per FIELD_SKILL, but language is somewhat less technical than cutting-edge economic theory journals. This is consistent with ABS 2 status: rigorous but accessible to practitioners and policy makers. (RELAXATION from top-tier intensity, but not coded as delta because FIELD_SKILL baseline is ABS 4*/4, not ABS 2)

### Hedging and Causal Claims
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (clear claims preferred, hedging only where identification/scope warrant it). No observed deviation. (FIELD DEFAULT)

### Statistical Reporting
Not applicable; review articles synthesize and interpret prior work, not conduct original statistical estimation. Where prior results are cited, quantitative specificity expected per FIELD_SKILL. (FIELD DEFAULT)

### Structural Deviations
No structural deviations beyond the optional elements listed in Hard Constraints. Conclusion length follows FIELD_SKILL norm (shorter than introduction). (FIELD DEFAULT)

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Submitting unsolicited:** This journal will not accept or review unsolicited proposals. Authors must be invited by the editorial committee. Do not waste effort unless commissioned.
2. **Exceeding abstract length:** 150 words is a hard ceiling. Verbose abstracts that run to 250+ words (common in economic journals) will require significant trimming.
3. **Omitting Summary Points and Future Issues:** These lists are near-mandatory and must be included unless the production editor explicitly waives them. Do not treat them as optional.
4. **Using "References" instead of "Literature Cited":** The section must be titled "Literature Cited" per Annual Reviews conventions.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

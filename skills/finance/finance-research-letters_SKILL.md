# Journal Writing Skill: Finance Research Letters

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** FINANCE
- **Publisher:** Elsevier
- **Scope:** A "letters" journal (est. 2004, bimonthly) publishing short papers on any area of finance, emphasizing rapid publication of new, preliminary, or experimental results. Explicitly welcomes replication studies, transnational applicability checks, and papers challenging existing methodology; single-country replications of well-established results are explicitly out of scope.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/finance-research-letters/publish/guide-for-authors
- **Field baseline:** finance-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 5 abstracts/front-matter (RePEc/IDEAS abstract pages; full text paywalled): "Financial literacy, risk attitude, and consumer retirement planning" (v84, 2025); "Financial literacy of ChatGPT: Evidence through financial news" (v78, 2025); "Corporate site visits and the speed of leverage adjustment" (v73, 2025); "Policy coordination mechanism: Rural financial inclusion and public governance in developing countries" (v85, 2025); "Does the market value CEO climate activism?" (v87, 2026). Sample size limited by full-text paywall; abstracts and JEL/keyword front matter used to confirm topical/register fit, per the assignment's good-faith allowance for ABS 2 journals.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
No mandated section list beyond standard practice; papers are expected to be concise and focused on a single novel result rather than a full canonical multi-section empirical paper. Article highlights (a short bullet-point list of novel results/methods) are REQUIRED at submission, uploaded as a separate file with "highlights" in the filename — this has no equivalent in FIELD_SKILL.

### Length
**Manuscripts must be under 2,500 words** (main text only: body + in-text citations + footnotes; excludes abstract, references, tables, figures, captions/notes, acknowledgements, declarations, appendices/supplementary material). This is a defining constraint of the journal and far shorter than the canonical ABS 4*/4 finance article length assumed in FIELD_SKILL.

### Abstract
Single-paragraph abstract (no fixed word count found in guidelines, but observed exemplars are brief, 1 focused paragraph). Keywords and JEL codes reported as separate fields, consistent with FIELD_SKILL.

### Citations
Author-date parenthetical style — consistent with FIELD_SKILL, no delta.

### Headings
Numbered Arabic headings — consistent with FIELD_SKILL, no delta.

### Tables and Figures
No journal-specific deviation found beyond standard Elsevier formatting; not distinguishable from FIELD_SKILL at this access level.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Generative AI use in manuscript preparation must be disclosed at submission.
- Generative AI/AI-assisted tools are NOT permitted for producing artwork (including graphical abstracts).
- All submissions undergo desk review by one of the Editors-in-Chief before being sent to referees — a faster/harsher initial gate than the standard single-blind peer review process assumed generically in FIELD_SKILL.

### Structural Deviations
**ENFORCED.** The 2,500-word cap compresses the canonical FIELD_SKILL structure (Introduction / Data / Results / Mechanisms / Conclusion / Appendix) into a much shorter form. Spot-checked abstracts describe single, narrowly scoped findings (e.g., one event study, one survey-based test) rather than the multi-contribution, multi-robustness-section papers typical of ABS 4*/4 finance journals. Authors should expect to compress or omit dedicated Mechanisms sections and push most robustness material to supplementary material, since it falls outside the word count.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
**ENFORCED.** Mandatory "Highlights" bullet file at submission — not part of FIELD_SKILL and not a universal finance-journal requirement at the ABS 4*/4 tier.
**ENFORCED.** Explicit editorial preference for papers on replicability, transnational applicability, or methodological contingency of prior results — a scope signal, not a prose-style delta, but relevant when framing a paper's contribution for this journal (the "we are the first to show" framing from FIELD_SKILL can be adapted to "we test the replicability/generalizability of X" framing where applicable).

## Common Pitfalls
- Drafting a full-length ABS 4*/4-style paper and then cutting it down: the 2,500-word main-text cap should shape the outline from the start, not be applied as a late edit.
- Omitting the Highlights file — this is a submission requirement, not optional supplementary material.
- Using AI-generated images/graphical abstracts, which is explicitly disallowed even though AI use in text preparation is permitted with disclosure.
- Framing a paper as a plain single-country replication of an established result, which the journal states is generally out of scope; frame replications as cross-country, methodological, or contingency tests instead.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

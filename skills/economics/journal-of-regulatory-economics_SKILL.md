# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Regulatory Economics

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Springer Nature
- **Scope:** Forum for rigorous analysis of regulatory theories and institutions, encompassing both theoretical and applied work. Publishes empirical, experimental, and policy-relevant research in regulation and economic governance.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://link.springer.com/journal/11149/submission-guidelines
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 10 articles from 2025–2026 issues (full abstracts and metadata accessed; full texts paywalled)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows standard empirical and theory paper structures. No journal-mandated section order specified in publicly available guidelines; authors follow disciplinary norms (Introduction → Methods/Model → Results → Conclusion is standard).

### Length
**No word limit.** However, **16 typeset pages are included in publication costs**. Papers exceeding 16 pages incur a charge of **$100 per typeset page** (to the nearest quarter page) beyond the baseline. This creates a mild financial disincentive to lengthy manuscripts but does not forbid them.

### Abstract
**Mandatory structured abstract: 150–250 words.** Abstract should not contain undefined abbreviations or unspecified references.

**Keywords:** 4–6 keywords required for indexing.

### Citations
**Author-year format** (standard ECON field norm). Parenthetical or in-text citations acceptable. No deviations from FIELD_SKILL observed.

### Headings
No explicit naming convention mandated in public guidelines. Standard numbered or unnumbered headings observed in recent articles, aligned with ECON field practice.

### Tables and Figures
No explicit formatting rules in public guidelines. Standard economic table and figure conventions observed (notes below, sources cited, sample sizes and standard errors reported).

### Data Availability
Authors must include a **Data Availability Statement** explaining how to access data supporting the results, including links/citations to publicly archived datasets analyzed or generated during the study.

### Author Contributions and Competing Interests
**Author Contribution information and Competing Interest information must be provided at submission via the submission interface.** Only information submitted via the interface will appear in the final published version. This is a journal-specific administrative requirement not tied to writing style but critical for submission.

### File Format
Acceptable formats: **.docx or LaTeX.** All editable source files must be submitted; failure to do so results in desk rejection.

### Explicit Prohibitions
None identified in public guidelines. Standard scholarly standards apply (originality, no plagiarism, proper attribution).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Page Limit and Cost Disincentive
**Delta: RELAXATION.** Unlike many ABS 4*/4 journals, there is **no hard page or word limit**. However, the financial structure (16-page baseline, $100/page thereafter) creates a softer constraint. This permits longer manuscripts than typical ABS 4 journals (which often enforce strict 35-40 page limits) but may subtly discourage extremely lengthy papers.

**Classification:** ENFORCED (stated in guidelines). Authors may write longer papers but face publication costs above the baseline.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- **Voice and Person:** FIELD DEFAULT. First-person plural ("we") dominates in recent articles; active voice strongly preferred.
- **Sentence and Paragraph Style:** FIELD DEFAULT. Medium-to-long sentences; high paragraph density (4–10 sentences); technical vocabulary density high.
- **Argumentation:** FIELD DEFAULT. Introduction announces results quantitatively; explicit contribution positioning; clear identification of gaps; conclusion shorter than introduction.
- **Methods Conventions:** FIELD DEFAULT. Mixed tense (past for procedures, present for implications); sufficient detail for replication without step-by-step recipe; explicit identification strategy for empirical papers.
- **Results and Reporting:** FIELD DEFAULT. Results integrated with interpretation; tables primary vehicle; quantitative specificity expected; null results published when well-powered and important.
- **Citation Density:** FIELD DEFAULT. High in introduction, medium in methods, low-to-medium in results, low in conclusion. Author-year parenthetical format.
- **Hedging:** FIELD DEFAULT. Low overall hedging; clear claims preferred; hedging used only for external validity, causal interpretation limits, data limitations, and mechanism vs. reduced-form distinctions.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Exceeding 16 pages without budget consideration:** Authors frequently underestimate how many pages their manuscript will occupy after typesetting. Verify final page count before submission to avoid unexpected charges ($100/page).
2. **Incomplete Data Availability Statement:** Reviewers and editors check whether public/open-access data links are active and accurate. Broken links delay review.
3. **Undefined abbreviations in abstract:** Abstracts must be self-contained; any acronym or technical term used must be defined on first use within the abstract itself.
4. **Omitting author contributions and competing interests at submission:** The interface submission of these elements is mandatory for peer review; missing them can lead to desk rejection before review.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Psychological Research

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** PSYCH (GENERAL)
- **Publisher:** Springer Nature
- **Scope:** An international journal of perception, attention, memory, and
  action; publishes work contributing to a basic understanding of human
  cognition and behavior, on firm experimental grounds, without commitment
  to a particular school of thought.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://link.springer.com/journal/426/submission-guidelines (accessible only via search-engine-cached snippets; direct fetch redirects to a Springer login wall)
- **Field baseline:** psychology-general-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 7 (via abstracts/full text where accessible): "The perils of the first try: experimental evidence for visuomotor calibration in darts and hammering" (Smith, Furley, Wunderlich & Heuer, 2025, DOI 10.1007/s00426-025-02161-w); "Cognitive and affective Theory of Mind in adolescence" (2019, DOI 10.1007/s00426-019-01263-6); "A serial-position curve in high-performance darts" (2019, DOI 10.1007/s00426-019-01205-2); "Selective attention to stimulus representations in perception and memory" (2020, DOI 10.1007/s00426-020-01469-z); "Updates from the journal" editorial (2025, DOI 10.1007/s00426-025-02165-6); "Current developments of Psychological Research and the use of AI" editorial (2026, DOI 10.1007/s00426-026-02246-0); "Start of a new editor-in-chief" editorial (2025).

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Standard IMRaD as in FIELD_SKILL, but with two journal-specific additions:
a mandatory **Public Significance Statement** (see Abstract below) and,
since 2025/2026 editorial changes, a requirement to disclose AI use either
within the Methods section or in a dedicated AI-use subsection, with
examples supplied as an appendix. New article types beyond standard
Research Article and Review: **Forgotten Gem** (a short piece resurfacing
a neglected but historically important paper and its implications for
current research), **Registered Report**, and **Registered Replication**
(Stage 1 protocol peer-reviewed and provisionally accepted before data
collection, then registered on a repository such as OSF).

### Length
Not specified in accessible guidelines beyond Springer's general practice; no journal-specific limit found.

### Abstract
Unstructured, single-paragraph abstract, 150–250 words (Springer default,
consistent with FIELD_SKILL). In addition, a **Public Significance
Statement** is required as a separate, short element: 2–3 sentences
(60–80 words) stating the real-world relevance of the study in language
comprehensible to lay readers, not just experts.

### Citations
Author–year in-text citation (APA style), following APA 7 rules for
author lists in the reference list (list all authors up to 20; for
larger groups, first 19 names followed by an ellipsis and the final
author). Reference list includes only published or in-press works;
personal communications and unpublished work are cited in text only, not
in the reference list.

### Headings
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.

### Tables and Figures
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Simultaneous submission to more than one journal is prohibited. AI tools
may assist but a human author must take responsibility for the final
text; undisclosed substantive AI use (beyond basic language editing) is
treated as a transparency/integrity violation.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
Ethics/compliance disclosure ("Compliance with Ethical Standards": IRB
approval per Declaration of Helsinki, informed consent, competing
interests) is placed as a separate, explicitly labeled section
immediately before the reference list, rather than embedded in Method —
ENFORCED (stated in guidelines, confirmed in both spot-checked full
texts).

Mandatory a priori power analysis for all empirical submissions, and new
mandatory AI-use disclosure subsection when AI tools were used beyond
copyediting — ENFORCED, per 2025/2026 editorial policy statements; too
recent to confirm prevalence across the broader article sample, but
stated unambiguously as a submission requirement going forward.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Public Significance Statement as a required, separately labeled
manuscript element (not merely encouraged) — ENFORCED, confirmed present
in spot-checked recent full text. This is a journal-specific
operationalization not addressed by FIELD_SKILL.

Distinctive minor article type, "Forgotten Gem," has no analogue in
FIELD_SKILL's article-type coverage — GUIDELINE ONLY / low-volume format;
mention only if the user is targeting this specific article type.

## Common Pitfalls
- Omitting the Public Significance Statement or writing it at the same
  register as the scientific abstract — it must be lay-comprehensible and
  kept to 2–3 sentences (60–80 words).
- Failing to disclose AI-assisted writing/analysis beyond simple language
  editing; the journal now requires this in Methods or a dedicated
  subsection, with examples as an appendix.
- Placing the ethics/compliance statement inside Method rather than as a
  standalone "Compliance with Ethical Standards" section directly before
  the reference list.
- Submitting a confirmatory empirical study without an a priori power
  analysis, now treated as a baseline expectation rather than best practice.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

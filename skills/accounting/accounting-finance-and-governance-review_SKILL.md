# Journal Writing Skill: Accounting, Finance & Governance Review

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ACCOUNT
- **Publisher:** Irish Accounting and Finance Association (IAFA)
- **Scope:** Open-access journal publishing empirical research, analytical papers, systematic reviews, pedagogical work, and critical essays across accounting, finance, governance, auditing, taxation, sustainability, and social/environmental accounting.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://afgr.scholasticahq.com/for-authors
- **Field baseline:** account-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 12 (across 2023–2026; methodologies: systematic reviews, scoping reviews, qualitative participatory research, empirical archival; representative sample given ABS 1 tier and thin institutional access)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
- Manuscript must include a **separate cover page** with title, author(s), affiliation(s), and **one address for correspondence (including proofs)**.
- Section structure follows standard academic organization (Introduction, Literature/Theory/Hypothesis, Methods/Design, Results/Findings, Discussion/Conclusion, References). Journals welcome diverse methodological approaches, including systematic reviews, qualitative case studies, and analytical essays alongside empirical archival work.

### Length
- **Word limit:** Manuscripts should not normally exceed **8,000 words**. This is a firm constraint (ENFORCED).

### Abstract
- **Word limit:** Not more than **100 words** (ENFORCED).
- Format: Unstructured narrative (not labeled subsections per sample articles).
- Placement: At the beginning of the paper, before introduction.

### Keywords
- **Number:** Up to **6 keywords** (ENFORCED).
- Placement: Immediately following the abstract.

### Citations
- **Style:** Author-date in-text citations (e.g., "Black (1972)" or "(Brown, 1972)").
- **Format:** Multiple citations separated by semicolons in alphabetical/chronological order.
- **Journal abbreviations in references:** Follow standard academic convention (title case or full title per journal convention).

### Headings
- Practice varies by article type. Empirical/archival papers use numbered sections (1. Introduction, 2. Data, 3. Results, etc.); systematic reviews and qualitative papers use descriptive headings without rigid numbering.
- No explicit ALL-CAPS requirement observed (unlike some AAA journals). Title case is standard.

### Tables and Figures
- Placed after references or end of manuscript (per journal standard).
- Tables should include panel labels and notes with variable definitions.
- Figures should include captions.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Papers must be submitted via Scholastica platform (https://afgr.scholasticahq.com/for-authors).
- Double-blind peer review process; all submissions passing editorial scrutiny undergo formal review.
- Authors must understand that if accepted, the paper will be published as open-access under Creative Commons CC-BY-NC-4.0.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Methodological Scope (PRACTICE ONLY / RELAXATION)
FIELD_SKILL derives entirely from ABS 4*/4 quantitative archival and experimental traditions. AFGR, as an ABS 1 journal, explicitly welcomes a broader range: empirical research, analytical papers, systematic literature reviews, qualitative case studies, pedagogical essays, and critical essays. Spot-check of 12 recent articles (2023–2026) shows that **7 are systematic or scoping reviews**, 2 are qualitative participatory studies, 1 is analytical, and 2 are empirical archival/archival analysis. This represents a significant relaxation of the quantitative-dominant baseline. Authors should note that **systematic reviews and qualitative studies are strongly represented and welcome**, differing from the ABS 4*/4 baseline's emphasis on archival/experimental work.

### Citation Density in Reviews and Qualitative Work (PRACTICE ONLY)
While FIELD_SKILL specifies citation density by section for archival/experimental papers (high in intro/theory, low in results), systematic reviews and qualitative papers in AFGR show high citation density throughout, as these methodologies are inherently literature-integrated. Empirical archival papers follow FIELD_SKILL norms; reviews do not.

### Hedging in Qualitative and Review Work (PRACTICE ONLY)
FIELD_SKILL specifies medium hedging calibrated to identification strength. Qualitative and review papers in AFGR use more tentative language throughout (e.g., "suggest," "may indicate," "appears to") because systematic reviews explicitly frame findings as patterns across studies, not definitive claims. Empirical archival papers follow FIELD_SKILL norms.

### Discussion and Conclusion (PRACTICE ONLY)
Empirical papers merge Discussion and Conclusion (consistent with FIELD_SKILL). Systematic reviews and qualitative papers more often include separate sections: Conclusion, Limitations, and Future Research Directions as standalone sections, reflecting review and qualitative convention. This is a relaxation, not a reversal.

### Research Gaps and Limitations (PRACTICE ONLY)
AFGR papers, particularly reviews and qualitative work, place explicit emphasis on research gaps and limitations sections. While FIELD_SKILL mentions limitations as standard in conclusions, AFGR articles often dedicate full sections (e.g., "Research Gaps," "Future Research Directions") to gaps, even in brief papers. This is more explicit than field baseline.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- **Voice and person:** We-dominant first-person plural; active voice predominant (FIELD DEFAULT).
- **Formality and register:** High formality throughout; clinical/precise register in empirical work (FIELD DEFAULT).
- **Sentence length and paragraph density:** Medium to long sentences with embedded clauses; dense paragraphs in introduction and theory (FIELD DEFAULT).
- **Introduction structure:** Follows CARS-like pattern (establish significance, state gap, describe study, preview results, state contributions) (FIELD DEFAULT).
- **Contribution positioning:** Explicit and required (FIELD DEFAULT).
- **Confidence calibration:** Well-identified results warrant direct claims; mechanistic interpretations use softened language (FIELD DEFAULT).
- **Causal language in archival work:** Reserved for exogenously identified variation (FIELD DEFAULT).
- **Methods section conventions:** Past tense, high procedural detail, identification strategy central (FIELD DEFAULT).
- **Results reporting:** Tables are primary vehicle; prose interprets; organized by hypothesis/question (FIELD DEFAULT for empirical work).
- **Statistical reporting:** Coefficient estimates and significance levels reported precisely; economic significance increasingly expected (FIELD DEFAULT).
- **Reference formatting:** Author-date in-text citations with full reference list (FIELD DEFAULT).

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Exceeding word limit:** Manuscripts over 8,000 words will be rejected at desk review. Plan content carefully; the journal enforces this as a hard constraint. For longer work, consider splitting into multiple papers or adapting to the limit.
2. **Missing cover page:** A separate cover page with author(s), affiliation(s), and correspondence address is mandatory. Omission may result in desk rejection.
3. **Unstructured abstract over 100 words:** While other field journals may accept longer abstracts, AFGR strictly enforces the 100-word limit. Edit ruthlessly; every word counts.
4. **Misalignment of methodological expectations:** As an ABS 1 journal, AFGR welcomes reviews, qualitative, and pedagogical work. Do not assume that only archival empirical work fits. However, ensure your methodology section is transparent and rigorous regardless of method.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

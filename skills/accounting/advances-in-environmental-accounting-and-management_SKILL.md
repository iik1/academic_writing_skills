# Journal Writing Skill: Advances in Environmental Accounting and Management

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ACCOUNT
- **Publisher:** Emerald Group Publishing Limited
- **Format:** Edited book series (not a journal)
- **Scope:** Environmental accounting, sustainability reporting, climate risk disclosure, corporate environmental governance, and accounting for environmental impacts. Multi-method scholarship (empirical, qualitative, case study, meta-analytic).
- **Guidelines URL:** Emerald book chapter guidelines; specific call-for-chapters guidance from editor (Ven Tauringana)
- **Field baseline:** account-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 4 empirical chapters from volumes 11-12 (2023-2024); titles: "Board Interlocks and Carbon Emissions Performance" (Abang'a & Simbi, 2023), "Avenues for Systems Thinking Towards Exploring the Ethics of Sustainability Within Supply Chains" (Mackay, 2024), "Greenhouse Gas Emissions Research in Top-Ranked Journals" (Michael et al., 2023); 1 meta-analysis

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
**Book chapter format (NOT journal article).** Standard sections for empirical chapters: Introduction, Hypothesis Development/Literature Review, Research Design/Methodology, Results/Findings, Discussion and Conclusion, References. Qualitative, case study, and critical chapters adapt structure accordingly. No manuscript-style cover pages or running heads required for chapter submission (publisher handles formatting).

### Length
**7,000–10,000 words inclusive of abstract, text, and references.** This is a RELAXATION from the longer FIELD_SKILL archival papers (which commonly exceed 10,000 words); editorial discretion allowed for complex empirical studies or integrative reviews, but chapters should be concise.

### Abstract
**Maximum 250 words; unstructured.** Include keywords naturally within the abstract text where possible for discoverability. Separate keywords line follows.

### Keywords
**6 keywords maximum.** List separately after abstract. Encapsulate principal topics of the chapter.

### Citations
**Author-date format (e.g., Smith 2023; Jones & Lee 2022).** Emerald's standard is APA 7th Edition, though field convention (ACCOUNT) is acceptable if consistent. Multiple citations separated by semicolons. This aligns with FIELD_SKILL norm.

### Headings
**No prescribed numbering system in guidelines.** Observed practice in chapters: Arabic numerals with title case (1. Introduction, 2. Research Design) or unnumbered section headings (Introduction, Methodology). Follow journal house style or use consistent convention throughout.

### Tables and Figures
**All figures, charts, tables must be submitted electronically and of clear quality.** Number consecutively (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Table 1, Table 2). Appendix items labeled A1, A2, A3, etc. If two appendices, name as Appendix A and Appendix B. Tables carry notes clarifying definitions and statistical choices (clustering, fixed effects, specifications). Matches FIELD_SKILL norm.

### Explicit Prohibitions
**Clear permission required for any non-original content** (legal requirement). AI use for copy-editing (grammar/clarity) is permitted; substantive AI-generated content should be disclosed. No other explicit prohibitions noted in guidelines.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Length and Scope Relaxation
**RELAXATION:** FIELD_SKILL expects longer archival papers (15,000–30,000 words typical). Advances in Environmental Accounting and Management enforces a tighter 7,000–10,000-word budget per chapter. Authors must condense hypothesis development, results, and robustness discussion; this may limit robustness checks or mechanism tests, or push them to appendices more aggressively than field norm.

### Abstract Format
**RELAXATION:** FIELD_SKILL describes "structured" abstracts (research question, method, findings, contribution). Advances in Environmental Accounting and Management permits unstructured abstracts up to 250 words. Spot-checked chapters show variation: some follow research question → method → key findings → contribution pattern; others are more discursive. Neither format is mandated.

### Chapter Heterogeneity
**PRACTICE:** FIELD_SKILL assumes quantitative archival/experimental canonical structure. Advances in Environmental Accounting and Management invites diverse methods (qualitative case studies, critical essays, meta-analyses, policy analysis). Observed chapters depart from the hypothesis-testing structure when appropriate (e.g., systems-thinking and ethics chapter takes a conceptual argument approach; meta-analysis chapter follows literature → coding → synthesis structure). This is encouraged in the call for chapters, which explicitly invites "conceptual and critical analyses" and "case studies."

### Scope and Audience
**PRACTICE:** FIELD_SKILL is centered on accounting specialist readership (archival, experimental, institutional focus). Advances in Environmental Accounting and Management addresses a broader audience: accounting practitioners, corporate environmental managers, policy makers, and sustainability specialists. Chapters are more accessible in register and may emphasize practical implications and stakeholder engagement more explicitly than FIELD_SKILL norm.

## Common Pitfalls
> Submission mistakes specific to THIS journal, evidenced in research and guidelines.

1. **Exceeding length.** The 7,000–10,000-word budget is firm. Papers that overrun (common in ACCOUNT field) must be condensed; authors often over-estimate available space for hypothesis development and results narrative relative to field norms. Trim robustness checks, subsection detail, and extended mechanism tests to fit.

2. **Unstructured abstract without keywords integration.** While unstructured abstracts are permitted, failing to embed primary keywords naturally within the abstract text reduces online discoverability in Emerald's platform. Chapter indexing is central to the series' value proposition; keywords matter.

3. **Inconsistent section numbering or heading format across chapters in the same volume.** While individual author choice is honored, Emerald's production team standardizes on submission. Chapters within a volume should appear uniform; inconsistent numbering (Roman vs. Arabic, numbered vs. unnumbered) may trigger copy-editing queries or revisions.

4. **Underestimating multi-method scope.** Authors trained in FIELD_SKILL's quantitative archival tradition may undervalue or underdevelop qualitative, case-study, or critical frameworks. Advances in Environmental Accounting and Management actively seeks diverse methodologies. If submitting qualitative work, signal this clearly in the call response; reviewers and editors expect method-appropriate structure and argumentation, not a forced fit to the hypothesis-testing template.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

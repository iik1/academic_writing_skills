# Journal Writing Skill: Team Performance Management

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** HRM&EMP
- **Publisher:** Emerald Publishing Limited
- **Scope:** Peer-reviewed international journal bridging research and practice on group and team phenomena across different levels and contexts. Publishes empirical investigations (quantitative and qualitative), theory development, and systematic literature reviews on team effectiveness, team processes, and team management in organizational settings.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.emeraldgrouppublishing.com/journal/tpm (journal page); formal author guidelines were not publicly accessible during search
- **Field baseline:** hrm-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 9 articles sampled via abstracts and metadata from Vol. 31 Issues 5–8 (2025): esports teams systematic review (Raetze et al.), agile team model qualitative study (Bastiaansen & Van Dun), team diversity and cohesion study (Lee et al.), esports organizational teams (McCauley), sustainability in esports (Kim), League of Legends careers (Kang), and others.
- **Status note:** Journal merged into IJPPM end of 2025; no longer accepting submissions as of research date.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Manuscript Types and Length
Research manuscripts: **4000–7000 words** (minimum 4000, maximum 7000), including abstract, keywords, and references. Content must rigorously describe empirical investigations, theory development, or qualitative research with clear contribution to understanding team phenomena.

Research notes: **up to 3000 words**. Short manuscripts accurately describing a robust empirical study, with short theoretical background introduction, emphasis on careful description of empirical design, materials, and procedures, and succinct results for transparency and replicability.

Systematic literature reviews: Accepted as full research manuscripts (4000–7000 words).

### Abstract
Structured abstract, 250 words maximum. Must include standard labels/fields (e.g., Purpose, Design/methodology, Findings, Originality/Value, or similar structured headings). Originality/Value statement is mandatory. No citations in abstract.

### Keywords
Provide **up to 6 keywords** encapsulating principal topics of the paper.

### Citation Style
**Emerald Harvard** referencing system required. For three or more authors, use "et al." (italicized). In-text citations formatted as (Author et al., Year). Reference list formatted per Harvard conventions. See https://www.emeraldgrouppublishing.com/how-to/authoring-editing-reviewing/use-harvard-reference-system for full Harvard style guidance from Emerald.

### Headings and Structure
Empirical research typically follows IMRaD-with-theory structure (consistent with field baseline). Research notes emphasize methods and results more than discussion. Section headings should be numbered or styled consistently. Theoretical framework, introduction, methods/design, results/findings, and discussion sections are standard.

### Tables and Figures
Follow Emerald conventions: tables and figures must be numbered sequentially, titled descriptively, and placed near relevant text or in appendix if disruptive. No specific constraints identified; follow journal submission template if provided.

### Scope and Contribution
Explicitly relate manuscript content to journal's aims and scope (team-level and multi-level studies on team effectiveness, team processes, leadership, composition, emergent states). Studies at different levels of analysis are welcomed (individual, team, organizational, multi-level). Emphasis on bridging research and practice; practical implications for practitioners expected in discussion.

### Explicit Prohibitions
No citations in abstract. Research notes must not exceed 3000 words. Full manuscripts outside 4000–7000 word range may be desk-rejected.

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Sentence and paragraph style:** FIELD DEFAULT. Medium to long sentences with subordination are expected; paragraphs structured around claims.
- **Voice and person:** FIELD DEFAULT. Formal, impersonal register with first-person plural ("we") permitted in introduction, methods, and discussion sections.
- **Argumentation and CARS-like structure:** FIELD DEFAULT. Introductions establish importance, identify gaps, and state contributions.
- **Explicit contribution statements:** FIELD DEFAULT. Contributions framed in relation to theory and practice; "this study contributes by..." statements expected.
- **Methods reporting conventions:** FIELD DEFAULT. Detailed sample, measures, and estimation strategy expected in quantitative work. Qualitative papers report sampling, access strategy, analytic approach, and reflexivity.
- **Ethical approval statements:** FIELD DEFAULT. Required for primary data collection.
- **Results and findings narration:** FIELD DEFAULT. Quantitative results presented via tables with prose interpretation; qualitative findings thematically organized with illustrative quotations.
- **Discussion structure:** FIELD DEFAULT. Revisit contributions explicitly, distinguish theoretical from practical implications, close with limitations and future directions.
- **Hedging and causal language:** FIELD DEFAULT. Moderate to high hedging in discussion/conclusion; observational designs use cautious language ("associated with," "linked to," "suggests").
- **Null and unexpected findings:** FIELD DEFAULT. Reported straightforwardly without minimization.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Exceeding word limits.** Research manuscripts must not exceed 7000 words; research notes must not exceed 3000. Submissions outside these ranges risk desk rejection. Word count includes abstract and keywords but typically excludes reference list per Emerald conventions.

2. **Unstructured or unfocused abstract.** The abstract must follow a structured format with labeled sections (Purpose, Design/methodology/approach, Findings, Originality/Value, etc.) and include no citations. An unstructured or citation-heavy abstract will require revision.

3. **Weak articulation of practical implications.** This journal bridges research and practice; a discussion that focuses purely on theoretical contribution without addressing managerial, organizational, or HR practitioner implications will be viewed unfavorably. Include a distinct section or subsection on implications for practice.

4. **Insufficient methodological transparency in research notes.** Research notes prioritize clear description of empirical design, materials, and procedures for replicability. Condensing methods to save space defeats the purpose of this format and will lead to rejection or revision requests.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

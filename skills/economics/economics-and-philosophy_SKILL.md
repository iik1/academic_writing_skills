# Journal Writing Skill: Economics and Philosophy

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Cambridge University Press
- **Scope:** Publishes articles, book reviews, critical notices, discussion articles, and symposia at the intersection of economics and philosophy. Topics include decision theory, game theory, rationality, moral and political philosophy of economics, experimental work, and methodology/epistemology of economics.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/economics-and-philosophy/information/author-instructions
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 5 recent articles (2024-2026)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows standard academic sections. Common article types: Articles, Book Reviews, Critical Notices, Discussion Articles, Replies, Symposium Articles, Symposium Introductions. The journal expects clear section organization but does not mandate a specific section nomenclature.

### Length
**Maximum 12,000 words** (ENFORCED). The Editors prefer papers of fewer than 12,000 words and may return longer papers without review requesting resubmission in shorter form. This is significantly shorter than typical ABS 4*/4 economics papers (which often run 40–60+ pages). Budget accordingly.

### Abstract
Not explicitly detailed in publicly accessible guidelines. Based on spot-check, abstracts are unstructured, concise (100–150 words typical), and lead with the central claim or problem rather than background/motivation.

### Citations
Author-year format expected. Complete formatting details appear in the downloadable "Instructions for Contributors" PDF on the Cambridge page (not accessible without purchase/download).

### Headings
Not specified in publicly accessible guidelines. Spot-check articles use Arabic numerals (1, 2, 3) and descriptive section titles; subsections use decimal notation (2.1, 2.2).

### Tables and Figures
Not restricted. Spot-check articles frequently use categorical tables (no numerical data) and few figures. Figures are not standard unless necessary for illustration.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Unsolicited book reviews not considered (editor solicitation required).
- Papers building on previous author work must explicitly discuss and differentiate from prior arguments.
- Historical or text-interpretive work not published unless it makes a contribution to **current debates** about economics and philosophy.
- All submissions must anonymize author identity; exceptions allowed only with explicit editor consent.

### Review Process
**Double-blind peer review** (ENFORCED). Authors are known to Editors but not referees. Referees are known to Editors but not authors unless referee waives anonymity. Revision deadline: Eight months for first revision (papers not resubmitted by deadline considered withdrawn).

### Open Access
As of Volume 41, Issue 1 (January 2025), Economics and Philosophy is **Gold Open Access**. All accepted articles are published open access.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Methodology and Evidence Norms
**PRACTICE DEVIATION (MAJOR):** Unlike ABS 4*/4 economics, which privileges empirical data and econometric modeling, *Economics and Philosophy* at ABS 2 **accepts and publishes purely conceptual/philosophical work without quantitative evidence.** Thought experiments, logical analysis, and conceptual distinctions are treated as primary evidence. Examples from spot-check:
- "Desert or Dignity? Rethinking Injustice in Wages" uses garden-shop thought experiments and conceptual reductios, not wage data.
- "Legal Inflation and Defective Laws" develops categorical typologies with no numerical data.
- "Redefining Predistribution" is entirely normative philosophy with no empirical referents.

This is a relaxation of the FIELD_SKILL norm (which assumes empirical grounding or formal modeling). **Implication:** Papers addressing economics-adjacent topics (justice, distribution, rationality) may be publishable in *Economics and Philosophy* as pure philosophy even if they would not be publishable in an economics journal due to absence of data or econometric validation.

### Hedging and Epistemic Caution
**PRACTICE DEVIATION (MINOR):** Hedging intensity is **higher than FIELD_SKILL baseline** in philosophical/normative sections. First-person singular ("I argue," "I believe") is used strategically to signal personal scholarly judgment. Hedging clusters on novel positive proposals ("arguably," "it seems," "at least in part," "in some cases") while confidence remains on logical/critical work. FIELD_SKILL's preference for "clear claims" over hedged ones is relaxed here; measured epistemic caution is expected when proposing philosophical positions. Examples:
- "I will argue that..."; "It seems that..."; "arguably the better way..."—phrases absent from ABS 4* abstracts.

### Voice and Person
**PRACTICE DEVIATION (MODERATE):** Both first-person singular and plural are used, with **first-person singular ("I") more common in philosophy sections than in FIELD_SKILL.** This is explicitly used to claim intellectual ownership of argumentative positions. Mixed use is standard: "I will defend X, which we can apply to Y." FIELD_SKILL's near-universal first-person plural is relaxed; singular voice is acceptable and even preferred for novel philosophical claims. Examples:
- "I do not intend to wade into the details..."
- "I believe the better way of stating this..."
- Alternates with "we find that..." and "our intuitions suggest..."

### Citation Patterns
**PRACTICE DEVIATION (MAJOR):** Citation focus shifts from **empirical studies and datasets** (FIELD_SKILL baseline) to **conceptual genealogies and philosophical secondary literature.** Spot-check articles cite O'Neill, Murphy & Nagel, Rawls, Feinberg, Gaus, Sen as primary interlocutors rather than citing empirical results. Footnotes carry substantive theoretical discussion (15–62 footnotes per article) rather than supplementary detail. **Implications:**
- Citations per article: 50–65 items typical (higher density than some economics venues).
- Deep engagement with single contemporary scholars (e.g., O'Neill across sections) rather than broad literature survey.
- Minimal citation of recent empirical studies or datasets.
- Direct quotation from philosophical texts is extremely rare; paraphrase and attribution remain standard.

### Argument Development and Rhetorical Structure
**PRACTICE DEVIATION (MAJOR):** Arguments **do not follow the FIELD_SKILL introduction pattern** (motivating hook → research question → approach → results → contributions). Instead:
1. **Problem identification** through critique of existing frameworks (e.g., "Desert-based wage justice is logically incoherent because...").
2. **Conceptual reconstruction** via fine-grained distinctions (e.g., "procedural priority" vs. "temporal priority"; "dignity vs. status dignity").
3. **Systematic objection-response** addressing anticipated counterarguments (often in subsections 3.1–4.5).
4. **Philosophical defense** of the reconstructed position rather than empirical validation.

This mirrors analytic philosophy's vernacular. The "preview of main results" (FIELD_SKILL) is replaced by "thesis statement" or "central claim." No quantitative claim ("X increases Y by Z percent") is expected or present.

### Structural Deviations
**PRACTICE DEVIATION (MINOR):** While section headings are numbered and structured, **subsection granularity is higher** than typical ABS 4* papers. Spot-check articles use 2–3 levels of nested subsections (1, 2, 2.1, 2.1.1) to scaffold conceptual scaffolding, where ABS 4* might use fewer hierarchical breaks. This allows philosophical argument to be built incrementally.

### Normative vs. Positive Framing
**PRACTICE DEVIATION (MAJOR):** Papers explicitly engage with **normative claims ("should," "ought," "is just," "is fair")** rather than positive claims ("predicts," "explains," "is caused by"). FIELD_SKILL does not prohibit normativity, but ABS 4*/4 economics journals treat positive framing as default. Here, normativity is a primary mode. Examples:
- "Employment income is not the kind of thing that is deserved..."
- "Predistribution should be defined as ex ante distribution..."
- "Legal systems ought to repeal defective laws..."

The boundary between descriptive and prescriptive framing is blurred; this is acceptable in *Economics and Philosophy* at ABS 2.

### Quantitative and Statistical Reporting
**PRACTICE DEVIATION (MAJOR):** Papers show **no expectation for statistical reporting** (standard errors, confidence intervals, p-values, regression tables) even when empirical topics are addressed. Categorical tables with text summaries replace quantitative tables. FIELD_SKILL expects "statistical narration precision" and "economic significance alongside statistical significance"; *Economics and Philosophy* relaxes this entirely for philosophical work. Empirical studies in the journal (rare) follow economics norms; purely philosophical papers ignore empirical validation.

### Tone and Register
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (formal, technical, economical, no contractions). No deviation observed.

### Title Style
**OBSERVATION (MINOR):** Spot-check articles include colloquial or rhetorical questions as titles:
- "Can I get a little less life satisfaction, please?"
- "Desert or dignity?"
This is atypical of ABS 4*/4 economics (which favor declarative statements) but acceptable here.

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Sentence and paragraph style:** FIELD DEFAULT. Sentence length and paragraph density follow ECON norms (medium–long, 4–10 sentences per paragraph).
- **Passive voice avoidance:** FIELD DEFAULT. Active voice is strongly preferred where possible.
- **Formality and contractions:** FIELD DEFAULT. High formality; no contractions.
- **Acronyms and technical vocabulary:** FIELD DEFAULT. Discipline-specific terminology used without definition; readers assumed trained in economics and philosophy.
- **Conclusion structure:** FIELD DEFAULT. Conclusions restate findings, acknowledge limitations, suggest future directions.
- **Paraphrase over quotation:** FIELD DEFAULT. Direct quotation is extremely rare; paraphrase and attribution standard.
- **Handling disagreement:** FIELD DEFAULT. Disagreement is handled with precision and without hostility.
- **Related Literature section:** FIELD DEFAULT. Literature engagement is expected but may be distributed through introduction/body rather than as standalone section.

## Common Pitfalls (Journal-Specific)

1. **Over-empiricizing a philosophical claim:** Submitting a purely conceptual argument but attempting to validate it with econometric analysis or data. *Economics and Philosophy* values conceptual coherence; unnecessary empirical claims weaken philosophical papers.

2. **Insufficient engagement with interlocutors:** Failing to identify and engage deeply with key prior work on your topic. Spot-check articles address O'Neill, Murphy & Nagel, or similar figures across multiple sections rather than citing them once. Build dialogue, not just citations.

3. **Vague normative framing:** Using "should" and "ought" without carefully reconstructing the underlying principles (justice, fairness, desert, rights, etc.). The journal expects philosophical precision in normative arguments; hand-waving is immediately visible.

4. **Exceeding 12,000 words:** This hard constraint is enforced. Papers longer than this may be returned unreviewed. Plan for ~8,000–11,000 words to allow breathing room.

5. **Treating philosophical distinctions as empirical facts:** Confusing "we should define X as Y because it clarifies argument Z" (philosophical) with "X is defined as Y in the data" (empirical). The journal publishes both, but conflation is a common rejection reason.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

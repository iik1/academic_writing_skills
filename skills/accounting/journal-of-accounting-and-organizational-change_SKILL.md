# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Accounting and Organizational Change

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ACCOUNT
- **Publisher:** Emerald Publishing Limited
- **Scope:** Platform for interdisciplinary research on contemporary organizational changes and accounting practices across industry, public sector, and nonprofit organizations.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.emeraldgrouppublishing.com/journal/jaoc (author guidelines behind Emerald login)
- **Field baseline:** account-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 8 articles from Volume 21, Issue 1 (January 2025)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

### Abstract Format
**ENFORCED:** Structured abstract with 4–7 labeled sub-headings (all per Emerald standard):
- **Purpose** (required) — research objective in one or two sentences
- **Design/methodology/approach** (required) — study design, data source, sample size, analytical method
- **Findings** (required) — key results in bullet or narrative form
- **Research limitations/implications** (optional) — bounds on generalizability, methodological constraints
- **Practical implications** (optional) — application to practice or policy
- **Social implications** (optional) — broader societal impact
- **Originality/value** (required) — contribution to literature or method

Maximum 250 words total (including keywords and article classification). This is a **structural requirement** not present in the ABS 4*/4 field baseline, which uses unstructured abstracts.

### Length
**RELAXATION:** Emerald journals at ABS 2 tier often accommodate shorter manuscripts than ABS 4 journals. No explicit word cap identified in public guidelines; articles in Vol. 21, Issue 1 range from ~7,000–12,000 words. Top-tier ACCOUNT journals (JAE, JAR) more commonly enforce 12,000–15,000 word limits. JAOC appears more flexible.

### Citation Format
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm: author-date, in-text parenthetical or narrative. Emerald house style specifies: multiple authors (3–5) cited as "Surname et al." at first and subsequent citations (may differ from field baseline which cites all authors at first mention in some contexts). Full author lists supplied in reference list.

### Headings
**PRACTICE ONLY (low prevalence):** Article titles occasionally use question format (e.g., "Determinants and impact of X on Y: a methodological approach") or declarative format with colons. No explicit heading level numbering (Roman vs. Arabic) observed in abstracts or visible metadata. Field baseline indicates journals vary by house style; JAOC does not mandate a specific convention in available public materials.

### Tables and Figures
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm: placed after references or in appendix. Notes required. Statistical choices (clustering, fixed effects) specified. Observed articles follow standard accounting practice.

### Explicit Prohibitions
**ENFORCED:** Originality and ethics statements required. Manuscripts must declare:
- Originality (not previously published, not under review elsewhere)
- Ethical approval statement if research involves human subjects or sensitive data (name of ethics board, approval number, date, national/international guidelines followed, informed consent details)
- Data availability statement (increasingly required)
- Author conflict-of-interest disclosure

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Abstract Structure
**ENFORCED DELTA:** JAOC enforces a **structured abstract** (4–7 labeled sub-headings) as a mandatory, journal-specific requirement. FIELD_SKILL baseline (ABS 4*/4 ACCOUNT journals) uses unstructured single-paragraph abstracts. This is a hard constraint that overrides field norms.

**Evidence:** All 8 articles in Vol. 21, Issue 1 follow this format consistently. Example: Bagonza et al. (2025, p. 1) opens with "Purpose — This study aims to examine whether…" followed by "Design/methodology/approach — Using fixed effects and the GMM model…" etc.

### Manuscript Length
**RELAXATION:** Emerald ABS 2 journals appear to accommodate shorter manuscripts (7,000–12,000 words observed) compared to ABS 4*/4 field baseline, which more rigorously enforces 12,000–15,000 word minimums. Not stated in guidelines but evident in practice.

### Voice and Register
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Observed articles use active voice, first-person plural ("we"), and measured language appropriate to evidence strength.

### Citation Density
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Introduction citations range from 20–40 prior works; results sections cite sparingly when interpreting findings. Author-date format throughout.

### Hedging and Causal Claims
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Well-identified quantitative designs use direct language ("we find," "the results show"). Qualitative and case-study work uses softened language ("suggests," "indicates"). Limitations are acknowledged in conclusions.

### Structural Deviations
**PRACTICE ONLY (high prevalence):** Methodology description in JAOC articles is notably **brief and compact** relative to ABS 4*/4 ACCOUNT journals. Where JAE/JAR papers devote 8–15 pages to methods, hypothesis development, and data description, JAOC articles (even empirical ones) condense this to 3–5 pages within the structured abstract or a dedicated Methods/Research Design section. This reflects the lower journal tier and interdisciplinary scope (not all readers are specialized accountants).

**Example:** Bagonza et al. (2025) summarizes a complex multi-year panel study (472 firms, 6-year window, GMM estimation) in ~500 words.

**Example:** Oyewo et al. (2025) describes SEM methodology, sample of 129 firms, and seven SMAT constructs in ~600 words.

### Findings Presentation
**PRACTICE ONLY:** Findings in JAOC often emphasize **practical or policy relevance** more explicitly than ABS 4*/4 baseline. Results sections frequently conclude with explicit statements about implications for managers, auditors, or policymakers, consistent with the journal's scope (organizational change, not pure accounting theory). This is more prominent than in JAE/JAR.

**Example:** Sumiyana et al. (2025) concludes findings with "recommendation for institutional reforms enabling LGIAs to transition from functional to structural officer roles," reflecting public-sector audit practice.

## Common Pitfalls for This Journal

1. **Unstructured abstract:** Submitting a single-paragraph abstract instead of the required 4–7 labeled sub-headings (Purpose, Design/methodology/approach, Findings, etc.). This is an immediate rejection-on-submission issue.

2. **Missing ethics statement:** Submitting qualitative or case-study work (common for JAOC's scope) without an explicit ethics board approval statement, informed consent documentation, or data governance disclosure. Emerald requires this.

3. **Over-brevity in methods:** Attempting to compress methodology into < 300 words in pursuit of short overall length. While JAOC manuscripts are shorter than ABS 4 norms, methods still require explicit sample description, variable definitions, and estimation approach. Skimping invites reviewer requests for clarification.

4. **Unclear originality claim:** Failing to clearly state what is novel in the introduction. JAOC's interdisciplinary scope means reviewers include practitioners and scholars from non-accounting disciplines; implicit contributions do not work.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Agricultural and Resource Economics Review

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Cambridge University Press (for the Northeastern Agricultural and Resource Economics Association)
- **Scope:** Economics of agriculture, food, natural resources, rural communities, and the environment, and associated subfields.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/agricultural-and-resource-economics-review/information/author-instructions and /information/author-instructions/preparing-your-materials
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 15 (10 FirstView 2025 titles/abstracts + 5 full abstract/structure pulls: "The effectiveness of a green default nudge in achieving resource conservation" Vol 53(2); "Market structure, power, and the unfair trading practices directive in the EU food sector" Vol 53(3); "Contract noncompliance in agricultural conservation programs: Panel evidence from Louisiana, USA" Vol 53(2); "The relationship between commodity diversification and the adoption of technological innovations for Southeast beef cattle producers"; "The value of water quality for coastal recreation in New England, USA"; plus the invited NAREA award piece "Reflections on 40 Years of Applied Economics Research on Agriculture and Water Quality")

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Title page submitted as a separate file (title, author details, acknowledgments, competing interests, data availability statement, funding statement) — manuscript body itself must be fully anonymized. No mandated body section-heading scheme is prescribed by the guidelines themselves, but observed practice is a consistent applied-empirical template (see Observed Deltas).

### Length
Two explicit article types with different word budgets: **Research Articles** ≈ 10,000 words including references; **Research Insights** (a shorter, concise-findings format) capped at 3,000 words. FIELD_SKILL does not specify length; this is journal-specific.

### Abstract
Abstract on the first page, maximum 200 words.

### Citations
References follow **Cambridge A style**, ordered by first appearance in text (not strict alphabetical reference-list ordering as in many other econ outlets).

### Headings
No fixed heading list mandated; author instructions require headings/subheadings for organization but leave naming to authors.

### Tables and Figures
All illustrations referred to as "Figures" (no separate "Graph"/"Chart" labels); numbered with consecutive Arabic numerals; captions mandatory for all figures and tables; alt-text/accessibility descriptions required to WCAG 2.1 Level AA; figures may be embedded in the main file for initial submission but must be supplied as separate files at revision. Guidance to "use only essential mathematical notation," with in-paragraph math rendered as formatted text (bold for vectors/matrices, italics for variables) rather than as displayed equations where possible — a stronger anti-formalism instruction than FIELD_SKILL's baseline.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Manuscript body must be anonymized (double-anonymized peer review); no simultaneous submission elsewhere; any generative-AI use for text or images must be disclosed; ORCID iD required for corresponding author; authors must explicitly cite/acknowledge prior work that "heavily influenced" the hypothesis, study design, or data used (phrased more pointedly than standard citation-ethics boilerplate).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
Applied/empirical articles converge on a near-uniform section template that is slightly more subdivided than the FIELD_SKILL empirical default: **Introduction → (Literature Review, sometimes standalone rather than folded into the Introduction) → Conceptual Framework/Overview (optional, topic-specific) → Data and Methods → Results (sometimes split as Results + Discussion, or Results + Discussion and Conclusion combined) → Conclusions → Supplementary material → Data availability statement → Funding statement → Competing interests.** The standalone "Literature Review" section (as its own numbered heading rather than absorbed into the Introduction) appears more consistently here than the FIELD_SKILL norm, which treats it as optional/often-omitted at the ABS 4*/4 level. ENFORCED-by-practice (PRACTICE ONLY — not stated in guidelines, but consistent across the sample).

Standardized end-matter blocks (Supplementary material, Data availability statement, Funding statement, Competing interests) appear as explicit numbered/labeled sections in every article checked, not folded into acknowledgments footnotes as is common at ABS 4*/4 outlets. PRACTICE ONLY, though the title-page requirement for competing interests/data availability/funding statements is itself ENFORCED by the guidelines.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Occasional non-standard formats appear: an invited NAREA award "Reflections" piece departed entirely from the empirical template (thematic sections, no Data/Methods/Results structure) — this is a recognized alternate article type (invited essay), not a deviation to emulate for a standard Research Article submission.

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting the manuscript body with author-identifying information left in (violates double-anonymized review; title page must be a separate file).
- Exceeding the 200-word abstract cap, or omitting the required keywords/JEL classifications on the first page.
- Formatting references alphabetically instead of by first appearance (Cambridge A style order differs from the more common alphabetical author-year list).
- Missing the mandatory Data Availability, Funding, and Competing Interests statements as distinct labeled sections.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: International Journal of Economic Theory

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Wiley-Blackwell
- **Scope:** Theoretical economics across all fields including applied macroeconomics, decision theory, game theory, general equilibrium, industrial organization, mathematical methods for economics, and experimental economics. Also publishes empirical and experimental analyses with strong relevance to economic theory.
- **Editors:** Kazuo Nishimura (Kobe University, Kyoto University) and Makoto Yano (Kyoto University, RIETI)
- **Guidelines URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/page/journal/17427363/homepage/forauthors.html
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 12 (from volume 21-22, 2025-2026; details in Sources)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Manuscripts must be presented in this order:
1. Title page (with short running title of less than 40 characters)
2. Abstract and keywords
3. Text (main body sections)
4. References
5. Appendices (if any)
6. Figure legends

This is a strict ordering requirement specific to IJET submission.

### Title
- Must be short and informative
- Should contain major keywords
- Do not use abbreviations in the title

### Abstract
Standard length requirement (no explicit word limit found in accessible guidelines, but peer journals indicate 250 words is typical for economic theory journals). No evidence of structured format requirement (i.e., no Background/Methods/Results/Conclusions mandate observed in sample abstracts).

### Keywords
Required; exact number not specified in accessible guidelines, though 5-6 keywords is typical for Wiley economics journals.

### Language
- **US spelling mandatory** — follow Merriam-Webster's Collegiate Dictionary (latest edition)
- **Abbreviations:** Avoid unless used repeatedly and helpful to reader. Introduce term in full, followed by abbreviation in parentheses on first use; use abbreviation only thereafter.
- Non-native English speakers are requested to have manuscripts checked by a native English speaker before submission.

### Citations
- No explicit format requirement found in accessible guidelines. FIELD_SKILL (author-year, parenthetical or in-text) applies.

### Tables and Figures
- Electronic figures should be supplied as separate files
- All other content (tables, text) supplied as single file

### Submission
- Submit electronically at https://wiley.atyponrex.com/journal/IJET (as of 2025, via Research Exchange platform)
- Multiple concurrent submissions not permitted; only one manuscript at a time

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Multiple submissions (of different articles) will not be accepted simultaneously

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Accessibility and Scope
RELAXATION: This is an ABS 2 journal (lower tier than the ABS 4*/4 baseline), published in cooperation with Kyoto University. The journal explicitly aims to "encourage the work of young researchers who are not yet established in the profession." This signals a lower barrier to entry than top-tier outlets. However, acceptance criteria remain "quality and originality of the research and its significance to the readership."

### Theory-Specific Emphasis
PRACTICE: Theory papers dominate the journal (100% of spot-check sample). While FIELD_SKILL covers both empirical and theory papers, IJET is explicitly theory-focused. Empirical work is published but is rare and must demonstrate "strong relevance to economic theory." This tightens the implicit expectations around novelty and theoretical rigor compared to a mixed journal.

### Mathematical Exposition
PRACTICE: Theory papers in IJET follow formal theorem-proof structure more strictly than the FIELD_SKILL baseline suggests. All major theoretical contributions appear as numbered theorems or formal propositions with proofs (usually in appendix). Economic interpretation is provided in main text, consistent with FIELD_SKILL, but the formalism is non-negotiable. No deviations from FIELD_SKILL in voice or prose style observed; the mathematics and prose are clearly separated.

### Structural Diversity
PRACTICE: Beyond the standard FIELD_SKILL structure (Introduction, Model/Theory, Results, Conclusion), papers in IJET frequently include subsections for Extensions or Robustness checks within the body. No separate "Discussion" section is common; interpretation is integrated into Results as in FIELD_SKILL.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Abbreviating in the title.** IJET explicitly prohibits this; reviewers expect a clean, unabbreviated title even if terms are abbreviated in the body.

2. **Inconsistent abbreviation introduction.** Always introduce the term in full first, then place the abbreviation in parentheses. Reviewers catch repeated lapses in this convention.

3. **Weak theoretical positioning for empirical papers.** If submitting empirical work, the connection to theory must be explicit and substantial. "Empirically tests the prediction of [theory]" is the standard framing. Papers that feel primarily empirical with theory added as an afterthought are at risk.

4. **Mismatched file format at submission.** Ensure the entire manuscript (except figures) is in a single file. Submitting text and tables as separate files causes desk rejects.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

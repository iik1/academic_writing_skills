# Journal Writing Skill: Economic Record

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2025 Rating:** A
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Wiley-Blackwell
- **Scope:** Advances in knowledge and understanding of economics across theory, applied, and policy areas. Publishes original articles of relevance to the Australian economy and internationally. Quarterly publication (March, June, September, December) with a special fifth issue annually featuring selected papers from the Australian Conference of Economists.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/page/journal/14754932/homepage/forauthors.html (accessible via Wiley author portal; publicly visible guidelines available through Economic Society of Australia website)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 4 (2026 volume 102; sample includes empirical and econometric papers from recent issues)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. No mandatory deviation from typical empirical or theory paper structure. Observed articles follow: Introduction → Background/Institutional Context (where relevant) → Data/Methodology → Results → Conclusion. Free Format submission is available, indicating flexibility.

### Length
No explicit word or page limit found in publicly accessible guidelines. Observed articles range 15–25 pages in journal format (approximately 6,000–12,000 words estimated). The journal's statement that "the editor gives preference to articles that are succinctly written" suggests brevity is valued but not enforced as a hard cap.

### Abstract
Standard requirement; format not explicitly detailed in accessible guidelines. Observed abstracts are unstructured, approximately 150–250 words.

### Citations
**Citation format:** Harvard (author-year, parenthetical). This is a FIELD_SKILL norm and is confirmed as the journal's standard.

**Data and replication:** Strict requirement. Authors must ensure "data used in the analysis are clearly and precisely documented, are available to others to enable replication of results, and where details of the computations sufficient for replication are provided." If data cannot be made available, authors must inform the Editor at submission time. This is a journal-specific ENFORCED requirement not universally mandated at the field level.

### Headings
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Descriptive section headings (not Roman numerals or numbers alone). Observed articles use title-case, descriptive headings reflecting content.

### Tables and Figures
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Both are common; figures illustrate key variation and model fit; tables present detailed results. Notes explain variable definitions, standard error clustering, and sample restrictions.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Anonymity requirement: The journal operates double-blind review; authors must anonymise manuscripts thoroughly to remain anonymous to reviewers throughout the review process. This is a FIELD_SKILL norm applied strictly.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

After spot-checking recent articles across multiple issues and analyzing the RBA working paper version of an article subsequently published in Economic Record (Bowman, Hambur & Markovski 2026 on occupational entry regulations), no significant deviations from FIELD_SKILL norms were observed in the following dimensions:

- **Voice and Register:** First-person plural ("we") is the standard; active voice preferred; formality high but economical. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Sentence and Paragraph Style:** Medium to long sentences; high paragraph density (4–10 sentences); high technical vocabulary density. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Argumentation:** Introductions follow the CARS-variant structure (hook → question → approach → results preview → contributions → roadmap). FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Methods and Identification:** Empirical papers state identification strategy explicitly. Procedural detail is sufficient for replication without being prescriptive. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Results Reporting:** Results and interpretation are integrated; tables are the primary vehicle; quantitative specificity is the norm; statistical significance is reported alongside economic significance. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Citation Density:** High in Introduction; medium in Methods; low to medium in Results; low in Conclusion. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Hedging:** Low overall; hedging appears only in contexts where FIELD_SKILL expects it (external validity, causal claims beyond the identified parameter, data limitations). FIELD DEFAULT.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Anonymisation failure in submission:** The journal requires strict double-blind anonymisation. Remove author names, identifying references (e.g., "as we showed in Smith 2020"), and self-citations that reveal identity. Check footnotes, headers, and document metadata.

2. **Insufficient data archiving plan:** Clearly document where replication data will be deposited *at submission time*. If data cannot be released (e.g., confidential), state this explicitly and explain why. Vague promises of availability upon request are insufficient.

3. **Harvard reference style inconsistency:** While FIELD_SKILL treats Harvard as the norm, Economic Record enforces it strictly. Ensure in-text citations use (Author Year) format and reference list is properly alphabetised. Publisher formatting rules apply at the proofs stage, but submissions should be consistently formatted.

4. **Overstated causal language:** Empirical papers using reduced-form or non-experimental identification should hedge causal interpretation appropriately (e.g., "consistent with" rather than "proves"). The journal publishes well-identified null results, so strong identification is valued over strong claims.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

- Economic Society of Australia official announcements (https://esacentral.org.au)
- RePEc/IDEAS journal archive with abstracts and metadata
- Working paper versions of accepted articles (e.g., RBA Research Discussion Papers, author repositories)

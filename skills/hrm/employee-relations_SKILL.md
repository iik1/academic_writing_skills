# Journal Writing Skill: Employee Relations

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** HRM&EMP
- **Publisher:** Emerald Publishing
- **Scope:** International journal covering HR, employment, and industrial relations with emphasis on contemporary employment issues within institutional and national contexts. Publishes empirical (quantitative and qualitative), conceptual, and review articles.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.emeraldgrouppublishing.com/journal/er
- **Field baseline:** hrm-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 8+ (Vol 47–48, 2024–2025; limited access; analysis based on abstract structures, metadata, and related HRM journal examples)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Main sections must follow: Introduction, Literature Review, Methodology, Findings (or Results), Discussion, and Conclusion. Back matter includes Acknowledgements (optional), Ethics Approval Statement (if applicable), and Author Contributions (optional).

### Length
No explicit word count limit found in publicly accessible guidelines. Emerald journals typically set journal-specific limits (often 6,000–9,000 words). Word count includes abstract, all text in tables and figures, and references. Each table or figure adds a standard 280 words to the total count.

### Abstract
**Structured abstract required** with the following labelled sections (four obligatory; three optional):
- Purpose (obligatory)
- Design/Methodology/Approach (obligatory)
- Findings (obligatory)
- Originality/Value (obligatory)
- Research Limitations/Implications (optional; omit if not applicable)
- Practical Implications (optional; omit if not applicable)
- Social Implications (optional; omit if not applicable)

Maximum 250 words total, including keywords. Abstract must accurately reflect what appears in the paper.

### Keywords
Provide exactly 5 keywords (not more) immediately after the abstract. Keywords are used for indexing; ensure they reflect the core concepts of the manuscript.

### Citations
Harvard style (author-date system). All major claims require citation support. Direct quotation of prior scholarship is rare; paraphrase is strongly preferred.

### Headings
Standard hierarchical structure: Introduction as first-level heading, main sections as level-one headings, subsections as level-two (e.g., "2.1 Theoretical Framework"). Avoid over-fragmentation of subsections.

### Tables and Figures
All tables and figures should be embedded and referenced in text. Captions must be clear and self-contained. Figures should use accessible fonts and avoid colour-only differentiation (consider colour-blind accessibility).

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Do not use generative AI to draft or create new content (e.g., writing methodology, results, or discussion sections). Copy-editing existing work for clarity and grammar is permitted.
- Originality: work must not have been previously published or submitted elsewhere. Simultaneous submission to other journals is not permitted.
- Conflict of interest must be declared if any exists.
- If citing employees, organizations, or companies by name using non-public information, written consent from the named party (individual, HR department, or board member) is required before publication.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Structured Abstract Format
**ENFORCED (stated in guidelines AND observed in published abstracts).** This is the most significant structural deviation from general HRM field norms. The four-part structured abstract (Purpose, Design/Methodology, Findings, Originality/Value) is mandatory and must be clearly labelled. This differs from the field baseline, which does not mandate structured abstracts. Introductory paragraphs should make the paper's contribution explicit so reviewers can quickly assess fit and significance.

### Practical Implications Emphasis
**ENFORCED.** While the FIELD_SKILL baseline notes that "practical implications are treated as a required component of the discussion," Employee Relations gives explicit status to "Practical Implications" as an optional abstract subsection and expects discussion sections to clearly distinguish theoretical contributions from practical/policy implications. This reflects the journal's applied orientation toward HR practitioners and managers. Failure to articulate "so what?" for practice may be flagged in review.

### Keyword Discipline
**ENFORCED.** Exactly 5 keywords are required and must be provided after the abstract. This is more rigid than the field baseline and is used for indexing purposes. Ensure keywords are precise descriptors of the paper's main concepts, not merely topic words.

## Dimensions With No Delta
The following aspects align with FIELD_SKILL norms and require no journal-specific modification:

- Voice and register: FIELD DEFAULT. Formal, impersonal academic register with first-person plural permitted in introduction, methods, and discussion.
- Sentence and paragraph style: FIELD DEFAULT. Medium-to-long sentences with subordination and topic-sentence-led paragraphs common.
- Argumentation and CARS structure: FIELD DEFAULT. Introduction should establish importance, identify gap, and state contribution.
- Methods conventions: FIELD DEFAULT. Quantitative papers should report sample, measures with reliability statistics, and estimation strategy; qualitative papers should report sampling, data collection, and analytic approach; reflexivity and ethical approval common.
- Results reporting: FIELD DEFAULT. Quantitative results narrated in prose with reference to tables; qualitative findings organized thematically.
- Discussion and conclusion norms: FIELD DEFAULT. Discussion revisits contributions, distinguishes theoretical from practical implications, and includes limitations and future directions.
- Citation density and engagement: FIELD DEFAULT. Highest in introduction and literature review; paraphrase preferred over direct quotation; diplomacy in disagreement.
- Hedging and epistemic caution: FIELD DEFAULT. Moderate-to-high hedging in discussion and conclusion; causal language qualified in observational designs.
- Qualitative/quantitative balance: FIELD DEFAULT. Both are equally valued; no methodological hierarchy.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Unstructured abstract.** Submitting a narrative abstract instead of the four-part structured format. Reviewers and editors screen for this immediately and may desk-reject.
- **Muddled practical implications.** Failing to explicitly articulate what practitioners or managers should do with the findings. This journal expects a clear translation from research to practice.
- **Keyword duplication or vagueness.** Using 6+ keywords, repeating words from the title, or choosing keywords too broad or too narrow. Exactly 5 precise, indexable keywords are expected.
- **Missing consent statements.** If the paper names employees, organizations, or uses non-public information, failing to note written consent in the methods or ethics section may lead to revise-and-resubmit or rejection on ethical grounds.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

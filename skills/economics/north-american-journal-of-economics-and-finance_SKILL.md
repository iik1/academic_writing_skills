# Journal Writing Skill: North American Journal of Economics and Finance

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Elsevier
- **Scope:** Financial economics, monetary economics, econometrics, volatility, stock market analysis, financial intermediation, capital markets, law and finance, international finance, and corporate governance. The journal emphasizes balance between theoretical and empirical work and expects empirical analyses to be replicable.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/the-north-american-journal-of-economics-and-finance/publish/guide-for-authors (paywalled; see citation style guide access notes below)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 11 (2025 volumes 77–80; diverse empirical and theoretical articles)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows standard ECON empirical and theoretical formats. No journal-specific mandate documented; articles observed follow FIELD_SKILL conventions (Introduction, Methods/Model, Results, Conclusion with optional Background/Institutional Setting and Discussion sections).

### Length
No explicit word or page limit documented in publicly accessible guidelines. Articles in volumes 77–80 (2025) range from approximately 30–50 pages (full text with appendices), suggesting no strict limit.

### Abstract
Standard structured abstract (implied from article patterns, not explicitly confirmed in accessible guidelines). Observed abstracts include: research question, methodology, key findings, and implications. No explicit word limit found in public guidelines; observed abstracts are 150–250 words.

### Citations
**Author-year format (parenthetical):** Mandatory. Examples from observed articles: "(Lee & Wu, 2025)", "(Mo et al., 2025)". This matches FIELD_SKILL norm exactly.

**Bibliography format:** Follows standard Elsevier/APA-adjacent conventions:
- Journal articles: Author(s). (Year). Article title. *Journal Title*, Volume(Issue), page range or article number.
- Books: Author(s). (Year). *Book Title*. Publisher.
- Book chapters: Author(s). (Year). Chapter title. In Editor(s) (Ed.), *Book Title* (pp. page range). Publisher.

Direct quotations are rare; paraphrase with citation is the norm (consistent with FIELD_SKILL).

### Headings
No explicit journal mandate observed. Practice shows:
- Section headings use Arabic numerals or no numbering
- Subsection headings appear indented or in bold
- Standard primary headings: Introduction, [Background/Institutional Setting], Data/Methodology, Results, Conclusion, References
- Examples: "1. Introduction", "2. Methodology", "3. Results" or unnumbered equivalents

### Tables and Figures
- **Tables:** Standard practice; typically include notes explaining variable definitions, data sources, sample composition, standard error clustering, and statistical significance markers (*, **, ***).
- **Figures:** Common for visualizing trends, distributions, and key outcome variation. Caption and note conventions follow Elsevier standard.
- **Data availability:** AI-generated artwork in figures is explicitly prohibited (stated in public guidelines).

### Explicit Prohibitions
1. **Generative AI artwork:** Use of generative AI or AI-assisted tools for graphical abstracts and figures is not permitted.
2. **Supplementary material:** All supplementary files must be cited in the manuscript text and submitted simultaneously.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Keywords and JEL Classification
**ENFORCED PRACTICE** (observed in all 11 articles examined, not explicitly mandated in accessible guidelines but systematic across the journal).

All empirical and theoretical papers include:
- **Keywords:** 4–6 discipline-specific terms (e.g., "Safe havens, cryptocurrencies, geopolitical risk, quantile analysis, traditional assets").
- **JEL classification codes:** 2–4 codes placed after abstract (examples from observed articles: G10, G14, G41; C58, G15).

This is not stated in FIELD_SKILL and appears to be a journal standard, not a field norm.

### Voice and Register
**Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.** All 11 articles observed use first-person plural ("we show," "we find," "we develop," "we estimate") as the dominant voice. Active voice strongly preferred. Technical vocabulary density is high. No journal-specific deviation.

### Sentence and Paragraph Style
**Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.** Observed articles maintain medium-to-long sentence length driven by technical content. Paragraph density is high (4–8 sentences per paragraph). No journal-specific deviation.

### Argumentation and Rhetorical Moves
**Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.** Introduction structure follows the ECON pattern: motivating hook, research question, approach/data, preview of main results (quantitative where possible), contributions to literature, and roadmap. All 11 articles examined follow this structure. No journal-specific deviation.

### Methods Conventions
**Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.** Mixed tense convention observed: past tense for data/estimation procedures, present tense for model properties and identification logic. Procedural detail is sufficient for replication. Identification strategy is stated prominently in empirical papers. Robustness checks are standard. No journal-specific deviation.

### Results and Reporting Norms
**Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.** Results and interpretation are integrated. Tables are the primary vehicle for quantitative results. Prose highlights key coefficients and magnitudes bearing on main claims. Statistical and economic significance are reported together. Null results are reported directly. No journal-specific deviation.

### Citation Density
**Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.** High density in Introduction and Methods; medium in Results; low in Conclusion. Examples from observed articles: Mo et al. (2025) cites extensively in Introduction (48 references total); Chen & Wu (2025) integrates citations into interpretation of results. No journal-specific deviation.

### Hedging and Epistemic Caution
**Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.** Overall hedging intensity is low. Clear claims are preferred. Hedging appears only in contexts where appropriate: external validity, mechanism vs. reduced-form interpretation, data limitations. Examples: "This result holds for" (external validity qualifier), "consistent with the prediction of [model]" (mechanism hedging). No journal-specific deviation.

## Journal-Specific Practices (Not Deviations from FIELD_SKILL)

### Keywords and JEL Classification
Include 4–6 keywords and 2–4 JEL classification codes after the abstract. This is systematic practice in NAJEF but not mandated in ECON field baseline. Consider this a journal convention to match when submitting.

Examples from articles examined:
- Keywords: "Safe havens, cryptocurrencies, geopolitical risk, quantile analysis, traditional assets"
- JEL: G10 (General Financial Markets), G14 (Information and Market Efficiency), G41 (Behavioral Finance)

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Missing keywords and JEL codes:** While not explicitly stated in publicly accessible guidelines, all 11 articles examined include these. Include them in your submission to match journal practice.

2. **Insufficient robustness checks:** NAJEF publishes empirical work on financial markets and policy. Editors and reviewers expect robustness across sample definitions, time periods, and alternative specifications. Null or unexpected results are publishable if robustness is strong and the question is important.

3. **Vague claims without magnitudes:** State results with quantitative specificity (e.g., "oil price changes with κ>12 can strongly predict bear stock markets" rather than "oil prices predict market downturns"). The journal's focus on financial economics demands precise effect sizes.

4. **Insufficient engagement with existing literature:** NAJEF authors engage intensively with prior work in positioning contributions. Identify the papers most similar to yours and explain explicitly what your paper does that those do not.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

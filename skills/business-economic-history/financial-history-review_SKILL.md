# Journal Writing Skill: Financial History Review

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** BUS HIST & ECON HIST
- **Publisher:** Cambridge University Press
- **Scope:** Scholarly research on banking, financial, and monetary history across all periods and regions. Open to analytical narratives, theoretically-inspired research, advanced empirical analysis, and interdisciplinary connections between finance, history, policy, culture, and society.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/financial-history-review/information/author-instructions
- **Field baseline:** business-economic-history-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 11 (from Volume 31 Issues 1–2 and Volume 32 Issue 3, 2024–2025)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Articles follow a traditional structure typical of historical writing: opening section establishing the historical puzzle and argument, literature/historiographical review (often woven into introduction), methods/sources section (where applicable), substantive sections organized chronologically or thematically, and a conclusion. Three article types are accepted: full Articles, Shorter Papers, and "The Past Mirror" (notes, surveys, debates). ENFORCED.

### Length
- **Articles:** Approximately 8,000 words maximum, including footnotes, tables, figures, and all illustrative material (a graph occupying an A4 page counts as ~300 words). ENFORCED.
- **Shorter Papers and "Past Mirror" contributions:** Word limits not explicitly stated in guidelines but implied to be shorter than standard articles. GUIDELINE ONLY.

### Abstract
- **Length:** Maximum 200 words. ENFORCED.
- **Keywords:** Maximum 4 keywords. ENFORCED.
- **Format:** Non-technical summary of the puzzle, argument, and contribution (unstructured, not divided into background/methods/results sections). ENFORCED.

### Citations
- **System:** Chicago Manual of Style Notes and Bibliography system (numbered footnotes/endnotes with full publication details on first citation, abbreviated on subsequent citations). ENFORCED.
- **Footnote placement:** Typed, double-spaced, numbered consecutively, grouped together at the end of the manuscript. ENFORCED.
- **Footnote content:** Footnotes should be concerned solely with source citation; substantive footnotes involving commentary or argument should be avoided. ENFORCED.
- **Bibliography:** References appear after footnotes, arranged alphabetically by author surname (in capital letters). ENFORCED.

### Headings
Follows standard scholarly heading hierarchy (not explicitly detailed in guidelines; practice in published articles shows clear hierarchical structure with main section headings, and occasionally subsection headings). FIELD DEFAULT.

### Tables and Figures
- **Format:** Supplied as separate PDF documents (distinct from main text Word/RTF document). ENFORCED.
- **Presentation:** Must be clearly sourced and accompanied by prose interpretation rather than left to stand alone (consistent with field baseline). FIELD DEFAULT.

### Manuscript Preparation
- **Physical format:** Double-spaced throughout on A4 (29cm x 21cm or 11" x 8"), with wide margins on all sides. Pages numbered consecutively starting from title page. ENFORCED.
- **File format:** Main text in Word (.docx, .doc) or RTF; tables and figures as separate PDF. ENFORCED.
- **Language:** English; authors whose native language is not English encouraged to have manuscripts reviewed by professional native translator before submission. ENFORCED.

### Submission
Manuscripts submitted through ScholarOne system (mc.manuscriptcentral.com/fhr). ENFORCED.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Strict Word Limit
The journal enforces a tight ~8,000-word maximum (including all supporting material), whereas the field baseline does not specify length constraints. This is a RELAXATION relative to some top-tier journals (ABS 4 journals often accommodate longer, more discursive pieces) but reflects FHR's positioning as an ABS 2 journal. Authors must be disciplined about synthesis and argument compression. Figures and tables count toward the limit, incentivizing selective use of illustrative material.

### Footnote Content Restriction
FHR explicitly prohibits substantive commentary or argument in footnotes, restricting them to source citation only. The field baseline allows substantive footnotes for interpretive commentary. This is a RELAXATION that forces argumentative moves into the main text where they must compete for space under the word limit, making the writing tighter and more linear than in narrative-heavy field pieces.

### Abstract Word Limit
The 200-word abstract maximum is at the lower end of practice in comparable field journals (some allow 250+ words). This RELAXATION compels economical previewing of argument and contribution.

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Voice and Register:** FIELD DEFAULT. Formal scholarly register with narrative character; first-person plural and impersonal constructions both accepted; measured, interpretive claims favored over bold assertion.
- **Sentence and Paragraph Style:** FIELD DEFAULT. Medium-to-long sentences with subordinate clauses; substantial paragraphs developed with evidence; lighter signposting than social-science writing; qualifying clauses normal.
- **Argumentation and Rhetorical Moves:** FIELD DEFAULT. CARS-like pattern of establishing phenomenon, showing gap in existing literature, staking claim; historiographical framing valued; counter-narratives and revisionist claims rewarded; evidence accumulation over formal hypothesis-testing.
- **Methods Conventions:** FIELD DEFAULT. Procedural detail expected on sources, data, and limitations; past tense dominates; for quantitative work, econometric framing applied; reflexivity about archive limits valued.
- **Results and Reporting:** FIELD DEFAULT. Narrative articles integrate evidence throughout; quantitative economic history follows distinct results/discussion sections; tables and figures accompanied by prose interpretation; statistical precision expected in quantitative work.
- **Discussion and Conclusion:** FIELD DEFAULT. Restatement of contribution, positioning within broader debates, discussion of generalizability and limitations expected.
- **Citation Density:** FIELD DEFAULT. High throughout; citations to primary and secondary sources; paraphrase dominates for secondary literature; direct quotation for primary sources and distinctive phrasing; courteous disagreement with prior work.
- **Hedging and Epistemic Caution:** FIELD DEFAULT. Moderate hedging with language like "suggests," "appears," "likely"; confident direct statements of central argument expected; uncertainty about counterfactuals flagged.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Exceeding the word limit:** At ~8,000 words maximum (including footnotes, tables, figures), manuscripts must be tightly written. Overly discursive introductions, lengthy historiographical reviews, or exhaustive source descriptions will quickly consume space. Compress ruthlessly and move interpretive detail into the main argument.

2. **Substantive commentary in footnotes:** Temptation to relegate argument or interpretation to footnotes to save space is futile under FHR guidelines; footnotes are citation-only. All argumentative moves must be in the body text. This enforces a more linear, linear narrative flow than some field journals.

3. **Tables and figures not sourced or interpreted:** Illustrative material must be clearly sourced and accompanied by explanatory prose. Figures and tables left to stand alone without textual engagement will be flagged; remember they also count toward the word limit, so only include material essential to the argument.

4. **Incorrect citation format or endnote structure:** Ensure strict adherence to Chicago Notes-Bibliography system: full citations on first mention, abbreviated on subsequent; footnotes grouped at end (not at bottom of page); substantive content removed from notes. Editors and reviewers trained in historical conventions will scrutinize citation accuracy.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

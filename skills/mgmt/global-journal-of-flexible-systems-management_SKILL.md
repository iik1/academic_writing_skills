# Journal Writing Skill: Global Journal of Flexible Systems Management

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN / General Management
- **Publisher:** Springer Nature
- **Scope:** Publishes conceptual frameworks, empirical studies, case experiences, reviews, and mixed-method research on organizational flexibility across strategic, manufacturing, supply chain, organizational, information systems, marketing, and financial domains.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://link.springer.com/journal/40171/submission-guidelines (authentication required)
- **Field baseline:** mgmt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 8 (2024–2025 articles; abstracts and metadata accessed via IDEAS/RePEc and Springer; full texts behind paywall)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

### Structure
Follows standard empirical article format: Abstract → Introduction → Methodology → Results/Findings → Discussion/Conclusions. Systematic reviews and theoretical articles may organize conceptual sections thematically rather than by pre-specified hypotheses.

### Length
No explicit word limit stated in accessible guidelines. Typical articles range 8,000–18,000 words based on article type (empirical quantitative, qualitative, or review).

### Abstract
- Unstructured (no labeled sections required).
- Typically 150–200 words.
- States research gap, method/approach, key findings, and contribution.
- No citations in abstract.

### Citations
- **Style:** Author-date in parentheses (APA format).
- **Format:** (Author, Year) or Author (Year) when author is part of sentence.
- **Multiple citations:** (Jones, 2010; Smith & Lee, 2015) alphabetically ordered.
- **Three+ authors:** Use et al. from first citation.
- **Reference list:** Alphabetical by surname.

### Headings
- Hierarchical (Introduction, Methodology, Results/Findings, Discussion, Conclusions).
- Numbered headings not standard; section titles are semantic (descriptive of content, not generic).
- Subsection headings permitted and commonly used.

### Tables and Figures
- Tables used extensively for data presentation, statistical results, and systematic organization of findings.
- Figures include network visualizations (VOS viewer maps), trend plots, and conceptual models.
- Table notes and figure captions describe content and data sources.
- Tables and figures numbered consecutively; captions appear below tables and above figures.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- **Anonymity:** Springer's system maintains author anonymity for double-blind review. Author information must be submitted separately via the submission interface, not embedded in manuscript or on title page.
- **File formats:** Submit editable source files (.docx, LaTeX); PDFs or image-only versions will not be considered for review.
- **Declarations:** Author contributions, competing interests, and data availability statements required at submission via interface (not in manuscript body).

### Data Sharing
- Authors encouraged to deposit supporting data in publicly available repositories.
- Open Choice option available (Creative Commons licensed).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Article Type Prevalence
GJFSM publishes a notably high proportion of **systematic literature reviews** and **mixed-method studies** compared to the MGMT baseline. Empirical hypothesis-driven quantitative work is present but not dominant. Review articles use **thematic synthesis** rather than hypothesis-testing. This reflects the journal's scope on "flexibility research" as an integrative theme rather than a single theoretical tradition.

**Classification:** PRACTICE ONLY (not enforced; reflects journal's editorial portfolio, not a style requirement). Hypotheses remain appropriate for empirical studies, but reviews and conceptual work dominate submissions.

### Methodology Labeling
The journal uses **"Methodology"** (or "Research Methodology") as the section heading rather than "Methods." For review articles, "Search Strategy," "Inclusion Criteria," and "Analysis Approach" appear as subsections or separate headings.

**Classification:** ENFORCED (observed consistently across 2024–2025 articles).

### Keyword Prominence
Abstracts and front matter emphasize keyword co-occurrence and thematic categorization. Articles often note key research domains (e.g., "strategic flexibility," "supply chain flexibility") as organizing frameworks in the introduction.

**Classification:** PRACTICE ONLY (not a mandate, but common organizational strategy for clarity in a domain-heavy field).

### Evidence Presentation
Quantitative findings presented via regression tables or statistical summaries. Qualitative and mixed-method articles use **data displays** (tables of codes, process models, typologies) and thematic categorization to organize findings. Quotations and illustrative examples less prominent than in pure qualitative management work (AMJ, ASQ).

**Classification:** FIELD DEFAULT (consistent with MGMT baseline for evidence presentation; no journal-specific deviation).

### Citation Density in Results
Results sections cite sparingly (consistent with FIELD_SKILL). Discussion sections integrate citations heavily to connect findings to the flexibility research literature (global, strategic, manufacturing, supply chain domains).

**Classification:** FIELD DEFAULT (no deviation).

### Hedging and Theoretical Claims
Papers use moderate hedging for empirical findings ("suggest," "may indicate," "appear to") but make more assertive claims about theoretical frameworks and conceptual contributions. Reviews assert synthesized conclusions (e.g., "This review identifies three critical success factors...") without extensive qualification.

**Classification:** FIELD DEFAULT (consistent with MGMT convention; no anomaly).

### Future Research Signposting
Discussion and conclusion sections explicitly identify **gaps in current research** and call for **specific types of studies** (empirical validation, longitudinal designs, cross-cultural investigation). This is more explicit and structured than in some MGMT outlets.

**Classification:** PRACTICE ONLY (very common but not explicitly mandated).

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Submitting author-identifying information in the manuscript:** The journal uses a new Springer submission system requiring author details to be submitted separately via the interface for double-blind review. Do NOT include author names, affiliations, or identifying statements on a title page or embedded in the text.

2. **Incomplete editable source files:** The journal requires ALL original source files (.docx or .tex) to accompany submission. PDFs or screenshots will not be considered. Missing files delay or block review.

3. **Weak gap framing in introduction:** GJFSM reviews often emphasize conceptual consensus or integration. Avoid framing a gap as "research has not addressed X." Instead, frame it as "research has not integrated X perspective" or "contradictory findings exist regarding how X relates to Y in flexible systems context."

4. **Unclear thematic organization in reviews:** Systematic reviews in GJFSM organize findings by thematic category (e.g., strategic flexibility, supply chain flexibility) rather than by methodology or chronology. Ensure synthesis sections align findings with these core flexibility domains.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

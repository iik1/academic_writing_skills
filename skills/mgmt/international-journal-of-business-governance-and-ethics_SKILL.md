# Journal Writing Skill: International Journal of Business Governance and Ethics

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN
- **Publisher:** Inderscience Enterprises Ltd
- **Scope:** Publishes theoretical, empirical (surveys and case studies), and critically reflective work on business governance and ethics topics including board composition, stakeholder governance, business ethics, and corporate social responsibility.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.inderscience.com/ijbge/ (author guidelines available via submissions portal)
- **Field baseline:** mgmt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 10 articles from Vol. 18–19 (2024–2025), plus 62 forthcoming article titles examined

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows standard management article templates (quantitative deductive: Abstract → Introduction → Theory/Hypotheses → Methods → Results → Discussion/Conclusion; theoretical/conceptual: Abstract → Introduction → [Conceptual sections] → Discussion/Conclusion). No mandatory departures observed from field baseline.

### Length
No explicit word limit stated in accessible guidelines. Spot-checked articles range from empirical studies (~10,000–15,000 words typical) to conceptual/review work. No evidence of unusual length constraints.

### Abstract
Follows field baseline. Spot-checked abstracts are unstructured, typically 100–200 words, state research question/gap, method/approach, and main finding. No explicit abstract word limit published.

### Citations
Spot-checked articles follow APA style (Author, Year) in-text citations with alphabetized reference lists. Density patterns match field baseline: theory-heavy intro and discussion sections, sparse in results. No journal-specific deviation observed.

### Headings
Spot-checked articles use numbered or unnumbered Level 1–3 headings following field baseline conventions. No unusual heading hierarchy observed. Inderscience general guidelines suggest Times New Roman 12pt for body text; heading formatting follows academic convention.

### Tables and Figures
Tables present regression coefficients with standard errors in parentheses, significance asterisks, and table notes—consistent with field baseline. Figures include process models, typologies, and conceptual diagrams in theoretical work. No journal-specific rule observed.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Research briefs and notes are **not** accepted (confirmed from official guidelines).
- All manuscripts must be original and not previously published or under consideration elsewhere.
- Conference papers accepted only if completely rewritten with copyright clearance.
- Double-blind peer review process mandatory.
- All authors must declare agreement with manuscript content and comply with Ethical Guidelines for Authors.
- Open-access only: article processing charge (APC) applies (EUR 1,700 as of 2026).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Governance and Ethics Specificity
While FIELD_SKILL describes general management article conventions, IJBGE's scope is narrower: boards, executive compensation, CSR, corporate social responsibility disclosure, ESG, stakeholder governance, and earnings management are dominant topics. Articles routinely examine governance mechanisms (board diversity, composition, independence) and ethics-related outcomes (CSR reporting, whistleblowing, inclusiveness, ethical climate). This is a *topical narrowing*, not a structural or stylistic delta—writers should expect reviewers familiar with governance and ethics literature and ensure theoretical framing is precise in these domains.

### Emphasis on Institutional and Empirical Rigor
Spot-checked quantitative articles (e.g., "Agency problem as missing link between CSR and firm performance," "Does foreign ownership affect corporate cash holdings?") show frequent use of system GMM estimation, difference-in-differences, and fixed-effects panel models for governance-related endogeneity concerns. While field baseline expects robustness checks, IJBGE articles show systematic attention to identification strategies (e.g., testing CSR reporting's causal effect on firm performance using agency-theory framing). This reflects ABS 2 tier expectations, not a written-form delta.

### Qualitative and Mixed-Methods Presence
Several spot-checked articles (e.g., "Organisational inclusiveness on perception of ethical climate") employ survey-based quantitative methodology with conclusive research design. Others (e.g., "Social ties, group dynamics, and executive compensation") use integrative two-stage frameworks blending economic and socio-political theories. No delta in how these are written—FIELD_SKILL's provision for qualitative sections applies—but the *balance* toward empirical governance studies is notable.

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Voice and person:** First-person plural ("we") throughout empirical work; tone and hedging follow field baseline. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Macro structure:** Quantitative deductive (hypothesis-driven) and theoretical/conceptual formats match FIELD_SKILL. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Abstract format:** Unstructured abstracts, ~100–250 words, no citations. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Methods reporting:** Quantitative methods section: regression, panel models, identification strategies reported as field baseline expects. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Results and findings:** Tables report coefficients with standard errors and significance asterisks; prose interprets and revisits hypotheses. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Discussion and conclusion:** Opens with finding summary, develops theoretical implications, addresses limitations, closes with future directions. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Argumentation:** Hypotheses (H1, H2…) in quantitative work; propositions (P1, P2…) in theoretical work; logical reasoning chains and boundary conditions explicit. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Literature engagement:** Citation-dense intro and theory sections; selective citation in results; discussion engages cited work to interpret findings. FIELD DEFAULT.
- **Epistemic stance:** Moderate hedging; "we find," "we argue," "suggest," "appears," "may indicate" used as field baseline prescribes. FIELD DEFAULT.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Governance/ethics framing unclear:** ABS 2 tier expects precise theoretical grounding. Submitting a general organizational behavior paper without explicit connection to governance mechanisms (e.g., board role, CSR reporting, stakeholder accountability) will be seen as misaligned with journal scope. Frame hypotheses using board, CSR, ethics, or accountability theory early.

2. **Endogeneity unaddressed:** Governance studies routinely face reverse causality and selection bias (e.g., does CSR improve performance, or do better-performing firms afford CSR?). Reviewers expect identification strategies (instrumental variables, natural experiments, dynamic panel models). Omitting this discussion signals methodological naiveté in the governance literature.

3. **Open-access APC overlooked:** This is open-access only (EUR 1,700 APC). Authors must be aware of this cost at submission time. The journal's ethical guidelines require author agreement on content and ethics compliance; these are non-negotiable and checked by editors.

4. **Hypotheses vs. Propositions misuse:** Quantitative empirical work uses numbered hypotheses (H1, H2…) with directional predictions. Theoretical work uses propositions (P1, P2…) with logical rather than empirical support. Mixing these signals poor understanding of the article type.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

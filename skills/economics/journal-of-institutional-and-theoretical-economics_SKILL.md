# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Mohr Siebeck
- **Scope:** Institutional and theoretical economics, law and economics, organizational economics, competition policy, public choice, auctions, and behavioral approaches to economic problems
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.mohrsiebeck.com/en/journal/journal-of-institutional-and-theoretical-economics-jite/submissions/
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 12 (various issues, 2024–2025)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
FIELD_SKILL structure is appropriate. No journal-specific deviations observed.

### Length
**Maximum 25 pages** (including tables, figures, appendices; measured single-spaced with wide margins). This is substantially shorter than top-tier journals, reflecting the journal's compact publication format. ENFORCED.

### Abstract
**Maximum 100 words** (hard limit, stated in guidelines). Single paragraph, unstructured format. ENFORCED.

### Keywords
**Exactly 5 keywords** (or fewer). Positioned immediately after the abstract. ENFORCED.

### JEL Classification
**Required on first page.** Authors must include Journal of Economic Literature classification codes. ENFORCED.

### Citations
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm of author-year parenthetical style (e.g., Greeley 2012; multiple references separated by semicolons).

### Headings
FIELD_SKILL convention applies. Section headings may use Roman or Arabic numerals; no journal-specific preference stated.

### Tables and Figures
FIELD_SKILL norm. Tables are primary for quantitative results. Figures used for key outcome patterns, distributional results, and model properties. No stated journal-specific restrictions on placement or formatting beyond standard practice.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- No simultaneous submissions to other journals (standard ethical requirement). ENFORCED.
- Footnote usage should be **minimal** (stated explicitly in guidelines). GUIDELINE ONLY—practice shows footnotes are used sparingly but not eliminated.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Scope and Author Base
JITE is ABS 2, a lower tier than FIELD_SKILL (which encodes ABS 4*/4 norms). The journal shows a broader intellectual scope: institutional economics, public choice, behavioral economics, and law & economics appear more prominently than in top-tier journals. Theory and empirical papers coexist frequently within single issues; no strict disciplinary boundary is evident. This reflects JITE's historical identity as the successor to *Zeitschrift für die gesamte Staatswissenschaft* (founded 1844). PRACTICE ONLY—not a constraint, but a context shift.

### Article Length Constraint
The 25-page limit is substantially tighter than ABS 4* journal norms (which permit 40–60+ pages). This creates pressure to streamline exposition. ENFORCED and implies a relaxation of FIELD_SKILL's expectation of extensive robustness sections and detailed appendices. Authors typically integrate robustness checks into the main Results section rather than sequestering them in supplemental material. RELAXATION.

### Abstract Format
FIELD_SKILL does not specify abstract structure. JITE requires unstructured, single-paragraph abstracts (100 words max). This contrasts with some economic journals that use structured abstracts (Background/Objective/Methods/Results). ENFORCED.

### Procedural Detail and Methods Exposition
Given the 25-page limit, procedural detail is more economical than in FIELD_SKILL top-tier practice. Standard methods are cited and named with minimal further explanation. Non-standard estimators receive fuller treatment, but the scope is compressed. Identification strategy is still explicitly stated (following FIELD_SKILL norm) but is often embedded within the empirical or theoretical development rather than as a standalone subsection. RELAXATION.

### Data Description
Summary statistics tables and source documentation follow FIELD_SKILL norm, but are often more compressed. Variable definitions may appear in footnotes or inline rather than in a separate table, reflecting space constraints. PRACTICE ONLY.

### Robustness and Extensions
Formal robustness sections (as in FIELD_SKILL) are compressed or merged into Results. Alternative specifications, sample definitions, and sensitivity checks are present but are integrated with main results rather than sequestered. Well-powered null results are publishable (consistent with FIELD_SKILL). RELAXATION.

### No Other Deltas
Voice and register, citation density by section, citation format, hedging norms, sentence structure, argumentation patterns, introduction structure (CARS + results announcement), and conclusion style: all follow FIELD_SKILL without discernible deviation. First-person plural ("we") is standard. Active voice dominates. Direct quotation is rare. Hedging intensity is low and tied to identification, external validity, and data limitations (consistent with FIELD_SKILL).

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Exceeding 25 pages.** Authors accustomed to longer outlets often underestimate page density. Even papers at 28–30 pages in draft form face desk rejection. Plan for aggressive compression from the outset.

2. **Overloading footnotes with methodological detail.** The guideline "keep footnotes to a minimum" is stated explicitly but often tested by authors hiding caveats or procedural alternatives in footnotes. Editors flag this; integrate procedural detail into main prose or omit.

3. **Structured abstract.** Some authors use the structured format from other journals (Objective / Methods / Results / Conclusions). JITE requires unstructured, single-paragraph format. This is frequently the reason for rejection at the administrative check.

4. **Missing JEL codes on first page.** The requirement is non-negotiable. Absence is grounds for immediate resubmission request.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

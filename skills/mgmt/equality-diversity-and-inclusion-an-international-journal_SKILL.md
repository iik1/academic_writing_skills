# Journal Writing Skill: Equality, Diversity and Inclusion: an International Journal

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN
- **Publisher:** Emerald Group Publishing Limited
- **Scope:** Platform for critical and rigorous exploration of equal opportunities concerns including gender, ethnicity, class, disability, age, sexual orientation, religion, and other emerging forms of inequalities in the context of society, organizations, and work.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.emeraldgrouppublishing.com/journal/edi (paywalled; general Emerald templates available at emeraldgrouppublishing.com)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

### Structure
Follows Emerald standard format: **Title page (separate) → Anonymous main document → Structured abstract → Introduction → [Content sections, typically Introduction–Methodology–Results–Discussion–Conclusion for empirical work] → Conclusion (or merged Discussion/Conclusion) → References**.

- For empirical/quantitative articles: Introduction → Theory/Hypotheses → Methods → Results → Discussion → Conclusion
- For qualitative articles: Introduction → Theoretical Background → Research Setting/Methods → Findings → Discussion → Conclusion
- Standalone Conclusion section is acceptable; merged Discussion/Conclusion also appears

### Abstract
- **Structured (mandatory)**: Four obligatory fields:
  - **Purpose** (what is being studied and why)
  - **Design/methodology/approach** (research design, method, data, sample)
  - **Findings** (key results or conclusions)
  - **Originality/value** (contribution to theory or practice)
- Three optional fields: Research limitations/implications, Practical implications, Social implications
- **Maximum 250 words** (including keywords and article classification)
- Emerald permits up to eight keywords; these are part of the abstract word count

### Length
- Typical empirical articles: 4,000–8,000 words (Emerald guidance: 1,000–4,000 words baseline; Equality, Diversity and Inclusion typically accepts longer pieces for empirical work)
- Word count includes abstract, main text, and references
- Tables and figures count toward word limit if embedded in-text; separate figure files may not

### Citations
- **Harvard style** (Author, Year) or Author (Year) when author is part of sentence
- Multiple citations alphabetically or chronologically: (Author et al., Year; Second Author, Year)
- Harvard full reference format: Author(s) surname, initials. (Year). Title of article. *Journal Title*, volume(issue), pp. pages.
- NOT APA (though some crossover in parenthetical format)

### Headings
- Follows standard numbered or titled heading hierarchy
- Main sections bolded or numbered (1, 2, 3…); subsections (1.1, 1.2…) as appropriate
- Align with generic structural labels (Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion, Conclusion) for empirical work or thematic labels for theoretical papers

### Tables and Figures
- Tables embedded in the manuscript with captions above; figures with captions below
- High resolution and quality required for online/PDF publication
- Footnotes or source attribution required; follow Harvard style for sources
- No embedded images without clear captions and figure numbers

### Author Information and Declarations
- Brief author biography (max. 100 words per author) provided separately or at article end
- Declarations of competing interests, funding acknowledgements, and data availability statements at article end
- Emerald requires explicit statements on potential conflicts

### Word Count Definition
- Includes structured abstract, main text, and references
- Does **not** typically include author biographies or declarations
- Tables and figures (if embedded) count toward word limit

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Citation Style
**ENFORCED - Delta from field baseline**: FIELD_SKILL specifies **APA in-text citations** as the management field standard. **Equality, Diversity and Inclusion mandates Harvard style**. This is a hard constraint. Harvard (Author, Year) superficially resembles APA, but reference formatting is distinctly Harvard. If the journal's guidelines explicitly state Harvard, use Harvard throughout; do not default to APA.

### Structured Abstract Format
**ENFORCED - Delta from field baseline**: FIELD_SKILL notes that management abstracts are "structured or unstructured" and typically cover gap, method, and finding in 150–250 words. **Equality, Diversity and Inclusion requires a four-field structured abstract (Purpose, Design/methodology/approach, Findings, Originality/value)**, with optional fields. This is more prescriptive than the field default. Ensure all four mandatory fields are present.

### Word Count
**ENFORCED - Tighter boundary**: The general Emerald baseline is 1,000–4,000 words. **Equality, Diversity and Inclusion issues reviewed show empirical articles typically 5,000–8,000 words**, suggesting the journal accepts longer pieces than Emerald's stated minimum but is tighter than some ABS 2 journals (which may allow 10,000+). Confirm with current guidelines; do not assume all Emerald journals accept the same range.

### Scope and Voice: Equity-Centered Framing
**PRACTICE ONLY**: Articles explicitly foreground equity, inclusion, and systemic barriers as central constructs, not afterthoughts. While FIELD_SKILL expects management research to address "practical implications," Equality, Diversity and Inclusion articles consistently frame findings through lenses of equity, discrimination, representation, or institutional inclusivity. Use this framing explicitly in Introduction (niche), Findings, and Discussion; avoid treating EDI as a contextual variable rather than the focal construct.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Citation style confusion**: Defaulting to APA when Harvard is required. Double-check journal guidelines and reference format (Emerald is Harvard, not APA). Watch for in-text citation format slipping from (Author, Year) Harvard to (Author Year) APA.

2. **Underselling equity framing**: Writing the research as if EDI is one contextual variable among others. Equality, Diversity and Inclusion expects the research question, methods, and interpretation to center equity and inclusion explicitly. If studying organizational practices, frame through the lens of whose voices are heard, whose opportunities are constrained, and what barriers exist.

3. **Shallow abstract structure**: Providing a narrative abstract when the journal requires four-field structured format. Each of Purpose, Design/methodology/approach, Findings, and Originality/value must be distinct and explicit (even if combined into 250 words). Keywords count toward the word limit.

4. **Exceeding word count with unnecessary theory**: Emerald journals are strict on word limits. Equality, Diversity and Inclusion at 5,000–8,000 words allows more space than the stated 1,000–4,000 Emerald baseline, but not unlimited literature review. Front-load the Introduction with prior work; keep Theory/Background concise; let findings and implications drive length.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Sociological Methodology

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** SOC SCI
- **Publisher:** SAGE (official journal of the American Sociological Association)
- **Scope:** The only ASA journal devoted entirely to research methods. Publishes qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods contributions addressing methodological problems in empirical social science — conceptualization, measurement, data collection, modeling, and research design — plus critical evaluative discussions of research practices and philosophical issues underpinning sociological research. Not a substantive-findings journal: the methodological contribution itself is the paper's point.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/author-instructions/smx (SAGE author-instructions page returns 403 to automated fetch; guidelines cross-verified via SAGE guidelines PDF search snippet and secondary sources)
- **Field baseline:** soc-sci-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 (Rinderknecht, Doan, and Sayer 2025, "The Daily Lives of Crowdsourced U.S. Respondents," full text via PMC; Lubbers, Bojanowski, Targarona Rifa, and Ciordia 2025, "The Measurement Properties of Aggregated Relational Data and NSUM-Estimated Network Size," abstract; Zhang and Leung 2026, "Joint Text-and-Image Clustering for Social Science Research," abstract; Duxbury 2024, "Micro-Macro Mediation Analysis in Social Networks," abstract; Thomas, Huang, Luo, Hipp, and Butts 2024, "Marginal-Preserving Imputation of Three-Way Array Data in Nested Structures," abstract; plus current-issue TOC scan for additional titles/scope confirmation)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Title page, abstract, text, notes, references, tables, figures, appendixes — in that order. No mandated internal section-heading scheme beyond this; internal organization (Background, Data and Methods, Results, Discussion, Conclusion, etc.) is left to authors, consistent with FIELD_SKILL's canonical progression.

### Length
Articles generally 10,000 words or less.

### Abstract
150–200 words. Unstructured (single paragraph, no internal headings). Must NOT include authors' names or other identifying information (double-blind review).

### Citations
ASA Style Guide (7th ed., 2022), author-date format: parenthetical (Author Year) with a colon before page numbers, e.g., (Smith 2020:45) — no comma before the page number as in APA. Full reference list titled "References," alphabetical by first author's surname, full first names (not initials) for all authors, hanging indent, double-spaced.

### Headings
No specific heading-level scheme mandated beyond the ASA Style Guide; format per Word/LaTeX template requirements (double-spaced, 12-point Times New Roman, 1.25-inch margins).

### Tables and Figures
Manuscript file must be Word or LaTeX (LaTeX submissions require an accompanying PDF). Tables/figures placed after references/notes per the required section order above.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Do not identify yourself as the author when citing your own prior work (double-blind review).
- Simultaneous submission to another professional journal while under review at SM is treated as unethical by ASA.
- Authors must share data/documentation per ASA data-sharing norms, except where proprietary or confidentiality concerns preclude it.
- A short author biography (under 100 words per author) is required at submission, separate from the anonymized manuscript.
- Non-refundable $25 manuscript processing fee (procedural, not stylistic).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
Papers are explicitly framed as methodological contributions rather than substantive-findings papers: the CARS "gap" in the introduction is typically a gap in method or measurement (e.g., unvalidated properties of a data-collection technique, an unaddressed estimation problem) rather than a gap in substantive knowledge about a social phenomenon. FIELD_SKILL's Theory/Literature Review section is correspondingly thinner or replaced by a "Background" section that reviews prior methodological approaches and their limitations, not competing substantive theories. PRACTICE ONLY, consistent across all articles sampled — a specialization of FIELD_SKILL's structure rather than a contradiction of it.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Single-authored contributions are common and use "the author" in third person as an alternative to first-person "I" (e.g., Duxbury 2024 abstract: "the author defines..."), alongside the FIELD_SKILL-consistent first-person singular. PRACTICE ONLY, low-prevalence relative to first-person use — noted as an accepted variant, not a norm shift.

## Dimensions With No Delta
- Voice and person: FIELD DEFAULT (first-person "we"/"I" dominant; passive reserved for procedure, consistent with FIELD_SKILL)
- Statistical reporting: FIELD DEFAULT (coefficients with SEs, significance markers, effect-size narration as in FIELD_SKILL)
- Citation density by section: FIELD DEFAULT
- Hedging: FIELD DEFAULT
- Discussion/Conclusion norms: FIELD DEFAULT
- Contribution framing: FIELD DEFAULT (explicit contribution statements, constructive engagement with prior work)

---

## Common Pitfalls
- Naming yourself as author when citing your own prior work — breaks double-blind review requirement.
- Framing the paper around a substantive sociological finding rather than a methodological contribution; SM reviewers expect the novelty to be in the method, measurement, or research-design advance itself.
- Exceeding the 150–200 word abstract limit or including identifying information in it.
- Missing the required short author biography (under 100 words) at submission.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

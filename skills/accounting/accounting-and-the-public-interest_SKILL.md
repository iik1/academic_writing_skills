# Journal Writing Skill: Accounting and the Public Interest

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ACCOUNT
- **Publisher:** American Accounting Association (Public Interest, Ethics, and Sustainability Section)
- **Scope:** Accounting research explicitly situated within a historical, social, or political context, spanning financial accounting, auditing, social/environmental accounting, taxation, ethics, governance, and technology, with findings expected to provide guidance for "responsible action." Publishes one issue per year and explicitly welcomes theoretical/empirical contributions, literature reviews, and replications/reinterpretations of prior work, alongside "innovation and eclecticism" in theory and method.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://aaahq.org/Research/Journals/Accounting-and-the-Public-Interest/Guide-for-Authors ; https://aaahq.org/Research/Journals/Accounting-and-the-Public-Interest/Editorial-Policy ; general AAA Manuscript Preparation Guide at https://aaahq.org/Research/Journals/Manuscript-Preparation-Guide
- **Field baseline:** account-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 12, via title/author/abstract-snippet-level access (front-matter only; full text was paywalled behind Editorial Manager / Allen Press / Meridian, which returned 403 on all direct fetch attempts). Sample: Beelitz, Cho, Michelon & Patten (2021) "Measuring CSR disclosure when assessing stock market effects"; Henri, Journeault & Rodrigue (2021) "The domino effect of perceived stakeholder pressures on eco-controls"; untitled progressive-tax/income-inequality paper (2022); Adams (2023) "Discussion of: The inherent conflict between progressive tax rates and income inequality"; Evans, Beyer, Mason & West (2023) "Data breach severity and debt market responses"; Fiolleau & Thorne (2023) "The Impact of the Global Pandemic on Ethics, Professionalism, and Judgment in Accounting and Financial Reporting" (symposium introduction, vol. 23, pp. 174-176); Schmidt "Discussion of: Technology, Ethics, and the Pandemic: Responses from Key Accounting Actors" (2023); Lai (2023) "The effect of tenure, specialization, conservatism, and fees on the relationship between going-concern opinions and client bankruptcy"; Akinyele, Demek & Tian (2023) "Self-interest or public interest: The role of incentive schemes in public sector employees' decisions"; Farnsel & Neuman (2024) "Local income inequality and the value of corporate tax responsibility"; Lao & McPhee (2024) "Internal information quality and corporate social responsibility"; Stuart, Riley & Nolder (2024) "Self-concept priming and message framings as strategies for improving individuals' compliance." Access limitation: this is a low-volume (one issue/year) ABS 2 journal with no open-access full text found; sample is title/abstract-page level, sufficient to judge structural and topical patterns but not fine-grained prose style.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Standard AAA journal document order: AI-use disclosure statement, then text (abstract first, then introduction), references, figures (captions), tables, exhibits, appendices. Title page submitted as a separate file from the manuscript text for anonymous review. No journal-specific deviation from the general AAA structure found beyond the symposium format noted below.

### Length
Initial submissions and revisions should not exceed 55 pages including references, figures, tables, and appendices (standard AAA limit, not unique to this journal but stated explicitly in its guide).

### Abstract
100-150 words, on a separate page immediately preceding the text, followed by exactly four keywords for indexing. This word count is narrower than a generic unstructured-abstract norm and should be treated as a hard constraint.

### Citations
Author-date, e.g., "(Berry 2003)," "(Fehr and Schmidt 2003)." Standard AAA format; follows FIELD_SKILL norm.

### Headings
Standard AAA four-level scheme: Level 1 centered/bold/all-caps/roman-numeral; Level 2 flush-left/bold/title-case; Level 3 flush-left/bold-italic/title-case; Level 4 paragraph-indent/bold/lowercase-lead. No journal-specific deviation found.

### Tables and Figures
Arabic numerals, complete titles, each cited directly in text (not "insert table X here"); tables via Word/Excel only; figures as separate EPS/TIFF/PDF/JPEG/PNG files; all variables defined in table notes or appendix; alt-text required for accessibility (standard AAA requirement).

### Explicit Prohibitions
No plagiarism/fabrication/falsification (CrossCheck screening on all submissions); no undisclosed conflicts of interest (COI form + title-page disclosure + cover-letter statement required for every author); mandatory AI-tool-use disclosure statement as the first submission document; IRB approval required for human-subjects research; ORCID required for the submitting author.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
This journal runs thematic **symposia**: a lead/introductory piece framing a special topic (e.g., Fiolleau & Thorne 2023 on pandemic ethics) is paired with short discussant commentaries explicitly titled "Discussion of: [main paper title]" (e.g., Adams 2023; Schmidt 2023). These discussion pieces run only a few pages and are structured as direct response/commentary rather than standalone empirical papers. FIELD_SKILL's canonical structure (Abstract/Intro/Hypotheses/Data/Results/Discussion) does not anticipate this discussant-commentary format. PRACTICE ONLY — observed in multiple 2023 symposium entries but not stated as a formal manuscript type in the author guidelines; treat as a possible submission track (thematic symposium contribution) rather than the default article format.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Explicit mission framing requirement: guidelines state studies "should be linked to the public interest by situating them within a historical, social, or political context, and findings should ultimately provide guidance for responsible action." This is a stated (ENFORCED) expectation beyond FIELD_SKILL's generic contribution-framing norm — authors should include an explicit "public interest"/societal-context framing move, typically in the introduction, that ties the research question to broader stakeholder or policy consequences. Consistent with topics observed in the sample (CSR disclosure, income inequality and taxation, data breaches and debt markets, public-sector incentive schemes).

## Common Pitfalls
- Exceeding the 100-150 word abstract cap or omitting the required four keywords (ENFORCED, checked at submission).
- Omitting the required "public interest" societal/historical/political framing in the introduction — a paper that reads as a purely technical archival contribution without this framing move risks desk rejection at this journal even if methodologically sound for a mainstream ACCOUNT outlet.
- Submitting an AI-use disclosure statement inconsistently or omitting it — this is now the first required document in the file order, ahead of the abstract.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

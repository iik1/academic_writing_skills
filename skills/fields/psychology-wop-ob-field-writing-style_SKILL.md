# Field Writing Style: PSYCH (WOP-OB)

## Scope and Intended Use

This baseline describes the writing conventions shared across the elite
tier of Work, Organizational, and Personnel (WOP-OB) psychology journals —
those rated ABS 4* and ABS 4 in the AJG 2024 list. It is intended as a
prior for journal-specific writing skills in this field: those skills
should assume everything below holds unless they explicitly note a
deviation. This baseline applies to quantitative empirical articles, the
dominant article type at this level; where meta-analyses, scale-development
papers, or qualitative studies diverge, this is flagged as a point of
varying practice rather than a fixed rule.

## Typical Article Structure

At the ABS 4*/4 level, reviewers expect a canonical empirical-psychology
structure: Abstract, Introduction (with embedded literature review and
theoretical development), Hypotheses (either as a distinct heading or
interleaved within the introduction/theory section), Method, Results,
Discussion (with embedded or separately headed Limitations, Theoretical
Implications, and Practical Implications), and Conclusion. A separate,
early "Theoretical Background" or "Theory and Hypotheses" section is
near-universal in single-study designs; in multi-study packages, each
study typically carries its own Method and Results subsection nested
under an overarching introduction and general discussion. Structured or
semi-structured abstracts stating purpose, method, key findings, and
implications are expected. A explicit numbered hypothesis list, keyed to
a conceptual/theoretical model figure, is the norm rather than the
exception. Leading journals in this field increasingly favor multi-study
programmatic designs (e.g., a primary field study supplemented by an
experimental or replication study) over single-study contributions,
though single-study designs remain acceptable when the design is
methodologically strong (e.g., large-scale field data, pre-registration).

## Voice, Person, and Register

Leading journals in this field expect a formal, impersonal register
overall, but with more tolerance for first-person plural ("we
hypothesize," "we tested") than is typical in some social science
fields — first person is standard for describing authorial decisions,
hypothesis statements, and study design choices. Passive voice remains
common and often dominant in Method and Results reporting ("Participants
were recruited," "Data were analyzed using..."), reflecting a norm of
foregrounding the procedure over the actor. Register is formal throughout;
colloquialisms, contractions, and rhetorical questions are avoided.
Overall tone favors measured, hypothesis-testing science-writing over
narrative or essayistic prose.

## Sentence and Paragraph Style

Sentences are typically medium to long, often compound, reflecting the
density of citations and conditional/moderated claims common in this
field ("Although X theory predicts Y, recent evidence suggests that Z
under conditions of..."). Paragraphs in the introduction and discussion
are topic-sentence-led and cumulative, building an argument chain toward
a hypothesis or interpretive claim. Paragraphs in the method and results
sections are shorter and more enumerative, often organized by
subheading (e.g., Participants, Measures, Analytic Strategy). Signposting
language ("First," "In addition," "Taken together") is used liberally to
guide the reader through multi-step arguments and multi-study reports.

## Argumentation and Rhetorical Moves

Introductions at this level closely follow a CARS-like (Create a Research
Space) logic: establish the importance and real-world relevance of the
phenomenon, synthesize what is theoretically or empirically known,
identify a specific gap (a boundary condition, an unexamined mechanism, a
methodological limitation, or a theoretical tension between competing
perspectives), and state how the present research addresses that gap.
Contributions are typically framed in terms of theoretical extension
(integrating or reconciling two theories), mechanism identification
(mediators/moderators), or methodological advance (e.g., longitudinal,
multi-source, or multi-level design overcoming common-method or
cross-sectional limitations). Novelty claims are usually paired with an
explicit statement of practical or applied relevance, consistent with
the field's applied orientation. Disagreement in the literature is
typically framed as unresolved tension between theoretical perspectives
or as an empirical inconsistency across studies, rather than as a direct
critique of named authors' competence or methods.

## Methods Conventions

Method sections are written in past tense with substantial procedural
detail intended to support replication and psychometric evaluation:
sample size, sampling frame and response rate, demographic composition,
recruitment procedure, and any exclusions are reported explicitly.
Measures are described with their original source, number of items,
response scale, example items, and reliability coefficients (typically
Cronbach's alpha or omega) for the present sample. A dedicated analytic
strategy subsection describing the statistical approach (e.g.,
multilevel modeling, structural equation modeling, moderated mediation)
and software is standard. Robustness is demonstrated through
supplementary analyses reported either in the main text or in an online
supplement — common method bias checks, alternative model
specifications, and measurement invariance or confirmatory factor
analyses are frequently expected, especially for survey-based designs.

## Results and Reporting Norms

Results sections separate factual reporting of statistical output from
interpretation, with interpretation deferred to the discussion. Findings
are reported with precision — exact test statistics, degrees of freedom,
p-values, effect sizes, and confidence intervals are expected wherever
feasible, consistent with APA reporting standards. Tables are used
extensively for descriptive statistics/correlations, measurement models,
and primary hypothesis tests; prose in the results section restates and
narrates the tabled findings rather than duplicating full statistical
detail. Figures depicting interaction plots, path models, or conceptual
models are common and expected when moderation, mediation, or multi-step
theoretical models are tested. Non-significant or unsupported hypotheses
are reported plainly and without evasive language.

## Discussion and Conclusion Norms

Discussions typically open with a compact restatement of key findings
relative to the hypotheses, then proceed to theoretical implications,
practical/managerial implications, limitations, and future research
directions, often as explicitly labeled subsections. Limitations are
addressed candidly and specifically (e.g., design limitations such as
cross-sectional data or single-source measurement, sample
generalizability, measurement constraints) rather than as a perfunctory
disclaimer. Practical implications are a near-universal and substantial
component of the discussion, reflecting the field's applied identity —
authors are expected to translate findings into actionable guidance for
practitioners, managers, or policy. Conclusions are typically brief,
synthesizing the contribution rather than introducing new content.

## Citation and Literature Engagement

Citation density is high throughout the introduction and discussion,
moderate in the method (mainly for measures and analytic techniques),
and comparatively low in the results. Paraphrase is the overwhelming
norm; direct quotation is rare and reserved for precise definitional or
theoretical statements. Multiple citations are frequently clustered to
support a single claim, particularly when establishing that a finding or
theoretical position is well-established. Engagement with prior work is
integrative and synthetic — authors group and characterize bodies of
work by theoretical tradition or empirical pattern rather than
walking through single studies serially, except when a specific prior
study is being directly extended, replicated, or contrasted.

## Hedging and Epistemic Caution

Hedging is used moderately and purposefully rather than pervasively.
Claims about hypothesized relationships are stated with confidence in
the introduction (as firm theoretical predictions) but findings are
described more cautiously in the discussion, especially regarding
causality, generalizability, and boundary conditions. Common hedges
include modal verbs ("may," "might," "could suggest"), attributive
framing ("these findings suggest," "this pattern is consistent with"),
and explicit qualification of causal language when the design is
correlational or cross-sectional. Overclaiming causality from
non-experimental designs is treated as a significant weakness by
reviewers at this level, so authors are expected to hedge causal
language specifically and consistently when the design does not support
causal inference.

## What This Baseline Does NOT Specify

This baseline does not specify word or page limits, reference list
formatting details, specific statistical software preferences, abstract
word caps, number-of-studies requirements, open-science/pre-registration
mandates, or submission/formatting requirements — these vary by journal
and are the province of journal-specific skills. Practice varies on: the
extent to which a distinct "Theoretical Background" heading versus an
integrated introduction is used; whether hypotheses are numbered inline
or collected in a summary table/figure; and the expected number of
studies per manuscript. No consensus norm exists on these points, and
journal-specific skills should record where a given outlet has a
distinct preference.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

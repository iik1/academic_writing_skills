# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Elsevier
- **Scope:** Behavioral and experimental economics; experimental, empirical, and theoretical work investigating decision-making, judgment, and economic behavior. Welcomes interdisciplinary approaches involving psychology, experimental methods, and behavioral economics.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/journal/journal-of-behavioral-and-experimental-economics/publish/guide-for-authors (Elsevier)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 5 (Incekara-Hafalir, Lou & Slonim 2026; Pham, Veszteg & Kamijo 2026; Boulu-Reshef & Rapoport 2026; Pan, Gomez-Gonzalez et al. 2026; Jobu Babin & Chauhan 2026; plus author guidelines review)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows standard empirical economics structure (Introduction, Methods/Experimental Design, Results, Conclusion) for experimental and empirical work. For theoretical contributions, follows theory paper structure per FIELD_SKILL. **DELTA from FIELD_SKILL:** Journal explicitly welcomes Registered Reports, which have a distinct structure: Introduction, Literature Review with Hypotheses, Methods, Pre-Analysis Plan. If submitting a Registered Report, this structure is mandatory.

### Length
**High contribution-to-length ratio expected.** Papers should not be unnecessarily long; short articles are explicitly welcome. No explicit word limit stated in guidelines. Contrast with FIELD_SKILL top-tier norms: this is ABS 2, so papers may be notably shorter than ABS 4* benchmarks.

### Abstract
Unstructured, maximum 250 words. Must briefly state purpose, principal results, and major conclusions. Confirm word count; no structured sections (Background/Methods/Results/Conclusion subsections are not required).

### Citations
**Author-year (parenthetical/in-text), Harvard referencing system.** Format: (Author, Year) for single references; (Author et al., Year) for multiple authors. Example: (Chen, 2007) or (Sato et al., 2006). Bibliography lists: Author(s), Year in parentheses, Title, Publication details. **DELTA from FIELD_SKILL:** Identical to field norm; no deviation.

### Headings
Standard section headings using Arabic numerals or Roman numerals (field norm); no journal-specific naming convention imposed. **FIELD DEFAULT**

### Tables and Figures
Both common; figures should illustrate key outcome variation and experimental design. **FIELD DEFAULT**

### Experimental Design and Procedures
**DELTA from FIELD_SKILL:** Experimental papers must describe experimental design with explicit detail:
- Factor structure (levels, between-subjects vs. within-subjects)
- Control groups and treatment assignments
- Subject recruitment and sample size
- Procedural regularity and any unexpected events affecting results
- Written instructions (often placed in appendix or supplementary materials)
- Comprehension checks and control questions
- Payment/incentive structure and real payoffs
These elements are expected in methods or experimental design sections. Level of detail exceeds typical field norm for non-experimental work.

### Data Availability and Replication
**ENFORCED, MANDATORY:** Authors must deposit research data in a relevant data repository and cite/link to the dataset in the article. If data cannot be shared, provide a written statement explaining why. This is a required submission element and may affect publication decision.

### Explicit Prohibitions
None stated in guidelines beyond standard research ethics (Elsevier Publishing Ethics Policy). No explicit language/tone prohibitions beyond requirement to be intelligible to generalist readership.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Voice and Register
**FIELD DEFAULT**—first-person plural "we," active voice, high formality maintained as per FIELD_SKILL. Spot-checked articles follow field convention.

### Methodological Transparency and Experimental Detail
**RELAXATION (compared to FIELD_SKILL top-tier norms but aligned with ABS 2):** While FIELD_SKILL emphasis on procedural detail is maintained, this journal may accept shorter descriptions of standard experimental procedures in the interest of the high contribution-to-length ratio. Non-standard or novel procedural elements receive full treatment, consistent with field norm. However, overall experimental detail level is expected to be high for any empirical/experimental paper; the relaxation applies to brevity, not rigor.

### Registered Reports as Distinct Submission Type
**PRACTICE ENFORCED:** Journal explicitly welcomes Registered Reports with pre-analysis plans. Structure differs from standard empirical papers: Introduction, Literature Review with stated hypotheses, Methods, Pre-Analysis Plan. If accepted in-principle based on proposal review, the final paper reports results (may be null) without risk of publication bias. This is not a standard FIELD_SKILL element; ABS 4* journals less frequently solicit Registered Reports.

### Citation Density
**FIELD DEFAULT**—high in Introduction (gap/literature), medium in Methods, low-to-medium in Results, low in Conclusion, following field norm.

### Structural Deviations
**FIELD DEFAULT**—no deviation from field norm Introduction structure (hook, question, approach, results preview, contributions, roadmap). Related Literature may be standalone or integrated into Introduction.

## Common Pitfalls
1. **Failing to deposit and cite research data:** Required by this journal. Submission will be incomplete without data repository link and explanation if sharing is not possible.
2. **Underselling experimental detail in interest of brevity:** While high contribution-to-length ratio is expected, experimental procedures, controls, and design must still be sufficiently detailed for replication. Do not trim procedural description to the point of ambiguity.
3. **Ignoring generalist readership expectation:** This is ABS 2, not a specialist journal. Assume readers are trained economists but may not specialize in behavioral/experimental work. Define domain-specific terms on first use (e.g., "loss aversion" should be briefly explained even if readers know the concept); avoid relying on sub-field jargon without context.
4. **Missing Registered Report eligibility:** If your design and hypothesis are set before running the experiment, consider Registered Report route to reduce publication bias. Check journal website for current Registered Report call.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

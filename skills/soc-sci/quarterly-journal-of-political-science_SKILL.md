# Journal Writing Skill: Quarterly Journal of Political Science

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** SOC SCI
- **Publisher:** Now Publishers Inc.
- **Scope:** Publishes research in positive political science and political economy, spanning formal theory, empirical causal-identification work, and political economy across local, national, and international levels of politics.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.nowpublishers.com/Journal/AuthorInstructions/QJPS (style guidelines PDF linked from this page was inaccessible - 403/404)
- **Field baseline:** soc-sci-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 (abstracts/front-matter only, full text paywalled): "A Formal Theory of Public Opinion" (Diermeier & Schnabel, 2025), "Gender Quotas and Upward Political Mobility in India" (Karekurve-Ramachandra, 2025), "Wolves in Sheep's Clothing" (Rogall, 2024), "Limited Foresight and Gridlock in Bargaining" (Parihar, 2025), plus title/structure review of ~25 additional articles across 2023-2025 issues via IDEAS/RePEc listings

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Submission requires two parts: (1) a title page with title and author information, (2) an anonymized submission containing title, abstract, main text, references, tables, and figures, with all identifying information removed. Single-blind review (referees remain anonymous to authors).

### Length
No stated word or page limit for full-length articles (per compiled political-science journal word-limit survey). In-house screening may reject manuscripts for "excessive length," but no numeric cap is published. Consistent with observed practice: published articles commonly run 30-50 pages.

### Abstract
No explicit structured-abstract requirement found; observed abstracts are single unstructured paragraphs.

### Citations
No journal-specific citation style found beyond standard political-science convention; not distinct enough from FIELD_SKILL to record as a delta.

### Headings
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.

### Tables and Figures
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Manuscript must not be previously published, under review elsewhere, or (if accepted) published elsewhere in similar form.
- Pre-registration is not required, but if authors pre-register, the pre-analysis plan must be time-stamped and posted to a known repository before data access, and all pre-registered analyses must appear in the paper or appendix.
- Authors of empirical papers may be required to supply a replication dataset/archive prior to publication.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
RELAXATION/PRACTICE ONLY: Single-authored papers are common and consistently use third-person self-reference ("the author") in abstracts and, based on abstract phrasing, apparently in the text as well, rather than "I." This is a lower-formality-neutral variant not flagged as unusual in FIELD_SKILL, which treats first-person singular as fully acceptable; QJPS abstracts favor the more distanced "the author" framing. Treat as PRACTICE ONLY, not a stated requirement — insufficient evidence to override FIELD_SKILL's "I is acceptable" default, but authors submitting here should not be surprised to see third-person self-reference as the observed convention in this journal's front matter.

### Structural Deviations
PRACTICE ONLY: The journal's scope leans more heavily toward formal/game-theoretic modeling than typical SOC SCI outlets (multiple spot-checked articles are pure formal-theory papers with equilibrium derivations rather than empirical Data/Methods/Results structure). For formal-theory submissions, expect the paper to substitute a "Model" section and equilibrium-characterization sections for the empirical Data-and-Methods/Results structure described in FIELD_SKILL, mirroring economics-journal conventions more than sociology conventions. This is within the range FIELD_SKILL already anticipates for methodologically diverse SOC SCI work, so it is not a contradiction, but authors should lean toward the quantitative/formal branch of FIELD_SKILL guidance rather than the qualitative/ethnographic branch by default for this journal.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
PRACTICE ONLY: Empirical papers consistently foreground an explicit identification strategy and a named natural experiment or quasi-experimental design in the abstract itself (e.g., staggered policy rollout, rainfall-induced exposure variation, close elections). This is consistent with FIELD_SKILL's quantitative-causal-inference branch but is more uniformly present here than the FIELD_SKILL baseline implies is universal across SOC SCI — treat identification-strategy framing in the abstract as close to a de facto norm for empirical submissions to this journal.

## Common Pitfalls
- Assuming a strict word cap exists and truncating an argument unnecessarily; the journal has no stated limit but does screen for "excessive length" in-house, so padding is still penalized even without a numeric ceiling.
- Submitting a qualitative/ethnographic-style paper without recognizing the journal's de facto center of gravity is formal theory and quasi-experimental causal inference; framing should lean toward that tradition unless the paper has a strong reason otherwise.
- Failing to remove identifying information for the anonymized submission (single-blind review requires a fully anonymized manuscript separate from the title page).

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: International Journal of Electronic Commerce

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** INFO MAN
- **Publisher:** Routledge / Taylor & Francis (formerly M.E. Sharpe)
- **Scope:** The leading refereed quarterly devoted to advancing the understanding and practice of electronic commerce, publishing empirical and interpretive research (experimental, theoretical, or survey-based) aimed at both researchers and practitioners.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.ijec-web.org/information-for-contributors/ (journal's own site; T&F's tandfonline.com/journals/mjec20 guidelines page returned 403 and could not be fetched directly)
- **Field baseline:** information-management-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 10 (titles/abstracts, front matter only — full text paywalled). Sampled across Vol. 27 No. 1 (2023), Vol. 28 No. 2 (2024), Vol. 29 No. 4 (2025), Vol. 30 No. 2 (2026): "How Feature Changes of a Dominant Ad Platform Shape Advertisers' Human Agency," "The Use of Inside-Out and Outside-In Big Data Analytics on E-Platforms," "Impact of Campaign Bundling on the Success of Donation-Based Crowdfunding," "Do Many Hands Make Light Work? The Contingent Effects of Online Team Size in Open Collaboration Contests," "The Influence of Consumer Skepticism Toward Online Reviews in Product Evaluations," "Gatekeeping in Online Markets: An Empirical Investigation of IT-based Self-regulation in Online Black Markets," "Will Bidders Bid Higher in Charity Auctions?," "The Comparative Effects of Digital Green Nudging, Gamification, and Monetary Incentives on Return Motivation in E-Commerce," "How User-to-User Co-Creation on Social Media Benefits Individuals During Times of Heightened Uncertainty," plus one 2024 special-issue lead article on platform ecosystem transformation.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Single manuscript file (Word or PDF); title, abstract, and keywords at the top; numbered pages; figures and tables placed on separate pages at the end of the manuscript (not inline), each with a numbered, meaningful caption, with approximate in-text placement indicated. Research instruments (e.g., survey items) submitted as a separate Word file with author-identifying information removed, to support double-blind review.

### Length
No explicit word/page limit stated in the guidelines.

### Abstract
Required, placed with keywords immediately after the title. No structured-abstract format or explicit word limit specified.

### Citations
ENFORCED — numbered citation style: in-text citations appear as bracketed numbers (e.g., "[9]"), and the reference list is arranged alphabetically by first author's name but keyed to the in-text numbers. This is a marked departure from the author-date (APA-style) citation convention that FIELD_SKILL treats as standard; do not use parenthetical author-year citations for this journal.

### Headings
Not specified in the guidelines.

### Tables and Figures
Must be cited specifically by number in text with a "meaningful caption"; placed on separate pages at manuscript end; must remain legible when reduced in size; high-resolution versions required only at final acceptance.

### Explicit Prohibitions
No prior copyright, no prior publication, no simultaneous submission elsewhere. Author-identifying information must be stripped for double-blind review, including from any submitted research instruments.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
ENFORCED (by consistent practice) — Every issue opens with a signed "Editor's Introduction" by the Editor-in-Chief (Vladimir Zwass), confirmed across issues from 2019 through 2026, which frames the issue's articles and, for special issues, introduces the guest editors and theme. This is a standing journal feature, not something FIELD_SKILL anticipates (which covers only the article-level structure). Authors submitting to a themed/special issue should expect their paper to be positioned within this editorial framing rather than standing alone.

PRACTICE ONLY — Special issues with named guest editors recur regularly (e.g., "Transformation and Sustainability of Digital Platforms and Ecosystems," 28(2); social commerce theme, 29(1)). Authors targeting a special issue should align framing with the stated call/theme; this is more common here than a general FIELD_SKILL expectation.

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
PRACTICE ONLY — Several spot-checked abstracts foreground the empirical design element explicitly in the abstract's second sentence (e.g., naming "sequential exploratory mixed-methods," dataset size, and time span, or auction-count and platform source) rather than opening with broad phenomenon-framing throughout. This is consistent with FIELD_SKILL's abstract norms generally but leans slightly more toward stating method/data specifics early rather than deferring them.

## Common Pitfalls
- Using APA author-date in-text citations instead of the journal's numbered bracket style [n].
- Placing tables/figures inline in the submission rather than on separate pages at the end with numbered captions.
- Leaving author-identifying information in a submitted research-instrument file, breaking double-blind review.
- Submitting to a themed issue without addressing the stated special-issue call/theme that the Editor's Introduction will frame.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

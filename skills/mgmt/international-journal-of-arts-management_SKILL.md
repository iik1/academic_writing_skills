# Journal Writing Skill: International Journal of Arts Management

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ETHICS-CSR-MAN
- **Publisher:** HEC Montréal (Carmelle and Rémi Marcoux Chair in Arts Management)
- **Scope:** Management processes and practices in arts and cultural organizations (marketing, HR, finance, operations, administration), with room for cultural policy, cultural studies, and economics perspectives; welcomes both scholarly and practitioner submissions.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://gestiondesarts.hec.ca/en/ijam/ (author guidelines and style guide); submissions via https://revue.hec.ca/index.php/ijam/
- **Field baseline:** mgmt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 6 (title/author/section-level only; full text paywalled — see Sources). Titles: "Predicting Museum Visitors' Intention Through Nonverbal Cues" (De Sainte Maresville & Petr); "Deep Roots, Creative Fruits" (Kim, Alvarez & Arikan); "Sensemaking and Artistic Directors in the Performing Arts Sector" (Margieson et al.); "The Art Infusion Effect of Intangible Cultural Heritage" (Yang, Li & Ji); "Adapting to Crisis: Digital Transformation... in the Italian Performing Arts" (Besana); "Chameleons in Practice" (Humphrey, Practitioner Perspectives); "Legacy of a Sonic Maverick" (Ravanas & Sinkovich, Case Study).

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Four distinct manuscript categories, each with its own expected structure — this is a journal-level taxonomy not present in FIELD_SKILL, which assumes a single article shape per methodological type:
- **Research Papers** (7,000–9,000 words, ±10%): standard empirical/theoretical structure per FIELD_SKILL.
- **Case Studies** (5,000–7,000 words, ±10%): must include an accompanying teaching note.
- **Book Reviews** (~750 words).
- **Practitioner Perspectives** (3,000–5,000 words, ±10%): explicitly designed to bridge scholarly debate and practice; written by/with practitioners rather than as conventional empirical papers.

### Length
- Research Papers: 7,000–9,000 words.
- Case Studies: 5,000–7,000 words.
- Book Reviews: ~750 words.
- Practitioner Perspectives: 3,000–5,000 words.
- All categories allow ±10% variance.
- These are shorter than FIELD_SKILL's typical 10,000–18,000-word empirical/qualitative articles — a real length RELAXATION appropriate to ABS 1.

### Abstract
- 150 words (research papers), unstructured.
- Up to six keywords required.
- Shorter than FIELD_SKILL's 150–250-word range (floor only, no upper range stated).

### Citations
- "Simplified Author-Date Chicago Style," not APA as in FIELD_SKILL.
- In-text: up to three authors listed by name with year; four or more use "et al." (e.g., "Bay et al. 2017").
- Page numbers in parentheses for direct quotes.
- References should include DOIs/URLs where available.

### Headings
- Title: 14-point bold; subheadings: 12-point bold.
- Headline-style capitalization for English titles; non-English titles retain source-language capitalization conventions.

### Tables and Figures
- Tables, figures, appendices, graphics, and short author bios (50–60 words) must all be placed at the end of the manuscript, after the reference list — not embedded in text as FIELD_SKILL assumes. Placement location is indicated by callout in the body text.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- No previously published work; no concurrent submission to other journals.
- Manuscript body, footnotes, references, and file metadata must be fully anonymized for double-blind review (no author names anywhere in the submitted file).
- Guidelines explicitly warn manuscripts "may be rejected if the language is not acceptable" — authors are responsible for professional proofreading before submission.
- 12-point Times New Roman, double-spaced, 1-inch margins, English only.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structural Deviations
Beyond standard empirical/theoretical papers, the journal runs a formal **Case Study** track (with mandatory teaching notes, aimed at classroom use) and a **Practitioner Perspectives** track (short, practice-authored pieces intended to speak directly to arts managers rather than primarily to theory). Neither format exists in FIELD_SKILL. ENFORCED — stated in guidelines and consistent with confirmed article titles across recent issues (e.g., "Legacy of a Sonic Maverick" as a case study; "Chameleons in Practice" as a Practitioner Perspective).

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Journal actively courts non-academic/practitioner audiences and mixed academic-practitioner authorship, consistent with its explicit scope statement inviting "practitioners" alongside scholars. This is a tone/audience orientation not present in FIELD_SKILL's management baseline (which assumes scholarly audience throughout). GUIDELINE ONLY / low confidence beyond the stated scope, given thin full-text access.

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting a Research-Paper-length manuscript (10,000+ words) as if this were a top-tier management outlet — IJAM's stated caps (7,000–9,000 words for research papers) are firmly enforced by category.
- Embedding tables/figures inline instead of placing them at the manuscript end after references, as the guidelines require.
- Leaving author-identifying metadata in the Word file (common blind-review failure specifically flagged in IJAM's submission checklist).
- Using APA-style in-text citations instead of the journal's simplified Author-Date Chicago format.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Public Policy and Marketing

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 3
- **Field:** MKT
- **Publisher:** American Marketing Association (distributed via SAGE Publications)
- **Scope:** Publishes conceptual and empirical research on how marketing practice shapes, and is shaped by, societally important factors — consumer protection, privacy, health, vulnerability, deregulation, and the legal/regulatory environment. Requires explicit public-policy relevance, not just theoretical or managerial contribution.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/author-instructions/ppo ; https://www.ama.org/submission-guidelines-american-marketing-association-journals/
- **Field baseline:** mkt-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 8 distinct sources (abstracts/scope descriptions via search-engine snippets and secondary summaries; full-text paywalled). Titles/topics identified: "Two Decades of Contributions to Marketing and Public Policy" (JPPM analysis); "What Exactly Is Marketing and Public Policy? Insights for JPPM Researchers" (Andrews et al. 2022); "Journal of Public Policy & Marketing: A Strategic Vision for Rigor, Relevance, and Inclusivity"; gender-neutral marketing multi-study paper; liberal/conservative charity-reward two-country study; mothers'-vulnerability social-media two-study paper; direct-marketing consumer-privacy empirical paper; 2025 issue topics (forced-consumption crisis study, political small-donor study). Full-text access blocked by SAGE paywall/anti-scraping for individual issue tables of contents; findings rely on abstracts, secondary descriptions, and the journal's own scope/guideline statements, which is sufficient to confirm structural and policy-framing conventions.

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Two required submission files: (1) anonymized title page, (2) main document; Web Appendix (if any) as a separate PDF. Main document must open with a **Policy Contribution Statement** (see below) on the first page, before the abstract/body. No section numbering permitted in headings.

### Length
Maximum 50 pages total (title, abstract, keywords, text, references, tables, figures, footnotes, print appendices), 12-point Times New Roman, double-spaced, 1-inch margins. Web appendices do not count toward the limit. The Policy Contribution Statement (≤300 words) also does not count toward the 50-page limit.

### Abstract
≤200 words, written in third person (not "we"). Up to 8 keywords required.

### Policy Contribution Statement (unique to this journal)
Required, ≤300 words, placed on the first page of the main document ahead of the abstract. Must explicitly address three things: (1) the policy conversation the paper initiates or contributes to, (2) how the paper moves understanding beyond existing marketing-and-public-policy literature, (3) which specific policy stakeholders are affected and how.

### Citations
Author-date parenthetical, NOT APA format: e.g., "(Thorelli 1960, p. 112)" — no comma between author and year. Use full first names in the reference list, not initials. References listed alphabetically. Three or fewer authors cited in full on every occurrence; four or more use "et al." from the first citation.

### Headings
Three levels, no numbering: primary (centered, bold, title case), secondary (left-aligned, bold italics, title case; minimum two required per section), tertiary (left-aligned italics, sentence case, on its own line above the text).

### Tables and Figures
Embedded in text at point of use (not collected at document end). Tables must be live, editable Word objects, not images. Numbered consecutively with descriptive titles that state the key takeaway, not just a generic label. Graphs require error bars where applicable. Original Excel/PowerPoint source files required upon acceptance; authors bear color-printing costs.

### Explicit Prohibitions
No author-identifying information in the main document (double-anonymized review); when describing data-collection sites, use generic descriptors ("a large public university," "a Fortune 500 company") rather than names. No self-plagiarism (checked via iThenticate). AI-generated content must be disclosed in the Methods section and Acknowledgments if used. Mail/email submissions not accepted — ScholarOne only.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Citation Style
FIELD_SKILL states APA in-text citations as the field default. This journal instead uses AMA-house author-date style without the comma ("(Author Year)" not "(Author, Year)"), full first names in references, and full author listing for ≤3 authors. ENFORCED (stated in guidelines; AMA-journal-wide convention).

### Structural Deviations
The mandatory Policy Contribution Statement — a distinct, explicitly three-part framing paragraph preceding the abstract — is not part of the FIELD_SKILL structure and is not found at other MKT journals in the baseline. ENFORCED (stated in guidelines; this is the journal's defining structural marker and is consistently referenced across editorial/scope commentary).

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
Beyond the required Policy Contribution Statement, the substantive contribution framing throughout the paper (introduction and discussion) is expected to name concrete policy stakeholders (regulators such as the FTC, legislators, industry self-regulatory bodies, consumer-protection advocates) and connect findings to an identifiable stage of the policy process (problem identification, agenda setting, formulation, implementation, evaluation) — a framing emphasis specific to this journal's mission, layered on top of FIELD_SKILL's standard "managerial implications" discussion norm rather than replacing it. PRACTICE ONLY / GUIDELINE-ADJACENT (drawn from the journal's own methodological/scope essays for authors, e.g., Andrews et al. 2022 "What Exactly Is Marketing and Public Policy?"; not confirmed as a strict enforced checklist item in every sampled abstract, since full texts were inaccessible).

- Macro structure (behavioral multi-study / quantitative empirical templates): FIELD DEFAULT — sampled articles use standard Study 1/Study 2 or Data/Model/Results templates consistent with FIELD_SKILL.
- Voice and person: FIELD DEFAULT — "we" throughout body text; abstract in third person per guidelines, matching general academic-abstract convention rather than a journal-specific deviation.
- Hypothesis form and argumentation architecture: FIELD DEFAULT.
- Statistical reporting conventions: FIELD DEFAULT — no evidence of deviation from FIELD_SKILL's ANOVA/regression/mediation reporting norms in sampled abstracts.
- Citation density: FIELD DEFAULT.
- Discussion/conclusion architecture (aside from the added policy-stakeholder framing noted above): FIELD DEFAULT.

---

## Common Pitfalls
- Omitting or under-developing the Policy Contribution Statement, or writing it as a generic "implications" paragraph rather than explicitly answering all three required prompts (policy conversation, incremental contribution beyond MKT-and-policy literature, affected stakeholders).
- Using APA-style "(Author, Year)" citations instead of the AMA house style "(Author Year)".
- Leaving identifying institutional detail in the main document (violates double-anonymized review requirement).
- Submitting a purely managerial/theoretical contribution without naming concrete policy stakeholders or regulatory bodies — a fit problem for this journal even if the paper is otherwise strong MKT work.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

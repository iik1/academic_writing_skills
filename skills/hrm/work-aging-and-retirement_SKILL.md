# Journal Writing Skill: Work, Aging and Retirement

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** HRM&EMP
- **Publisher:** Oxford University Press
- **Scope:** Peer-reviewed forum for empirical and conceptual research on worker aging, retirement decision-making, bridge employment, and retirement transitions. International scope with emphasis on translational and policy-relevant findings.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://academic.oup.com/workar/pages/General_Instructions
- **Field baseline:** hrm-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 10 (Growney et al., El Khawli et al., Rudolph & Zacher, Piszczek et al., Guillemyn & Horemans, Sousa-Ribeiro et al., and others from Volumes 10-11, 2024-2025)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Empirical articles follow standard IMRaD format: Abstract, Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion, Conclusion. Qualitative and review articles follow similar structure with Methods section detailing sampling/analysis approach and Results/Discussion organized thematically. All articles include Title page with author affiliations and corresponding author contact. Acknowledgments placed under "Author Note" on title page (removed in anonymous version). Back matter includes References, Tables, Figures (embedded in manuscript).

### Length
- **Empirical articles, reviews:** No specified word limit
- **Research reports:** Maximum 2,500 words (excluding abstract, references, tables, figures)
- **Commentaries:** Approximately 1,500 words
- **Abstract:** Maximum 250 words on separate page

### Abstract
Single paragraph format, unstructured. Maximum 250 words. Must clearly state research gap, methodology or approach, key findings or contribution, and implications. No keywords section within abstract; keywords supplied separately (see Keywords below).

### Keywords
Supply 3–5 keywords on separate line following abstract. Minimum three required to proceed with submission. No specific guidance on keyword format (descriptive terms or standardized vocabulary).

### Citations
APA 6th edition style. References arranged alphabetically by author surname (not numbered). In-text citations as (Author, Year). Paraphrase strongly preferred over direct quotation. Include only citations referenced in text. Frequency: highest in Introduction and Discussion, lowest in Results.

### Headings
Hierarchical structure used: Main sections (Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion, Conclusion) followed by subsections as needed (e.g., "Data and Sample," "Analytical Procedure" within Methods; "Theoretical Background," "Implications for Practice" within Discussion). No explicit prohibition on heading format, but observe APA conventions.

### Tables and Figures
- **Tables:** Double-spaced, numbered consecutively with Arabic numerals, include brief title, use superscript letters for footnotes, reserve asterisks for probability levels only
- **Figures:** Embed in manuscript file; line drawings 1200 dpi, color/halftone 300 dpi, provide alt-text descriptions for accessibility
- **Placement:** Tables and figures appear after References section; refer to them in text by number

### Explicit Prohibitions and Mandatory Elements
- Do NOT submit PDF versions; Word or compatible format required
- Two submission versions required: anonymous (no author identifiers) and non-anonymous (full author details)
- Mandatory data availability statement describing how data can be accessed (or justification for restrictions)
- Separate "Funding" section required if grant-funded; NIH-funded research must be clearly indicated
- Ethical approval statement required for primary data collection
- Copyright must be licensed to Oxford University Press upon acceptance
- No fabrication, plagiarism, or misappropriation of data

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL
> Only dimensions where this journal deviates. Omit any with no delta.

### Voice and Register
Observed practice: Passive voice appears more dominant in Methods and Results sections than FIELD_SKILL suggests ("The study included 213 participants"; "The data were analysed using..."). While first-person plural ("we") does appear, particularly in Introduction and Discussion, it is less prevalent than current FIELD_SKILL trend suggests. Formal register maintained throughout; no colloquialisms. Direct participant quotation present in qualitative work but less frequent than in some top-tier journals. This represents a PRACTICE ONLY tendency rather than a stated requirement—editors do not enforce passive voice, but authors tend toward it in this journal's empirical traditions.

### Hedging Language and Epistemic Caution
Hedging is present and measured, consistent with FIELD_SKILL, but emphasis falls heavily on temporal/trajectory framing ("suggest that," "partial support," "evidence for"). Causal claims appropriately qualified in observational designs. Generalisability limitations stated explicitly in qualitative work. ENFORCED by the journal's strong emphasis on longitudinal and life-course methodology, which naturally invites temporal qualification.

### Structural Deviations
Research reports are explicitly differentiated as a distinct article type with strict 2,500-word limit, whereas FIELD_SKILL describes this as a field-level variation. For this journal specifically, if submitting a research report, the word limit is ENFORCED. Otherwise, regular empirical articles and reviews have no stated word limit. Commentaries are accepted as a distinct type (~1,500 words) allowing authors to surface emerging phenomena and research gaps—this is a GUIDELINE ONLY practice, not required.

### Emphasis on International and Longitudinal Scope
Spot-checked articles show consistent emphasis on longitudinal/panel designs, multi-wave data, and trajectory modeling. Theoretical grounding in established frameworks (socioemotional selectivity theory, job demands-resources, effort-reward imbalance) appears standard. Geographic diversity across articles (UK, Germany, USA, Netherlands, Europe-wide studies evident). This is PRACTICE ONLY—no explicit journal policy mandates longitudinal design or specific frameworks, but the journal's scope and recent publication patterns favor these approaches.

### Data Availability and Transparency
Mandatory data availability statement (or justification for restrictions) is ENFORCED and goes beyond typical field practice. This reflects contemporary open-science standards adopted by Oxford University Press journals. Supplementary data, when provided, are common. This is a hard constraint that differs from field baseline.

- **Argumentation and Rhetorical Moves:** FIELD DEFAULT (CARS-like introduction, explicit contribution statements, gap justification by context or mechanism)

- **Results and Reporting Norms:** FIELD DEFAULT (tables and narration in quantitative work, thematic organization with evidence in qualitative work, interpretation in discussion section, null findings reported straightforwardly)
- **Discussion and Conclusion Norms:** FIELD DEFAULT (revisit contributions explicitly, limitations and future research as subsections, practical/policy implications expected)
- **Citation Density:** FIELD DEFAULT (highest in Introduction/Discussion, lowest in Results)
- **Literature Engagement:** FIELD DEFAULT (paraphrase preferred, diplomatic framing of disagreement, cumulative theory-building orientation)

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Exceeding word limits for research reports:** Submissions labeled as "Research Reports" face a hard 2,500-word constraint (excluding abstract and references). Reviewers will flag over-length manuscripts. If your paper requires more space, reframe it as a regular empirical article with no stated limit.

2. **Omitting data availability statement:** The journal now requires an explicit statement on data access or a justified explanation of restrictions (e.g., confidentiality, participant consent limitations). Reviewers expect this; absence may trigger a desk rejection or revision request.

3. **Missing international perspective or contextual grounding:** While not required, spot-checked articles consistently ground findings in specific geographic, occupational, or organizational contexts. Submissions that lack contextual detail or fail to discuss cross-national differences where relevant may appear narrow in scope to reviewers familiar with this journal's profile.

4. **Insufficient hedging on generalisability:** Causal claims in observational data must be carefully qualified. This journal's longitudinal/trajectory emphasis makes it easy to slip into pseudo-causal language. Use "associated with," "linked to," or "suggests" rather than definitive causal terms unless experimental/quasi-experimental design justifies stronger claims.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

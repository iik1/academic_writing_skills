# Journal Writing Skill: Econ Journal Watch

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Fraser Institute (online, semiannual)
- **Scope:** Scholarly comments on academic economics, including replication studies, essays, and pointed critique of assumptions, methodology, and claims in published economic research. Welcomes examination of irreplicability, weak argument chains, omitted evidence, and institutional failures in economics practice.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://econjwatch.org/call-for-papers/instructions-for-authors-style-guide
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 12 distinct articles across 2024–2026 (Magness & Makovi, Klein & Swanson & Young, Sorens, Francis, Kaestner, Weissman, Herby, Sémanne, Barker, Kohavi et al., Constable, Green & Hand)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
**Section Headings:** Use bold centered titles for major sections. Subsections require bold left-aligned titles only when there are at least two subsections. **Do not use numbered divisions** (Roman numerals, Arabic numerals, or letters) to divide sections.

### Length
**Abstract:** Up to 200 words, followed by a keyword list. No page or word count limit stated for full manuscripts; journals at this tier typically accept 4,000–15,000 word pieces.

### Citation Style
Follow **Chicago Manual of Style (14th edition)** for all citations and references. Use **parenthetic citations only** (Author Year) in text. For specific references, include page numbers: (Author Year, page). Multiple works separated by semicolons. **Footnotes must not be used for citations.** Substantive footnotes appear at page bottom using Arabic numerals.

### References
Provide a complete reference list at article end. Specific formatting rules apply for books (italicized titles), journal articles, book chapters, working papers, blog posts, newspaper articles, and legal cases (which receive a separate "Cases Cited" section).

### Tables and Figures
Include in body of manuscript. No special restrictions on number or placement stated.

### Data and Code
**Statistical results published in EJW must be accompanied by links to all data and code**, to ensure replicability.

### Explicit Prohibitions
- Do not use numbered divisions (Roman numerals, Arabic numerals, or letters) to organize sections.
- Do not use footnotes for citations.
- Do not use quotation marks around article titles.
- Long quotations (3+ lines): indent, single-space, and separate from body text with extra line spacing.

### Language and Tone
Authors are encouraged to use **"a down-to-earth style that does not compromise scholarliness."** The editorial team actively edits submissions for style and expression, as if a carefully edited magazine while maintaining academic rigor. Abbreviations should be used sparingly; introduce complete names before using abbreviations.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Section Heading Conventions
**Delta: ENFORCED.** FIELD_SKILL states "Roman or Arabic numerals; either is acceptable." EJW **explicitly prohibits** all numbered divisions (Roman, Arabic, letters). Use only bold centered (major) and bold left-aligned (subsection, if 2+) formatting. This is a hard constraint that overrides field practice.

### Citation in Footnotes
**Delta: ENFORCED.** FIELD_SKILL notes "Footnotes carry supplementary material and qualifications, not primary citations." EJW **explicitly forbids** any citations in footnotes. Only substantive footnotes (non-citation) appear at page bottom. This constraint tightens the field norm.

### Data and Code Availability
**Delta: ENFORCED.** FIELD_SKILL does not address data/code links. EJW **requires** "links to all data and code" for any statistical results. This is a replicability mandate unique to this journal among top-tier outlets and reflects its critical mission.

### Abbreviation Density
**Delta: RELAXATION.** FIELD_SKILL describes "high" acronym density for well-known estimators and datasets. EJW advises "use sparingly; introduce complete names first," explicitly restraining this field norm. Not a contradiction—a deliberate dialing-back for accessibility.

### Style Register and Accessibility
**Delta: RELAXATION.** FIELD_SKILL prescribes "precise, technical, economical" register with no reward for "needless complexity." EJW explicitly encourages a "down-to-earth style" alongside scholarliness. This is a deliberate shift toward plain-language clarity without losing rigor. Evident in sample articles: Magness & Makovi, Klein et al., Sorens, Francis all maintain field-level technical depth but with more expository prose than top-4 journals typically allow.

### Citation Format Specificity
**Delta: GUIDELINE ONLY.** Both FIELD_SKILL and EJW prefer author-year format. EJW specifies **parenthetic format per Chicago 14th ed.** with explicit prohibition of footnote citations. FIELD_SKILL leaves format choice open (parenthetical or in-text); EJW mandates parenthetic.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Numbered section divisions.** Do not use I., II., III. or 1., 2., 3. or (a), (b), (c) to organize major sections. Use bold headings only. This is a hard constraint and a frequent error for authors trained in top-4 ECON journals.

2. **Citation footnotes.** Do not place citations in footnotes. All citations must be parenthetic in text. Footnotes are for substantive remarks only (definitions, qualifications, side arguments).

3. **Missing data/code links.** If your article reports statistical results, provide hyperlinks to all datasets and code used. This is mandatory, not optional, and reflects the journal's replicability focus.

4. **Overly technical prose without explanation.** While technical depth is expected, EJW's "down-to-earth" style mandate means avoid unexplained jargon. Define technical terms on first use, even if the ECON field baseline would assume reader familiarity. This is a subtle but real tonal shift: accessibility matters here.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

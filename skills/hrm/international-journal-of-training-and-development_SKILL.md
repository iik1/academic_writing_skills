# Journal Writing Skill: International Journal of Training and Development

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** HRM&EMP
- **Publisher:** Wiley-Blackwell
- **Scope:** Multidisciplinary, international, and comparative forum for reporting high-quality original research (empirical quantitative/qualitative, conceptual, methodological, and policy-oriented work) in training and development, addressing major societal, organisational, and individual challenges.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/page/journal/14682419/homepage/forauthors.html (paywall; full public guidelines inaccessible)
- **Field baseline:** hrm-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 3 recent empirical studies (2025): Wisshak et al. Train-The-Trainer; Nyamweru et al. Sustainability in vocational agriculture; Kohmer et al. Digital training for critical online reasoning

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

### Structure
**ENFORCED:** Empirical papers must follow this required structure:
1. Introduction
2. Theoretical background and state of research
3. Materials & methods (also referred to as methodology or methods)
4. Results
5. Discussion
6. Conclusion

Theoretical/conceptual papers follow introduction + theoretical background and state of research + additional sections as appropriate. The journal now offers free-format submission flexibility while retaining these core structural expectations.

### Length
No explicit word or page limit found in publicly accessible guidelines. Given ABS 2 tier and Wiley's standard journal practices, manuscript length is likely moderate (10,000-15,000 words estimated) but this is **PRACTICE ONLY**, unconfirmed by published guidelines.

### Abstract
**ENFORCED:** Below 350 words maximum. Unstructured format (no explicit field headers required). Do not include references or equations in the abstract.

### Keywords
**ENFORCED:** Approximately 5 keywords, arranged in alphabetical order, separated by commas.

### Citations
**ENFORCED:** APA reference style (format unspecified beyond "APA"; consult current APA manual for specific edition). Citation density follows FIELD_SKILL norms (highest in introduction/literature review, lowest in results, rising again in discussion).

### Headings
Follows standard IMRaD hierarchy. FIELD_SKILL conventions apply; no journal-specific deviations documented.

### Tables and Figures
**GUIDELINE ONLY:** Figures and tables should have legends; legends should appear both beneath figures and in a complete list in the text. Separate files may be submitted for figures, supporting information, and appendices, or all may be combined into a single submission file. Free-format submission is supported.

### Explicit Prohibitions
**ENFORCED:** Evaluations or empirical studies that focus narrowly on testing a training programme or model **without wider contextual discussion and engagement with conceptual/theoretical issues will not be accepted**. All empirical work must connect to broader theoretical or policy frameworks in the field.

### Data Sharing
**ENFORCED:** Authors are expected to share research data. Select and declare an appropriate Wiley data availability statement on submission.

### Ethics
**ENFORCED:** IRB/ethical approval statements and confirmation of informed consent are required for primary data collection involving human subjects.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Empirical Structure Specificity
**ENFORCED:** This journal specifies a more formal delineation of the theoretical background and state-of-research section as *separate from* the introduction, whereas FIELD_SKILL notes that this integration varies in HRM journals. At IJTD, the two-section split (Introduction → Theoretical Background & State of Research) appears non-negotiable for empirical papers. This is more prescriptive than the ABS 4*/4 baseline.

### Explicit Prohibition on Atheoretical Evaluation Studies
**ENFORCED:** The journal's stated rejection of narrow programme evaluations without conceptual engagement represents a meaningful delta from FIELD_SKILL, which does not emphasize this screening criterion. Reviewers and the Editor-in-Chief apply this rule; papers that are purely utilitarian in framing are desk-rejected or receive unfavourable reviews.

### Manuscript Formatting Flexibility
**PRACTICE ONLY — Relaxation:** The journal's newer "free-format submission" policy (submitting text, figures, and tables in a single file rather than separate files) is a relaxation of traditional publishing norms. However, the core structure (introduction, theoretical background, methods, results, discussion, conclusion) remains required; only formatting containers are flexible.

### Voice and Register
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. No delta identified. Formal, impersonal register with permitted first-person plural ("we argue," "our study") in introduction, methods, and discussion.

### Citation Density
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. No delta identified.

### Hedging and Caution
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. No delta identified. Moderate to high hedging, especially in discussion/conclusion; causal language is qualified in observational designs.

### Practical Implications
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Practical implications are expected and treated as a required component. No journal-specific delta in emphasis or format.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Submitting a narrow programme evaluation without conceptual framing.** The journal will not accept papers that test a training model or report on a specific training intervention without connecting findings to broader theoretical frameworks (e.g., transfer theory, organisational learning, human capital development). Every empirical paper must address "so what?" at the conceptual level.

2. **Conflating introduction with theoretical background.** The journal explicitly requires these as separate sections for empirical papers. A common error is to merge all contextual and theoretical material into one introduction; the two must be delineated.

3. **Exceeding 350 words in the abstract or including citations or equations in it.** The abstract is intended as a standalone summary, and any deviation from this structure may trigger desk rejection at the first screen.

4. **Submitting with non-APA references or inconsistent citation formatting.** APA is non-negotiable; inconsistency may be grounds for a revise-and-resubmit desk decision.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

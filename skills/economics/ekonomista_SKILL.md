# Journal Writing Skill: Ekonomista

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 1
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Polish Economic Society (PTE) and Institute of Economics, Polish Academy of Sciences
- **Scope:** General economics journal (founded 1900) publishing theoretical, empirical, quantitative, qualitative, and experimental work across economics, with a strong tradition on Poland and Central-Eastern Europe but open to internationally significant papers; also publishes political economy, law and economics, economic history, and feminist economics.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://ekonomista.pte.pl/dl/c6bf3e1d57c0bd8220d4bd78b63d25db/ ("Dla autorów") and https://ekonomista.pte.pl/Cele-i-zakres,3497.html (aims and scope)
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 12 (Issue 2/2024: Jankowska & Götz; Jajuga, Pociecha & Szreder; Matera & Skodlarski; Markowski & Ostrowska; Lisicki & Szewieczek — abstract/TOC level. Issue 1/2025: Kantorowicz & Moszoro; Koryś, Tymiński & Bukowski; Banaszewska; Sawulski & Kiełczewska; Totleben & Piątek; Lewczuk — TOC level. Zajkowska & Broniatowska, "Should I Stay or Should I Go?", Ekonomista 3/2025, pp. 352-378 — full abstract/metadata level.)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Empirical papers are recommended (not mandated) to follow an IMRAD structure (Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion) — looser than FIELD_SKILL's more granular canonical section list (Data / Empirical Strategy / Results / Robustness as separate sections). The Introduction is explicitly required to state the paper's aim, justify the topic's significance, and — for empirical work — briefly describe method, data, and scope of analysis, plus a statement of the text's structure (a roadmap paragraph, consistent with FIELD_SKILL). Longer texts must use subheadings ("śródtytuły").

### Length
Maximum length: articles 45,000 characters with spaces (roughly 6,500-7,500 words); other texts ("inne teksty," e.g. polemics/notes) 18,000-36,000 characters; reviews 9,000 characters. This is materially shorter than typical ABS 4*/4 empirical papers.

### Abstract
Required for articles and research notes: approx. 1,000 characters with spaces (roughly 140-160 words) — short relative to many journals. Must cover: aim of the article, justification of the topic, description of method, and results obtained. Must be accompanied by 3-5 keywords and 3-5 JEL classification codes.

### Citations
In-text citations: author-year, parenthetical, per APA-7. Footnotes are used for substantive notes (placed at the bottom of the page, 10pt) — not for primary citation, consistent with FIELD_SKILL. Reference list at the end, alphabetical, formatted per APA-7 (a more prescriptive citation-style mandate than FIELD_SKILL, which only specifies author-year in general).

### Headings
Times New Roman 12pt body text, 10pt footnotes. Texts divided into parts must carry subheadings ("śródtytuły").

### Tables and Figures
Figures should be kept simple, black-and-white only, no color or special effects; lines/shading may use greyscale. Vector formats required for charts (PDF, EPS, or Excel). Tables and figures must appear both in the main text and be submitted as separate files via the editorial system. All must carry a source note (or "opracowanie własne" / own elaboration, if original). All mathematical symbols, formulas, and equations must be prepared with an equation editor.

### Explicit Prohibitions
Manuscripts must be double-blind anonymized: no author names/affiliations on the title page; citations to the authors' own prior work must be phrased to prevent identification; the review version must contain no acknowledgments or references to shared research projects, grants, or conferences (this is stricter than FIELD_SKILL, which has no blinding requirement). Submissions must be original and previously unpublished. No author fees are charged; no author honoraria are paid.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Voice and Register
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm at the level observable from abstracts/TOCs.

### Statistical Reporting
Follows FIELD_SKILL norm at the level observable from abstracts/TOCs; the one full abstract read (Zajkowska & Broniatowska) was noticeably more qualitative/descriptive and less quantitatively specific than the FIELD_SKILL norm of announcing precise magnitudes in the abstract — PRACTICE ONLY / low-prevalence given single-article evidence, not a stated journal norm, but plausibly a RELAXATION consistent with ABS 1 tier and the short mandated abstract length (~1,000 characters leaves little room for magnitude reporting).

### Citation Density
No delta observed.

### Structural Deviations
Bilingual publication in the same issue (Polish and English articles appear side by side, frequently split roughly evenly), which FIELD_SKILL does not address at all since it is derived from English-only ABS 4*/4 journals. This is a journal-specific/publisher characteristic rather than a stylistic delta, but material for anyone drafting: manuscripts in Polish are fully acceptable and are the historical majority, though the journal actively solicits English submissions (e.g., special issues such as "Political Economy in Central and Eastern Europe," Issue 1/2025, published entirely in English).

### Other Journal-Specific Patterns
The journal carries editorial content types beyond the research article covered by FIELD_SKILL: editor's notes ("Od Redaktora Naczelnego"), polemical discussion pieces responding to other authors' work, and book reviews, all with their own (unspecified in guidelines) shorter conventions. This is FIELD DEFAULT territory for the research article itself but a structural feature of the journal as a whole.

## Common Pitfalls
- Submitting a review-version manuscript with visible author names, affiliations, self-citations, acknowledgments, or references to grants/conferences — the journal enforces double-blind anonymization strictly (Guideline #6); FIELD_SKILL does not mention blinding at all, so authors used to top-5 journals may overlook this.
- Writing an abstract at FIELD_SKILL length/detail (with precise quantitative preview of results) — this journal's ~1,000-character abstract cap forces much greater compression than ABS 4*/4 norms.
- Using color figures or non-vector chart formats — the journal requires black-and-white, greyscale-only figures in vector formats (PDF/EPS/Excel).
- Formatting references in a non-APA-7 style; the guidelines are explicit and prescriptive on this point, unlike the looser "author-year is standard" framing in FIELD_SKILL.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

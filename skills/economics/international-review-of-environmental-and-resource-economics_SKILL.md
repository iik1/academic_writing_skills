# Journal Writing Skill: International Review of Environmental and Resource Economics

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** ECON
- **Publisher:** Now Publishers / Emerald Publishing
- **Scope:** International peer-reviewed journal publishing literature reviews, tutorial papers, and survey articles in environmental and resource economics. Original research accepted only if it contains a major literature review component or can be structured as a literature review with case study illustration.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.emerald.com/irere/pages/author-guidelines
- **Field baseline:** econ-field-writing-style
- **Articles spot-checked:** 14 (from 2023-2025; titles and authors extracted from IDEAS/RePEc and Emerald Publishing; full-text access limited by paywall)

---

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)

### Structure
Unlike the field baseline (which assumes empirical or theory papers), **this journal publishes literature reviews and tutorial papers exclusively**. Original research is not accepted unless substantially reframed as a literature review. 

Typical structure for a literature review:
1. Introduction (motivation, scope, research questions the review addresses)
2. Literature synthesis sections (organized by theme, methodology, or debate)
3. Critical assessment of findings, gaps, and controversies
4. Conclusions and directions for future research

Do NOT include sections titled "Data," "Empirical Strategy," "Results," or "Identification Strategy" unless the review synthesizes such sections across multiple studies.

### Length
No explicit page limit specified in publicly available guidelines. Articles observed range from approximately 40–100+ pages when published (e.g., "Machine Learning Models and Their Applications in Environmental Economics" is 58 pages; "Assessing Criticisms of Carbon Pricing" is 70 pages). Expect substantial length for a thorough review.

### Abstract
**Structured abstract required** (ENFORCED). Must be divided into exactly **four fields**, each with a **100-word limit** (400 words total maximum). Fields typically correspond to: (1) What the review covers; (2) Main findings or themes; (3) Critical gaps or disagreements in the literature; (4) Implications and future research directions. This is a hard constraint that overrides field baseline expectations for unstructured abstracts.

### Citations
**Author-year format** (parenthetical or in-text) is standard. For three or more authors: (Adams et al., 2006) with 'et al' in italics. **DOIs must be included at the end of each reference where available**. References should be in alphabetical order. This aligns with field baseline conventions but enforces DOI inclusion explicitly.

Review papers naturally have **very high citation density** throughout all sections (higher than the field baseline expectation). Citations appear in introduction, methodology/literature-synthesis sections, and conclusion—often multiple citations per paragraph when synthesizing competing perspectives.

### Headings
No specific convention stated in guidelines. Observed articles use both numbered (1., 2., 3.) and unnumbered headings. Arabic numerals appear common in the sample observed.

### Tables and Figures
Graphics submitted as separate files, preferably in Encapsulated PostScript (EPS) format. No other explicit constraints observed. Tables used to summarize findings across studies (e.g., comparative methodologies, effect sizes from multiple papers).

### Explicit Prohibitions
- **Original empirical/theoretical papers without literature review framing are rejected.**
- Journal will not consider research contributions that do not substantially review or synthesize prior literature.

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Fundamental Genre Delta: Literature Review, Not Empirical/Theory
**ENFORCED (stated in guidelines AND confirmed across all spot-checked articles).** This journal publishes reviews and tutorials, not original data-driven or theory-first research. The field baseline assumes a paper structure centered on novel findings (Data → Methods → Results). This journal is the inverse: the literature itself is the primary focus.

**Consequence for writing:** 
- The narrative arc is "what does the literature say?" not "what did we find?" 
- Typical structure shifts from (Introduction → Data → Methods → Results → Conclusion) to (Introduction → Thematic Literature Synthesis → Critical Assessment → Conclusion).
- The "novelty" of a review paper lies in synthesis, contradiction-resolution, or identifying gaps—not in original empirical findings.
- Authorial voice remains first-person plural ("we synthesize," "we assess," "we identify") but the verbs are review-centric ("we review," "we compare," "we critique"), not discovery-centric ("we find," "we estimate").

### Citation Density: Higher than Field Baseline
**PRACTICE CONFIRMED across multiple spot-checked papers.** Review papers cite heavily throughout—not just in introduction and discussion, but embedded in every section of the synthesis. Expect 5–15 citations per page in literature synthesis sections, compared to the field baseline's "high" density in introduction and "low" in results.

### No Results/Robustness/Statistical Reporting Sections
**FIELD DEFAULT, but structurally explicit here.** Literature reviews do not report regression coefficients, confidence intervals, or p-values as original contributions. If the review synthesizes effect sizes or empirical magnitudes from prior studies, those are presented in summary tables—not as novel statistical findings. No "robustness checks" section; instead, reviews note methodological variations across studies and their implications.

### Abstract as Stand-Alone Synthesis Tool
**ENFORCED.** The four-field structured abstract is not merely a summary; it is an argument. The abstract must do the work that an introduction + results summary + conclusion section does in an empirical paper. This means each field carries distinct semantic weight:
- Field 1: What does this review cover?
- Field 2: What are the main findings/themes in the literature?
- Field 3: What key disagreements or gaps exist?
- Field 4: What are the implications?

This is more prescriptive than the field baseline's generic abstract advice.

## Dimensions With No Delta

- **Voice and person:** First-person plural is standard (follows FIELD_SKILL).
- **Active voice preference:** Maintained in review papers (follows FIELD_SKILL).
- **Formality and register:** High formality, no contractions, economical prose (follows FIELD_SKILL).
- **Hedging intensity:** Low hedging for claims about literature state ("the literature shows," not "the literature may show"); claims are literature-based, not conjectures (follows FIELD_SKILL).
- **Citation format (author-year):** Follows FIELD_SKILL baseline.
- **Paraphrase over direct quotation:** Direct quotation remains extremely rare (follows FIELD_SKILL).

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Submitting original empirical or theory research without literature review framing.** The journal rejects papers structured as traditional empirical studies. If your work is empirical, frame it as a major review with a case study; ensure the literature synthesis is the primary narrative, not secondary.

2. **Unstructured or generic abstract.** The four-field structure is mandatory and non-negotiable. Reviewers and editors expect each field to answer a distinct question. A standard 200-word abstract will be rejected.

3. **Insufficient citation density in synthesis sections.** Reviewers expect every claim about the state of the literature to be supported by citations. Unattributed claims about what "the literature shows" are red flags.

4. **Failing to identify and assess disagreements or gaps.** A mere list of papers is not a review. Reviewers expect critical engagement: where does the literature contradict itself? What is missing? A strong review explicitly flags these tensions.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.

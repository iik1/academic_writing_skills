# Journal Writing Skill: Journal of Organizational Effectiveness: People and Performance

## Journal Profile
- **ABS 2024 Rating:** 2
- **Field:** HRM&EMP
- **Publisher:** Emerald Group Publishing Ltd
- **Scope:** Attracts papers in human resource management focusing on the role of people in organizational effectiveness. Includes synthetic and state-of-the-art reviews, conceptual pieces, and quantitative and qualitative studies on performance and people management process issues.
- **Guidelines URL:** https://www.emeraldgrouppublishing.com/journal/joepp (behind authentication; guidelines not fully accessible)

## Hard Constraints (Author Guidelines)
> These override FIELD_SKILL where they conflict.

### Structure
Follows standard IMRaD macro-structure: title, abstract, keywords, introduction, literature review/theoretical framing, methods, results/findings, discussion, conclusion. Back matter includes acknowledgements, ethics statements, and author contribution statements as standard.

### Length
Word limits not publicly accessible via author guidelines page; typical Emerald journal articles range 6,000–10,000 words based on comparable journals in the same publisher stable.

### Abstract
**Structured format (mandatory):** Must include the following sections with bolded headings:
- **Purpose** (state aim/objective)
- **Design/Methodology/Approach** (describe methods and approach)
- **Findings** (present key results)
- **Originality/Value** (articulate unique contribution)

Optional sections: Practical Implications, Research Limitations/Implications, Social Implications (may be omitted if not applicable).

**Word limit:** Maximum 250 words total for entire structured abstract.

### Keywords
Typically 4–6 keywords provided after abstract; capture principal research topics and concepts.

### Citations
Harvard or APA citation style accepted (publisher offers both options on article display pages). APA 6th or 7th edition conventions widely used.

### Headings
Standard hierarchical heading structure (H1, H2, H3) used. Primary sections (Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion, Conclusion) formatted consistently. Subheadings within sections follow a nested outline pattern.

### Tables and Figures
Present in typical academic format with numbered captions. No specific journal-stated restrictions on placement or style observed in sample articles; standard academic conventions apply.

### Explicit Prohibitions
None stated publicly. Journal adheres to standard Emerald Publishing ethical guidelines (research ethics approval for primary data; authorship transparency; conflict of interest disclosure).

## Observed Deltas from FIELD_SKILL

### Structured Abstract Requirement
**ENFORCED**: Journal mandates structured abstract format (Purpose, Design/Methodology/Approach, Findings, Originality/Value + optional sections) with bolded section headings. FIELD_SKILL makes no mention of structured abstracts; this is a hard constraint specific to Emerald journals and JOEPP in particular. This is a **methodological formalization** that goes beyond typical field practice.

### Practical Implications Emphasis
**ENFORCED**: The structured abstract template explicitly includes Practical Implications as a standard section (though technically optional, it appears in all sampled articles). FIELD_SKILL notes that practical implications are "required" in discussion sections but does not mandate a dedicated structured abstract field for this. JOEPP appears to treat practical implications as a formalized, callable section.

### Multi-level or Complex Methodological Designs
**PRACTICE ONLY**: Observed in 6 of 8 sampled articles (multilevel modeling, mediation analysis, moderation effects, mixed-method designs). Not stated in guidelines but prevalent in accepted submissions. This may reflect editorial preference for methodological sophistication in ABS 2 journals attempting to strengthen rigor.

### Originality/Value Framing
**ENFORCED**: The structured abstract requires explicit "Originality/Value" statement. While FIELD_SKILL mentions explicit contribution statements as "norm," requiring them in a structured abstract heading with its own line is more rigid than typical field practice, which embeds contributions throughout introduction/discussion.

### Voice and Register
**FIELD DEFAULT**: Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (formal, impersonal academic register, with first-person plural permitted; passive constructions in methods and literature summary; more narrative and interpretive prose than economics/finance fields).

### Citation Density
**FIELD DEFAULT**: Follows FIELD_SKILL norm (highest in introduction and literature review, lowest in results, rising again in discussion as findings related back to prior work).

### Discussion and Conclusion Format
**FIELD DEFAULT**: Follows FIELD_SKILL norm. Discussion sections revisit stated contributions, distinguish theoretical from practical implications, and typically close with limitations and future research directions. Conclusion remains concise; practice varies on whether discussion and conclusion are combined or separate.

## Common Pitfalls Specific to Journal of Organizational Effectiveness

1. **Unstructured abstract.** Submitting a traditional paragraph-format abstract instead of the required structured format with bolded Purpose, Design/Methodology/Approach, Findings, and Originality/Value headings. Emerald desk-rejects many manuscripts for this formatting error alone.

2. **Missing or vague Originality/Value statement in abstract.** While FIELD_SKILL expects contribution statements, JOEPP requires them to be explicit and concise within the structured abstract's Originality/Value field. Generic claims ("extends the literature") without specific novelty framing weakens desk-review chances.

3. **Insufficient practical implications development.** The structured abstract includes an optional Practical Implications field, but sampled articles show that accepted papers develop this section substantively in both abstract and discussion. Purely theoretical papers with minimal practical relevance may face harder reviewer scrutiny at ABS 2 tier.

4. **Overlooking multi-level or mediational complexity in research design.** While not mandated, 75% of sampled articles employed multilevel modeling, mediation, or moderation frameworks. Simple univariate or bivariate designs may be perceived as underspecified; if using simpler designs, ensure compelling theoretical or methodological novelty.

## Avoiding AI-Writing Patterns

<!-- academic-humanizer:start v0.3.3 -->
These conventions apply regardless of field or journal. Apply them in addition to everything above; only deviate where this journal's own guidelines explicitly require otherwise.

1. **No participial result clauses.** Findings should not carry an "-ing" tail asserting an interpretation the sentence has not earned ("...rose by 12 points, highlighting the importance of enforcement"; "...underscoring the need for validation"; "...demonstrating that norms shifted"). State the finding. If the implication is worth stating, state it as its own sentence.
2. **No sentence-initial transition glue.** Do not stitch paragraphs together with "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Ultimately," "Overall," "In essence," "Indeed," or "That said" at the start of a sentence. Transitions belong inside the argument, not bolted onto it.
3. **Limit rule-of-three constructions.** One or two triads per paper is natural ("clear, concise, and compelling"); a triad per paragraph is a tell. Vary the structure.
4. **Replace importance-inflation words with the actual magnitude.** Avoid "crucial," "critical," "pivotal," "vital," "paramount," "invaluable," "essential" as importance markers, and "significantly" or "dramatically" as intensifiers (also statistically ambiguous). Avoid "vast," "myriad," "plethora." State the size of the effect instead of asserting its importance.
5. **Avoid the 2023-era word list.** "Delve," "explore" (as metaphor), "unpack," "navigate," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "journey," "harness," "leverage," "unlock," "foster," "bolster," "garner," "showcase," "boast," "seamless," "holistic," "multifaceted," "nuanced," "intricate," "interplay." Also avoid "robust" outside its statistical sense and "comprehensive" as self-description.
6. **No empty balance.** Avoid "while challenges remain," "a double-edged sword," "strikes a balance," "it is not without limitations," "both approaches have merits" — these simulate judgment without making one. Do not open more than one or two sentences per section with a concessive "While X, Y" construction.
7. **Quantify instead of gesturing.** Avoid "a number of," "various," "several key," "a range of," "numerous studies" without citations attached. Give the count or the citation.
8. **No anthropomorphised prose.** Avoid "the data tell a story," "the analysis paints a picture," "this paper seeks to," "the results speak to."
9. **No throat-clearing openers.** Avoid "In recent years, there has been growing interest in...," "With the advent of...," "In today's rapidly evolving...." Delete and start with the claim.
10. **No padding verb phrases.** Replace "serves to," "acts as," "stands as," "functions as," "is able to," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "utilize," "prior to," "commence" with their one-word equivalents.
11. **No wrap-up restatement.** A conclusion should add the implication or end — not repeat what was just said via "Taken together, these results suggest...," "In sum," or "In conclusion" followed by a restated summary.
12. **Vary rhythm.** Avoid clustering sentence lengths in a narrow band, making every paragraph roughly equal, or opening every section the same way.
13. **No interpretive adverb openers.** Avoid "Interestingly," "Surprisingly," "Notably," "Strikingly" — if the fact is surprising, the reader will notice.
14. **No chatbot leakage.** Avoid "Certainly," "Here's," "Let's examine," "we can see that," "as mentioned above" — residue from copy-pasted chat output.
15. **No em dashes.** Do not use em dashes (—) at all. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead, whichever the sentence actually calls for.
16. **No antithesis padding.** Avoid the "X, not Y" contrast construction as a rhetorical flourish ("a correlation, not a coincidence"; "a policy shift, not a technical adjustment") unless the contrast is doing real argumentative work and stated once.
17. **No hedges without content.** Avoid "somewhat," "arguably," "to some extent," "in some sense," "it could be argued," "seems to," "may potentially" when they are not carrying a specific, defensible qualification. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real and specify its source.
18. **No fillers.** Cut "basically," "essentially," "simply," "just," "really," "very," "quite," "actually" unless the sentence fails without them.
19. **No flourish labels.** Do not bold or set off rhetorical labels like "The Bottom Line:," "Key Insight:," "Takeaway:," "Bottom line," "The upshot" — state the point in the sentence itself.
20. **No self-praise.** Do not characterize the paper's own contribution as "novel," "innovative," "unique," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art," or "groundbreaking." Describe what it does; let the reader judge novelty.
21. **No topic-announcing openers.** Avoid "This section discusses...," "This paper will discuss...," "Let's turn to...," "We now turn our attention to...." Open with the content itself.
